Q1 (a) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(+5q\) at the origin, \(-10q\) on the +y axis at distance \(r\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of each charge; the test charge feels no force where the two fields are equal and opposite

🧭 Why? Look at each region in turn:
  • Between them: the field of +5q points away from it (up, toward −10q) and the field of −10q points toward it (also up). Same direction → they add, never cancel.
  • Above −10q: the fields are opposite, but this point is nearer the bigger charge, so its field always wins.
  • Below +5q: opposite directions (+5q pushes down, −10q pulls up) and the smaller charge is nearer — the sizes can match. For unlike charges the null point is always outside, on the side of the smaller charge.

Step 1. Put the null point a distance \(x\) below the origin. Then it is \(x\) from +5q and \(r + x\) from −10q:

\[ \frac{k(5q)}{x^2} = \frac{k(10q)}{(r+x)^2}\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{r+x}{x} = \sqrt2\ \Longrightarrow\ x = \frac{r}{\sqrt2 - 1} = 2.41\,r \]
Answer: Below the positive charge, on the y-axis at \(y = -2.41r\) (i.e. \(2.41r\) below the +5q charge). Not between them, not above them.

Q1 (b) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta \]

θ = angle between the dipole moment p and the field E

Step 1. \(\sin\theta\) is largest (= 1) at \(\theta = 90^\circ\), and zero at \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\).

\[ \begin{gathered} \tau_{max} = pE\ \ (\theta = 90^\circ) \\ \tau = 0\ \ (\theta = 0^\circ \text{ or } 180^\circ) \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? The forces +qE and −qE are always equal and opposite. At 90° they sit on two different lines a full distance d apart → biggest twist. At 0° or 180° they act along the same line (the dipole axis) → no twist at all.
Answer: Maximum torque \(pE\) when \(\vec p \perp \vec E\) (dipole along the y-axis); zero torque when \(\vec p\) is parallel (0°, stable) or antiparallel (180°, unstable) to \(\vec E\).

Q1 (c) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

Step 1. Replace \(r\) by \(2r\):

\[ F' = \frac{kq_1q_2}{(2r)^2} = \frac14\,\frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{F}{4} \]
Answer: The force drops to one quarter of its original value (a 75% decrease) — inverse-square law: double r → ¼ F.

Q2 (a) Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(|q| = 10\) nC \(= 1.0\times10^{-8}\) C each; A and C positive, B negative
  • height AB = 4 m, base BC = 2 m, right angle at B
  • Axes: B at (0, 0), A at (0, 4), C at (2, 0)
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F_x = \sum F_{ix},\quad F_y = \sum F_{iy},\quad F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} \]

add the two forces as vectors

Step 1. Distances from A:

\[ \begin{gathered} r_{AB} = 4\ \text{m} \\ r_{AC} = \sqrt{2^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{20} = 4.47\ \text{m} \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Sizes:

\[ \begin{gathered} F_B = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10^{-8})^2}{4^2} = 5.62\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \\ F_C = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10^{-8})^2}{20} = 4.50\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? B is negative, A positive → attraction: A is pulled straight down toward B. C and A are both positive → repulsion: A is pushed away from C, along the line C→A, i.e. up and to the left.

Step 3. Components (C→A direction is \((-2, 4)/\sqrt{20} = (-0.447, 0.894)\)):

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec F_B = (0,\ -5.62)\times10^{-8} \\ \vec F_C = (-2.01,\ +4.02)\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F_x = -2.01\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \\ F_y = -5.62 + 4.02 = -1.60\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]

Step 4. Resultant:

\[ \begin{gathered} F = \sqrt{(2.01)^2 + (1.60)^2}\times10^{-8} = 2.57\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \\ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{1.60}{2.01} = 38.5^\circ \end{gathered} \]
The paper doesn't say where the right angle is. "4 m height and 2 m base BC" puts it at B, with AB vertical — that's used here; state it in your answer.
Answer: \(F = 2.57\times10^{-8}\) N, pointing down and to the left, 38.5° below the −x direction (218.5° from +x).

Q2 (b) Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +5\ \mu\)C at 1 m, \(q_2 = -40\ \mu\)C at 2 m, \(q_3 = +9\ \mu\)C at 5 m
  • \(q_0 = +1\ \mu\)C at \(x = 8\) m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F_{net} = \sum F_i\ \text{(signs along x)} \]

everything is on one line: take +x as positive

Step 1. Distances to q₀: 7 m, 6 m and 3 m.

\[ F_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(5\times10^{-6})(10^{-6})}{7^2} = 9.17\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(40\times10^{-6})(10^{-6})}{6^2} = 9.99\times10^{-3}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_3 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(9\times10^{-6})(10^{-6})}{3^2} = 8.99\times10^{-3}\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? All three charges are to the left of q₀. q₁ and q₃ are positive → they push q₀ away → +x. q₂ is negative → it pulls q₀ toward itself → −x.

Step 2. Add with signs:

\[ F = 9.17\times10^{-4} + 8.99\times10^{-3} - 9.99\times10^{-3} = -8.15\times10^{-5}\ \text{N} \]
Answer: (i) \(8.15\times10^{-5}\) N; (ii) along −x (toward the origin) — the pull of q₂ just beats the two pushes.

Q2 (c) Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = 10\) nC, \(q_2 = 20\) nC (taken as positive), each \(r = 2\) m from the origin, 45° either side of the +y axis
  • \(q_0 = +1\) nC at the origin
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F = \sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2}\ \ (\text{forces at }90^\circ) \]

q₁ is at 135°, q₂ at 45° — 90° apart, so their forces on q₀ are perpendicular

\[ \begin{gathered} F_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10\times10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{2^2} = 2.25\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \\ F_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(20\times10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{2^2} = 4.50\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? Both charges are positive, like q₀, so both push it away: q₁ (upper-left) pushes q₀ down-right, along 315°; q₂ (upper-right) pushes it down-left, along 225°.

Step 1. Components:

\[ \begin{gathered} F_x = 2.25\cos45^\circ - 4.50\cos45^\circ = -1.59\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \\ F_y = -(2.25 + 4.50)\sin45^\circ = -4.77\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Resultant:

\[ \begin{gathered} F = \sqrt{1.59^2 + 4.77^2}\times10^{-8} = 5.03\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \\ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{4.77}{1.59} = 71.6^\circ \end{gathered} \]
The paper gives no signs for q₁ and q₂; they're taken as positive (as written, "10 nC" and "20 nC").
Answer: \(F = 5.03\times10^{-8}\) N, pointing down and to the left, 71.6° below the −x axis (251.6° from +x).

Q3 (a) Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • charges \(\pm5e\), \(d = 0.4\) nm \(= 4\times10^{-10}\) m
  • \(E = 8\times10^{-9}\) N/C
  • \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ p = qd \]

dipole moment

\[ U = -pE\cos\theta \]

θ between p and E

Step 1. Dipole moment:

\[ p = (5)(1.6\times10^{-19})(4\times10^{-10}) = 3.2\times10^{-28}\ \text{C·m} \]

Step 2. (i) Parallel, θ = 0°:

\[ U = -pE\cos0^\circ = -(3.2\times10^{-28})(8\times10^{-9}) = -2.56\times10^{-36}\ \text{J} \]

Step 3. (ii) Perpendicular, θ = 90°:

\[ U = -pE\cos90^\circ = 0 \]
Answer: (i) \(|U| = 2.56\times10^{-36}\) J (U = −2.56×10⁻³⁶ J, the minimum); (ii) \(U = 0\).

Q3 (b) Math 6 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • Both charges are \(-q\) with \(q = -8\) C in the paper; read as −8 C each (see note)
  • A at \((-4, 0)\), B at \((4, 0)\), P at \((0, 4)\) m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge — away from +, toward −

\[ \vec E = \vec E_A + \vec E_B \]

add as vectors

Step 1. Each charge is the same distance from P:

\[ \begin{gathered} r = \sqrt{4^2 + 4^2} = 4\sqrt2 = 5.66\ \text{m} \\ E = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(8)}{32} = 2.25\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C each} \end{gathered} \]

(i) Both −8 C

🧭 Why? Negative charges: each field points toward its charge. From P, A is down-left and B is down-right (both at 45°). The sideways parts cancel; the downward parts add.
\[ E = 2(2.25\times10^9)\cos45^\circ = 3.18\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C, along } -y \]

(ii) A replaced by +8 C

🧭 Why? Now A is positive: its field points away from A → up-right. B (still negative) gives down-right. The up and down parts cancel; the rightward parts add.
\[ E = 2(2.25\times10^9)\cos45^\circ = 3.18\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C, along } +x \]
The figure labels both charges "−q" and gives q = −8 C, which would make them +8 C — but then part (ii), replacing one with +8 C, would change nothing. So the intended reading is that both charges are −8 C. Say so in your answer.
Answer: (i) \(3.18\times10^9\) N/C straight down (−y, toward the origin). (ii) \(3.18\times10^9\) N/C along +x.

Q3 (c) Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • 3 protons (+e) and 2 electrons (−e) on the arc
  • radius \(r = 4.00\) cm \(= 0.0400\) m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum\frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]

potential is a scalar: add with signs; the angles don't matter

Step 1. Every charge is the same distance r from the centre, so add the charges first:

\[ \sum q = 3(+e) + 2(-e) = +e \]

Step 2.

\[ V = \frac{ke}{r} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(1.6\times10^{-19})}{0.0400} = 3.60\times10^{-8}\ \text{V} \]
🧭 Why? θ₁…θ₄ are a distraction for potential: V has no direction, and every charge is at the same r. (They'd matter for the electric field.)
Answer: \(V = +3.60\times10^{-8}\) V.

Q4 (a) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

The formula given is the field of a ring of radius R and charge q at a point P on its axis, a distance z from the centre.

Step 1. Split the ring into small elements of length \(ds\), each carrying \(dq = \lambda\,ds\) with \(\lambda = q/2\pi R\). Treat each as a point charge:

\[ dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,ds}{z^2 + R^2} \]

Step 2. Symmetry: the element directly opposite gives a \(d\vec E\) with the same axial part and an equal but opposite sideways part. So all the sideways (\(dE\sin\theta\)) parts cancel and only the axial parts \(dE\cos\theta\) survive, with

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{z}{r} = \frac{z}{(z^2 + R^2)^{1/2}} \]

Step 3. Add up the axial parts around the whole ring (z and R are the same for every element):

\[ E = \oint dE\cos\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,\lambda}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\oint ds = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,\lambda\,(2\pi R)}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}} \]

Step 4. Since \(\lambda(2\pi R) = q\):

\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qz}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]
Answer: \(E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\), directed along the axis (away from the ring for q > 0). Checks: E = 0 at the centre (z = 0); E → kq/z² far away.

Q4 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Step 1. A dipole at angle θ to \(\vec E\) feels a torque that tries to decrease θ:

\[ \tau = -pE\sin\theta \]

Step 2. Work done by the field as the dipole turns from \(\theta_0\) to θ:

\[ W = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\tau\,d\theta = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}(-pE\sin\theta)\,d\theta = pE(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0) \]

Step 3. Potential energy change \(\Delta U = -W\). Choose the reference \(U = 0\) at \(\theta_0 = 90^\circ\) (\(\cos90^\circ = 0\)):

\[ U(\theta) - 0 = -pE\cos\theta + pE\cos90^\circ\ \Longrightarrow\ U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Step 4. Conditions:

\[ \begin{gathered} U_{max} = +pE\ \ \text{at } \theta = 180^\circ \\ U = 0\ \ \text{at } \theta = 90^\circ \\ U_{min} = -pE\ \ \text{at } \theta = 0^\circ \end{gathered} \]
Answer: \(U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E\). Maximum (+pE) when p is antiparallel to E (180°, unstable); zero when p ⊥ E (90°); minimum (−pE) when aligned (0°, stable).

Q5 (a) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Thin rod of length L with uniform charge density λ along the x-axis from 0 to L; P is a distance d from the left end, on the perpendicular through that end.

Step 1. Element \(dx\) at position x carries \(dq = \lambda\,dx\) and is a distance \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + d^2}\) from P:

\[ dV = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + d^2}} \]

Step 2. Potential is a scalar — just add (integrate) along the rod:

\[ V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^L\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + d^2}} \]

Step 3. Standard integral \(\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}} = \ln\left(x + \sqrt{x^2 + d^2}\right)\):

\[ \begin{gathered} V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Big[\ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\big)\Big]_0^L \\ = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Big[\ln\big(L + \sqrt{L^2+d^2}\big) - \ln d\Big] \end{gathered} \]
\[ V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\left[\frac{L + (L^2 + d^2)^{1/2}}{d}\right] \]
Answer: \(V = \dfrac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\left[\dfrac{L + (L^2+d^2)^{1/2}}{d}\right]\) — no vector components needed, because potential is a scalar.

Q5 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Step 1. Superpose the potentials of \(+q\) and \(-q\), at distances \(r_{(+)}\) and \(r_{(-)}\) from P:

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q}{r_{(+)}} - \frac{q}{r_{(-)}}\right) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{r_{(-)} - r_{(+)}}{r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)}} \]

Step 2. Far away (\(r \gg d\)) the two lines to P are almost parallel. Then

\[ \begin{gathered} r_{(-)} - r_{(+)} \approx d\cos\theta \\ r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)} \approx r^2 \end{gathered} \]

Step 3. Substitute, and use \(p = qd\):

\[ V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} \]
Answer: \(V = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}\). On the axis (θ = 0) V = kp/r²; on the bisector (θ = 90°) V = 0.
