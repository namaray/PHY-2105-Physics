💡 The one idea to carry through this chapter

The electric field is a vector — you need components and directions. The electric potential is a scalar — you just add numbers with their signs. Almost every problem here is easier than its Electric Field twin for exactly that reason.

1. The Idea: Height for Charges

A ball lifted above the ground has gravitational potential energy; let it go and that energy turns into motion. Charges behave the same way. A positive charge "falls" from high potential to low potential, the way a ball falls from high to low ground. Potential is the electrical version of height: it describes the place, whatever charge you put there.

2. Electric Potential Energy

Definition (slides): electric potential energy is the potential energy (in joules) that results from conservative Coulomb forces and is associated with the configuration of a particular set of point charges within a defined system.

When the system changes from an initial state \(i\) to a final state \(f\), the electrostatic force does work \(W\) on the particles. The change in potential energy is the negative of that work:

\[ \Delta U = U_f - U_i = -W \]
  • If the field does positive work, potential energy falls (like a falling ball).
  • If an external agent moves the charge without changing its speed, the agent does \( W_{ext} = +\Delta U \).
  • Reference: \(U = 0\) when the charges are infinitely far apart.

⚠️ N.B. 3 on the notice

The proof of the potential-energy equation is listed as not covered in class, so it is excluded. Know \(\Delta U = -W\) and how to use it; don't learn a derivation of it.

3. Electric Potential

Definition (slides): the electric potential at a point is the amount of work needed to move a unit positive charge from a reference point to that point inside the field without producing any acceleration. The reference point is usually Earth or infinity.

Equivalently, it is the potential energy per unit charge:

\[ V = \frac{U}{q} \qquad\qquad \Delta V = V_f - V_i = \frac{\Delta U}{q} = -\frac{W}{q} \]

The SI unit is the volt: 1 V = 1 J/C. Dividing out the charge leaves something that belongs to the field itself, not to the particle you happened to put there.

The electron-volt

A handy energy unit: the energy one elementary charge gains moving through 1 V.

\[ 1\ \text{eV} = e\,(1\ \text{V}) = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} \]

If a charge is written in units of \(e\) and the potential in volts, \(q\,\Delta V\) comes out in eV directly (PPS-5 #3: an electron through \(1.2\times10^9\) V → \(1.2\times10^9\) eV).

4. Worked Example: Electron in the Atmosphere (Slide Problem-1)

An electron is lifted 520 m by a downward field of 150 N/C

The electron is negative, so the force on it is opposite to \(\vec E\): upward, along the displacement.

\[ W = qEd\cos\theta = (1.6\times10^{-19})(150)(520) = 1.2\times10^{-14}\ \text{J} \]
\[ \Delta U = -W = -1.2\times10^{-14}\ \text{J},\qquad \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} = \frac{-1.2\times10^{-14}}{-1.6\times10^{-19}} = 7.5\times10^{4}\ \text{V} \]

The slide prints 4.5×10⁴ V — an arithmetic slip (1.2/1.6 = 0.75). The electron moved against the field, to higher potential, and its potential energy fell. Both are consistent because its charge is negative.

5. Potential from the Field

Move a positive test charge \(q_0\) through a small displacement \(d\vec s\) in a field \(\vec E\). The field does work \( dW = q_0\vec E\cdot d\vec s \). Add (integrate) along the path from \(i\) to \(f\), and use \(\Delta V = -W/q_0\):

\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Setting \(V_i = 0\) gives the potential at any point relative to the reference.

Uniform field

Move a distance \(d\) along the field lines: \(\vec E\) is constant and parallel to \(d\vec s\), so the integral is just \(Ed\):

\[ \Delta V = -Ed \qquad\text{(magnitude } V = Ed\text{)} \]

The potential falls along the direction of the field. Unit check: V/m = N/C.

6. Potential of a Point Charge

Take the test charge from a point \(P\) at distance \(R\) out to infinity, where \(V = 0\). The field of the point charge is \(E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q}{r^2}\), and doing the integral gives

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r} = \frac{kq}{r} \]
  • The sign of \(q\) stays in: positive charge → positive potential, negative charge → negative potential.
  • \(V\) falls as \(1/r\) — more slowly than \(E\), which falls as \(1/r^2\).
  • Handy shortcut: dividing the two formulas, \( V = Er \) for a point charge (PPS-5 #5).

7. Potential of a Group of Charges

Potentials add as plain numbers (superposition of scalars):

\[ V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} V_i = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{q_i}{r_i} \]

No components, no angles — only each charge's sign and its distance to the point.

Slide Problem-2: centre of a square

\(q_1 = 12\) nC, \(q_2 = -24\) nC, \(q_3 = 31\) nC, \(q_4 = 17\) nC on a square of side \(d = 1.3\) m. Every corner is \(r = d/\sqrt2 = 0.919\) m from the centre, so add the charges first:

\[ V = \frac{k(12 - 24 + 31 + 17)\times10^{-9}}{0.919} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(36\times10^{-9})}{0.919} \approx 350\ \text{V} \]

Slide Problem-3: zero potential, non-zero field

−1.0 μC at (0, 1) and +1.0 μC at (1, 0). At the origin each gives 9000 N/C, at right angles, so \(E = \sqrt2\times9000 = 1.27\times10^4\) N/C at 135°. But the potentials are +9000 V and −9000 V, which cancel: \(V = 0\), and no work is needed to bring a charge there (\(W = qV = 0\)).

8. Potential of an Electric Dipole

Apply the group formula with \(n = 2\) to charges \(+q\) and \(-q\) at distances \(r_{(+)}\) and \(r_{(-)}\) from P:

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q}{r_{(+)}} - \frac{q}{r_{(-)}}\right) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{r_{(-)} - r_{(+)}}{r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)}} \]

Far away (\(r \gg d\)) the two lines to P are nearly parallel, so \( r_{(-)} - r_{(+)} \approx d\cos\theta \) and \( r_{(-)}r_{(+)} \approx r^2 \). With \(p = qd\):

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} \]
  • \(\theta\) is measured from the dipole axis (the direction of \(\vec p\), from − to +).
  • On the axis, \(\theta = 0^\circ\): \(V = kp/r^2\) (PPS-5 #7, ammonia).
  • On the perpendicular bisector, \(\theta = 90^\circ\): \(V = 0\) — even though the field there is not zero.

9. Continuous Charge Distribution

Split the charge into elements \(dq\), treat each as a point charge, and add (integrate) — still a scalar sum:

\[ dV = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{r} \]

Here \(r\) is the distance from P to each element \(dq\). If every element is the same distance away (a ring seen from its axis, or charges on an arc seen from the centre), \(r\) comes out of the integral and \(V = kQ/r\) — this is why the angles in PPS-5 #12 don't matter.

10. Equipotential Surfaces

Definition (slides): adjacent points that have the same electric potential form an equipotential surface, which can be an imaginary surface or a real, physical one.

  • No net work is done on a charged particle by the field when it moves between two points on the same equipotential: \(\Delta V = 0\), so \(W = -q\Delta V = 0\).
  • Equipotentials are always perpendicular to the field lines. (If \(\vec E\) had a component along the surface, moving along it would take work.)
  • They are closer together where the field is stronger.
  • The work between two equipotentials is the same along any path — it depends only on the end points.

11. Field from Potential (Gradient)

Moving a test charge \(ds\) from one equipotential to the next, the field does work \(-q_0\,dV\); the same work is \(q_0E\cos\theta\,ds\). Equating them:

\[ E_s = E\cos\theta = -\frac{dV}{ds} \]

The component of \(\vec E\) in any direction is minus the rate at which \(V\) changes in that direction. \(\vec E\) points the way \(V\) falls fastest.

12. Potential Energy of a System of Charges

The work needed to assemble charges from infinity is stored as potential energy. For one pair:

\[ U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r} \]

For more charges, add one term for every pair (three charges → three pairs, four → six):

\[ U = k\left(\frac{q_1q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2q_3}{r_{23}}\right) \]
  • Like charges → \(U > 0\) (work was needed to push them together).
  • Unlike charges → \(U < 0\) (the system would release energy coming together; an agent must do work to separate it).

Slide Problem-6: three charges on an equilateral triangle

\(q_1 = +q\), \(q_2 = -4q\), \(q_3 = +2q\), side \(d = 12\) cm, \(q = 150\) nC:

\[ U = \frac{k}{d}\big[(q)(-4q) + (q)(2q) + (-4q)(2q)\big] = -\frac{10kq^2}{d} = -1.7\times10^{-2}\ \text{J} \]

Energy conservation: closest approach

Two charges fired at each other slow down as \(U\) rises. At the closest point the kinetic energy has become potential energy (the slide method):

\[ K_{initial} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r_{min}} \]

Slide Problem-4: proton and alpha particle

Proton \((m, +e)\) and alpha \((4m, +2e)\), each at speed \(v\):

\[ \tfrac12mv^2 + \tfrac12(4m)v^2 = \frac{k(e)(2e)}{r}\ \Longrightarrow\ r = \frac{4ke^2}{5mv^2} \]
