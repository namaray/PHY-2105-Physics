📐 A. Core Theory & Formulas

1. Phase Shift & Phase Difference

Phase angle \( \phi \) in \( y = A\sin(\omega t + \phi) \)

  • For \( y = 0 \): \( \sin(\omega t + \phi) = 0 \), so \( \omega t = -\phi \rightarrow \) wave starts from \( \omega t = -\phi \)
  • Positive \( \phi \rightarrow \) wave shifts LEFT (leads)
  • Negative \( \phi \rightarrow \) wave shifts RIGHT (lags)

Phase difference between two waves: \( \Delta\phi = \phi_2 - \phi_1 \)

4 graphical cases:

  • \( y = A\sin(\omega t + \pi/4) \)
  • \( y = A\sin(\omega t - \pi/4) \)
  • \( y = A\sin(\omega t + \pi/2) \)
  • \( y = A\sin(\omega t + \pi/3) \)

2. Simple Pendulum

Valid for small angles only (\( \theta < 15^\circ \))

  • Period: \( T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \)
  • Frequency: \( f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \)
  • Angular frequency: \( \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \)

3. DHM — Mechanical System (Spring-Mass with Damping)

Definition: amplitude decreases with time due to friction.

  • Damping force: \( F = -bv \) where \( b \) is damping coefficient
  • Newton's second law: \( ma = -kx - bv \)
  • Differential equation: \( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \frac{b}{m}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{k}{m}x = 0 \)
  • Standard form: \( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + 2\alpha\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega_0^2x = 0 \)

Where: \( \alpha = \frac{b}{2m} \) (damping factor), \( \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \) (natural angular frequency)

Solution: \( x(t) = Ae^{-\alpha t}\cos(\omega_1 t + \phi) \)

  • Damped angular frequency: \( \omega_1 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2} \)
  • Damped frequency: \( f_1 = \frac{\omega_1}{2\pi} \)
  • Damped period: \( T_1 = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_1} \)
  • Damping amplitude: \( A_d(t) = Ae^{-\alpha t} \)
  • Energy: \( E(t) = E_0e^{-2\alpha t} = E_0e^{-\frac{bt}{m}} \)
  • Lifetime/relaxation time: \( \tau = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{1}{2\alpha} \)

Three types:

  • Underdamped: \( \omega_0 > \alpha \) (oscillatory with decaying amplitude)
  • Critically damped: \( \omega_0 = \alpha \) (no oscillation, fastest return)
  • Overdamped: \( \omega_0 < \alpha \) (no oscillation, slow return)

4. DHM — RLC Circuit (Electrical System)

Kirchhoff's voltage law: \( iR + \frac{Q}{C} + L\frac{di}{dt} = 0 \)

Differential equation: \( \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{R}{L}\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}Q = 0 \)

Comparing: \( \alpha = \frac{R}{2L} \), \( \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \)

Three cases:

  • Oscillatory: \( \frac{1}{LC} > \frac{R^2}{4L^2} \)
  • Critical damping: \( \frac{1}{LC} = \frac{R^2}{4L^2} \)
  • Overdamped: \( \frac{1}{LC} < \frac{R^2}{4L^2} \)

Solution: \( Q(t) = Ae^{-\frac{R}{2L}t}\cos(\omega_1 t + \phi) \)

  • \( \omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{R^2}{4L^2}} \)
  • Frequency: \( f_1 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{R^2}{4L^2}} \)
  • Resonant frequency: \( f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \)
  • Critical resistance (series): \( R_c = 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \)
  • Critical resistance (parallel): \( R_c = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \)

Mechanical-Electrical analogy: \( m\leftrightarrow L \), \( b\leftrightarrow R \), \( k\leftrightarrow \frac{1}{C} \), \( x\leftrightarrow Q \), \( v\leftrightarrow i \)

13. Series: oscillatory?

\[ R < 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]

If yes → underdamped. If \(R>R_c\) → overdamped, no oscillation.

14. Series: resonant frequency

\[ f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \]

The lossless value — exists even when the circuit is overdamped.

15. Series: frequency of oscillation

\[ f_d = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \left(\frac{R}{2L}\right)^2} \]

16. Parallel: oscillatory?

\[ R > \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]

Inequality flips. Parallel needs a large R to oscillate; series needs a small one.

17. Parallel: critical R & damping

\[ R_c = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}, \quad \alpha = \frac{1}{2RC} \]

Note \(\alpha\) uses \(1/2RC\) here, not \(R/2L\).

18. Parallel: frequency of oscillation

\[ f_d = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{1}{4R^2C^2}} \]

\(f_0\) is unchanged — only the damping term differs.

⚡ Series vs. parallel — the one thing to remember

Both use the same \( \omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC} \). Only the damping term changes: series \( \alpha = R/2L \), parallel \( \alpha = 1/2RC \). Everything else follows from \( \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2} \), and it oscillates whenever \( \alpha < \omega_0 \).

Because \(R\) sits on top in series but on the bottom in parallel, the oscillatory condition reverses: series wants \(R\) small, parallel wants \(R\) large. The critical values differ by a factor of 4: \( 2\sqrt{L/C} \) vs \( \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{L/C} \).

⚠️ Two different “relaxation time” conventions

Card 7 above (from the course slides) defines \( \tau = m/b = 1/2\alpha \) — the time for the energy to fall to \(1/e\). Some books instead use \( \tau = 2m/b = 1/\alpha \) — the time for the amplitude to fall to \(1/e\). They differ by a factor of 2.

In the exam, use your slide value \( \tau = m/b \) unless the question explicitly says amplitude. Note the worked solutions on the problems page currently use the amplitude convention \(1/\alpha\).
