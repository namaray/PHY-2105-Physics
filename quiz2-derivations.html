D1. SHM differential equation and its solution Foundation

Set up

A mass \( m \) on a spring of stiffness \( k \). The spring pulls back toward equilibrium, so the restoring force is proportional to the displacement and opposite in sign:

\[ F = -kx \]
Apply Newton's second law
\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \frac{k}{m}x = 0 \]
Define the natural frequency

Write \( \omega_0^2 = \dfrac{k}{m} \), which gives the standard SHM equation:

\[ \ddot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0 \]
Solve

Try \( x = e^{\lambda t} \). The auxiliary equation is \( \lambda^2 + \omega_0^2 = 0 \), giving \( \lambda = \pm i\omega_0 \). Combining the two complex exponentials into a real form:

Result
\[ x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi), \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \]

D2. Simple pendulum In syllabus

Set up

A bob of mass \( m \) on a light string of length \( L \), displaced by angle \( \theta \) from the vertical. The component of gravity along the arc is \( mg\sin\theta \), directed back toward equilibrium.

Take torques about the pivot
\[ \tau = -mgL\sin\theta, \qquad I = mL^2 \]

Using \( \tau = I\ddot{\theta} \):

\[ mL^2\ddot{\theta} = -mgL\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta \]
Small-angle approximation

For small \( \theta \) (in radians), \( \sin\theta \approx \theta \). This is the step that turns the pendulum into an SHM problem:

\[ \ddot{\theta} + \frac{g}{L}\theta = 0 \]
Compare with D1

This is \( \ddot{\theta} + \omega_0^2\theta = 0 \) with \( \omega_0^2 = \dfrac{g}{L} \). Then \( T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega_0} \).

Result
\[ \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \]

Note \( T \) is independent of the mass and (for small swings) of the amplitude.

D3. DHM mechanical system — the differential equation Named in N.B. 9

Identify the forces

Two forces act on the mass once it is moving:

  • Restoring force from the spring: \( F_s = -kx \)
  • Damping force from friction / viscous drag. It opposes the motion, so it is proportional to velocity and opposite in sign: \( F_d = -b\dfrac{dx}{dt} \)

Here \( b \) is the damping coefficient (units kg/s).

Apply Newton's second law
\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx - b\frac{dx}{dt} \]
Rearrange
\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0 \]

Divide through by \( m \):

\[ \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \frac{b}{m}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{k}{m}x = 0 \]
Substitute the standard symbols

Put \( \alpha = \dfrac{b}{2m} \) and \( \omega_0^2 = \dfrac{k}{m} \), so \( \dfrac{b}{m} = 2\alpha \):

Result — the DHM equation
\[ m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \ddot{x} + 2\alpha\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = 0 \]

The factor of 2 in \( 2\alpha \) is put there deliberately so the solution comes out clean in D4 — that is the only reason \( \alpha = b/2m \) rather than \( b/m \).

D4. Solving it — solution characteristics of DHM Named in N.B. 9

Trial solution

The equation is linear with constant coefficients, so try \( x = e^{\lambda t} \). Then \( \dot{x} = \lambda e^{\lambda t} \) and \( \ddot{x} = \lambda^2 e^{\lambda t} \). Substituting into \( \ddot{x} + 2\alpha\dot{x} + \omega_0^2x = 0 \):

\[ \left(\lambda^2 + 2\alpha\lambda + \omega_0^2\right)e^{\lambda t} = 0 \]

Since \( e^{\lambda t} \neq 0 \), the bracket must vanish.

Auxiliary equation
\[ \lambda^2 + 2\alpha\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0 \]

By the quadratic formula:

\[ \lambda = \frac{-2\alpha \pm \sqrt{4\alpha^2 - 4\omega_0^2}}{2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2} \]
General solution
\[ x(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\left[C_1 e^{\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2}\;t} + C_2 e^{-\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2}\;t}\right] \]
The key observation

Everything now hinges on the sign of \( \alpha^2 - \omega_0^2 \) under the square root:

  • \( \alpha < \omega_0 \) → root is imaginary → oscillation
  • \( \alpha = \omega_0 \) → root is zero → repeated root, special case
  • \( \alpha > \omega_0 \) → root is real → pure decay, no oscillation

Those three cases are D5.

D5. The three types of damping condition Named in N.B. 9

Case 1 — Underdamped (oscillatory): \( \alpha < \omega_0 \), i.e. \( b^2 < 4mk \)

Write \( \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2} = i\omega_d \) where \( \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2} \) is real. The bracket in D4 becomes a sum of \( e^{\pm i\omega_d t} \), which combines into a cosine:

\[ x(t) = A_0 e^{-\alpha t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi), \qquad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2} \]

Characteristics: oscillates, but inside a shrinking envelope \( \pm A_0e^{-\alpha t} \). The damped period \( T_d = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega_d} \) is slightly longer than the undamped period, because \( \omega_d < \omega_0 \). This is the only case that crosses the time axis.

Case 2 — Critically damped: \( \alpha = \omega_0 \), i.e. \( b^2 = 4mk \)

The square root vanishes, so both roots collapse to \( \lambda = -\alpha \). For a repeated root the second independent solution carries a factor of \( t \):

\[ x(t) = (C_1 + C_2 t)\,e^{-\alpha t} \]

The critical damping coefficient follows from \( \dfrac{b_c}{2m} = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}} \):

\[ b_c = 2m\omega_0 = 2\sqrt{mk} \]

Characteristics: no oscillation, and it returns to equilibrium faster than any other case. This is the boundary between oscillating and not.

Case 3 — Overdamped: \( \alpha > \omega_0 \), i.e. \( b^2 > 4mk \)

The square root is real, giving two distinct real negative roots:

\[ x(t) = C_1e^{\lambda_1 t} + C_2e^{\lambda_2 t}, \qquad \lambda_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2} \]

Characteristics: no oscillation, returns to equilibrium slower than critical damping. The slower root \( \lambda_1 = -\alpha + \sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2} \) is the one you actually see, since the other term dies almost immediately.

⚠️ The counter-intuitive bit

More damping does not mean a faster return. Past critical damping, adding damping makes the system slower to settle — for very heavy damping the slow root approaches \( -\dfrac{\omega_0^2}{2\alpha} \), which tends to zero as \( \alpha \) grows. This is exactly what P7 is testing.

D6. Amplitude decay, energy decay and relaxation time

Amplitude

From Case 1, the cosine oscillates between \( \pm 1 \), so the amplitude at time \( t \) is just the envelope:

\[ A(t) = A_0e^{-\alpha t} \]
Energy

The total energy of an oscillator goes as the square of the amplitude, \( E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 \). Squaring the envelope:

\[ E(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}kA_0^2e^{-2\alpha t} = E_0e^{-2\alpha t} = E_0e^{-\frac{bt}{m}} \]

Energy decays at twice the rate of amplitude. That single fact is the source of every factor-of-2 confusion in this topic.

Relaxation time (lifetime)

Defined as the time for the energy to fall to \( 1/e \) of its initial value. Set \( E = E_0/e \):

\[ e^{-2\alpha\tau} = e^{-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2\alpha\tau = 1 \]
Result
\[ \tau = \frac{1}{2\alpha} = \frac{m}{b} \]

⚠️ Two decay times — know which one is being asked

If a question asks when the amplitude falls to \( 1/e \), set \( e^{-\alpha t} = e^{-1} \) instead, giving \( t = \dfrac{1}{\alpha} = \dfrac{2m}{b} \) — twice as long.

Your slides define lifetime by the energy, so \( \tau = m/b \) is the default. Similarly, "time for the amplitude to halve" is \( \dfrac{\ln 2}{\alpha} \), while "time for the energy to halve" is \( \dfrac{\ln 2}{2\alpha} \).

D7. Logarithmic decrement — getting \( \alpha \) off a graph

Why you need it

In the graph problems (P29–P36) you are handed a curve and must recover \( \alpha \). Consecutive peaks are one damped period \( T_d \) apart, and both touch the envelope.

Take the ratio of successive peaks
\[ \frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}} = \frac{A_0e^{-\alpha t}}{A_0e^{-\alpha (t+T_d)}} = e^{\alpha T_d} \]

The ratio is the same for every consecutive pair — which is why a damped curve's peaks fall by a constant factor.

Take logs
\[ \delta = \ln\!\left(\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}\right) = \alpha T_d \]
Result — the graph-reading recipe
\[ \alpha = \frac{\delta}{T_d} = \frac{1}{T_d}\ln\!\left(\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}\right), \qquad b = 2m\alpha \]

Read \( T_d \) as the peak-to-peak spacing and \( A_n, A_{n+1} \) as two consecutive peak heights. This is exactly how P36(i) is solved.

D8. Series RLC circuit — the differential equation

Set up

A charged capacitor \( C \) discharging around a loop containing an inductor \( L \) and a resistor \( R \). Apply Kirchhoff's voltage law: the voltages around the loop sum to zero.

\[ V_L + V_R + V_C = 0 \]
Write each voltage
  • Inductor: \( V_L = L\dfrac{di}{dt} \)
  • Resistor: \( V_R = iR \)
  • Capacitor: \( V_C = \dfrac{Q}{C} \)
\[ L\frac{di}{dt} + iR + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \]
Express everything in terms of charge

Current is the rate of flow of charge, \( i = \dfrac{dQ}{dt} \), so \( \dfrac{di}{dt} = \dfrac{d^2Q}{dt^2} \):

\[ L\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + R\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \]

Divide by \( L \):

\[ \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{R}{L}\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}Q = 0 \]
Result — identical in form to D3

Compare term by term with \( \ddot{x} + 2\alpha\dot{x} + \omega_0^2x = 0 \):

\[ 2\alpha = \frac{R}{L} \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha = \frac{R}{2L}, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{1}{LC} \;\Rightarrow\; \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \]

Because the equation has the same form, every result from D4–D7 carries over unchanged — you only swap in the new \( \alpha \) and \( \omega_0 \).

D9. Series RLC — damping conditions

Apply the D5 condition

The discharge is oscillatory when \( \alpha < \omega_0 \). Substituting the series values from D8:

\[ \frac{R}{2L} < \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \]
Rearrange for R
\[ R < \frac{2L}{\sqrt{LC}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{L^2}{LC}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]
Results
\[ \text{oscillatory if } R < 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}, \qquad R_c = 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]

and the frequency of oscillation follows from \( \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2} \):

\[ f_d = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \left(\frac{R}{2L}\right)^2}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \]

Note: N.B. 9 has "solution characteristics of RLC ckt" struck out, so you will not be asked for this as a theory question — but the formulas above are still needed for the math problems P13–P18.

D10. Parallel RLC — why the inequality flips

Set up with Kirchhoff's current law

With \( R \), \( L \) and \( C \) all across the same node pair, the branch currents sum to zero:

\[ i_R + i_L + i_C = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{V}{R} + \frac{1}{L}\int V\,dt + C\frac{dV}{dt} = 0 \]
Differentiate to remove the integral
\[ C\frac{d^2V}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{R}\frac{dV}{dt} + \frac{V}{L} = 0 \]

Divide by \( C \):

\[ \frac{d^2V}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{RC}\frac{dV}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}V = 0 \]
Read off the coefficients
\[ 2\alpha = \frac{1}{RC} \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha = \frac{1}{2RC}, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \;\text{(unchanged)} \]
Apply the oscillatory condition
\[ \frac{1}{2RC} < \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R > \frac{\sqrt{LC}}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]
Result — and the reason it flips
\[ \text{oscillatory if } R > \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}, \qquad f_d = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{1}{4R^2C^2}} \]

Why the sign reverses: in series, \( R \) sits in the numerator of \( \alpha = R/2L \), so raising \( R \) adds damping. In parallel, \( R \) sits in the denominator of \( \alpha = 1/2RC \), so raising \( R \) removes damping. Physically: a large parallel resistor draws almost no current, so it barely dissipates energy.

The two critical resistances therefore differ by a factor of 4: \( 2\sqrt{L/C} \) versus \( \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{L/C} \).

D11. Mechanical ↔ electrical analogy

Because D3 and D8 produce the same differential equation, the two systems map onto each other term by term. If you know one, you know the other.

MechanicalElectrical (series)Why
displacement \( x \)charge \( Q \)the variable being solved for
velocity \( \dot{x} \)current \( i = \dot{Q} \)its first derivative
mass \( m \)inductance \( L \)resists change — inertia
damping \( b \)resistance \( R \)dissipates energy
stiffness \( k \)\( 1/C \)stores potential energy
force \( F \)EMF \( \varepsilon \)the driving term
\( \alpha = b/2m \)\( \alpha = R/2L \)damping factor
\( \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} \)\( \omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC} \)natural frequency

Check the last row against the analogy: substituting \( m\to L \), \( k\to 1/C \) into \( 2\sqrt{mk} \) gives \( 2\sqrt{L/C} \). ✓

D12. Phase difference and phase shift In syllabus

The wave and its phase

For a wave travelling in the \( +x \) direction:

\[ y = A\sin(\omega t - kx + \phi_0), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\ \ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} \]

The whole bracket is the phase. Anything that changes it changes where in the cycle the particle sits.

Two points at the same instant (path → phase)

Freeze time and move a distance \( \Delta x \). Only the \( -kx \) term changes:

\[ \Delta\phi = k\,\Delta x = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x \]

So a path difference of one whole wavelength is a phase difference of \( 2\pi \) — in phase.

One point at two instants (time → phase)

Stay at one place and let time advance by \( \Delta t \). Only \( \omega t \) changes:

\[ \Delta\phi = \omega\,\Delta t = \frac{2\pi}{T}\Delta t \]
The two you actually use
\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{T}\Delta t \]

Phase shift \( \phi_0 \) slides the whole curve sideways: \( +\phi_0 \) shifts it left (leads), \( -\phi_0 \) shifts it right (lags). A shift of \( \phi_0 \) corresponds to a horizontal move of \( \dfrac{\phi_0}{2\pi}T \) on a \(y\)–\(t\) graph, or \( \dfrac{\phi_0}{2\pi}\lambda \) on a \(y\)–\(x\) graph.

🎯 If you only memorise three things

1. \( m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = 0 \) comes straight from Newton with \( F_{spring} = -kx \) and \( F_{damping} = -b\dot{x} \) (D3).

2. Trying \( x = e^{\lambda t} \) gives \( \lambda = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2} \), and the sign under that root decides underdamped / critical / overdamped (D4–D5).

3. The RLC circuit is the same equation with \( m\to L \), \( b\to R \), \( k\to 1/C \) (D8, D11).

Almost every short question in this topic is one of those three, or a direct consequence of them.
