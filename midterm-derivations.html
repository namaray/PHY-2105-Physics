Part A — Simple Harmonic Motion

A1. SHM differential equation and its solution Midterm

Set up

A mass \( m \) on a spring of stiffness \( k \). The spring pulls back toward equilibrium, so the restoring force is proportional to displacement and opposite in sign:

\[ F = -kx \]
Newton's second law
\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \frac{k}{m}x = 0 \]
Solve

Write \( \omega_0^2 = \dfrac{k}{m} \), giving \( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \omega_0^2x = 0 \). Trying \( x = e^{\lambda t} \) gives \( \lambda^2 + \omega_0^2 = 0 \), so \( \lambda = \pm i\omega_0 \). Combining into real form:

Result
\[ x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi), \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \]

Differentiating: \( v = -A\omega_0\sin(\omega_0t+\phi) \) so \( v_{max} = A\omega_0 \), and \( a = -A\omega_0^2\cos(\omega_0t+\phi) = -\omega_0^2x \) so \( a_{max} = A\omega_0^2 \).

A2. Energy in SHM Midterm

Write both energies

With \( x = A\cos(\omega_0t+\phi) \) and \( v = -A\omega_0\sin(\omega_0t+\phi) \):

\[ K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mA^2\omega_0^2\sin^2(\omega_0t+\phi) \]
\[ U = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\cos^2(\omega_0t+\phi) \]
Add them

Since \( k = m\omega_0^2 \), the two prefactors are equal: \( \tfrac{1}{2}mA^2\omega_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 \). So:

\[ E = K + U = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2\left[\sin^2(\omega_0t+\phi) + \cos^2(\omega_0t+\phi)\right] \]

The bracket is 1 by the Pythagorean identity — and that is the whole trick.

Result
\[ E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega_0^2A^2 = \text{constant} \]
Useful corollary — velocity at any position

Energy conservation says \( \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 \). Solving for \( v \):

\[ v(x) = \pm\omega_0\sqrt{A^2 - x^2} \]

Maximum at \( x=0 \) (gives \( A\omega_0 \)), zero at \( x = \pm A \). Note \( K \) and \( U \) each average to \( \tfrac{1}{4}kA^2 \) over a cycle.

A3. Simple pendulum Quiz-2 too

Set up

A bob of mass \( m \) on a light string of length \( L \), displaced by angle \( \theta \). The component of gravity along the arc is \( mg\sin\theta \), directed back toward equilibrium, so \( F = -mg\sin\theta \).

Take torques about the pivot
\[ \tau = -mgL\sin\theta, \qquad I = mL^2 \]

Using \( \tau = I\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} \):

\[ mL^2\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -mgL\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta \]
Small-angle approximation

For small \( \theta \) in radians, \( \sin\theta \approx \theta \). This is the step that makes it SHM:

\[ \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + \frac{g}{L}\theta = 0 \]
Result
\[ \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \]

Independent of the bob's mass, and (for small swings) of the amplitude.

A4. General (compound) pendulum Midterm

Energy method

For a rigid body pivoted a distance \( L \) from its centre of mass, the gravitational PE is \( mgL(1-\cos\theta) \). Using \( \cos\theta \approx 1 - \dfrac{\theta^2}{2} \) for small angles, this becomes \( \tfrac{1}{2}mgL\theta^2 \). With moment of inertia \( I \):

\[ E = \tfrac{1}{2}I\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}mgL\theta^2 = \text{constant} \]
Differentiate with respect to time

The total energy is constant, so its time derivative is zero:

\[ I\frac{d\theta}{dt}\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + mgL\theta\frac{d\theta}{dt} = 0 \]

Divide through by \( \frac{d\theta}{dt} \) (non-zero except at the turning points):

\[ I\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + mgL\theta = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac{mgL}{I}\theta \]
Result
\[ \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{mgL}{I}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgL}} \]

Check: for a simple pendulum all the mass sits at distance \( L \), so \( I = mL^2 \) and \( \omega_0 = \sqrt{mgL/mL^2} = \sqrt{g/L} \) ✓ — A3 is the special case.

Part B — Damped Harmonic Motion

B1. The DHM differential equation Quiz-2 too

Identify the forces
  • Restoring force from the spring: \( F_s = -kx \)
  • Damping force from friction or viscous drag. It opposes the motion, so it is proportional to velocity and opposite in sign: \( F_d = -b\dfrac{dx}{dt} \)

Here \( b \) is the damping coefficient, units kg/s.

Newton's second law, then rearrange
\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx - b\frac{dx}{dt} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0 \]

Divide by \( m \) and put \( \alpha = \dfrac{b}{2m} \), \( \omega_0^2 = \dfrac{k}{m} \):

Result — the DHM equation
\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + 2\alpha\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega_0^2x = 0 \]

The factor of 2 in \( 2\alpha \) is chosen so the solution comes out clean in B2 — that is the only reason \( \alpha = b/2m \) rather than \( b/m \).

B2. Solving it — solution characteristics Quiz-2 too

Trial solution

Linear with constant coefficients, so try \( x = e^{\lambda t} \). Then \( \frac{dx}{dt} = \lambda e^{\lambda t} \), \( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = \lambda^2 e^{\lambda t} \), and substituting:

\[ \left(\lambda^2 + 2\alpha\lambda + \omega_0^2\right)e^{\lambda t} = 0 \]

Since \( e^{\lambda t} \neq 0 \), the bracket must vanish.

Auxiliary equation
\[ \lambda^2 + 2\alpha\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2} \]
General solution
\[ x(t) = e^{-\alpha t}\left[C_1 e^{\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2}\;t} + C_2 e^{-\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2}\;t}\right] \]
The key observation

Everything hinges on the sign of \( \alpha^2 - \omega_0^2 \) under the root:

  • \( \alpha < \omega_0 \) → root imaginary → oscillation
  • \( \alpha = \omega_0 \) → root zero → repeated root
  • \( \alpha > \omega_0 \) → root real → pure decay

B3. The three types of damping condition Quiz-2 too

Case 1 — Underdamped (oscillatory): \( \alpha < \omega_0 \), i.e. \( b^2 < 4mk \)

Write \( \sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2} = i\omega_d \) with \( \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2-\alpha^2} \) real. The bracket becomes a sum of \( e^{\pm i\omega_dt} \), which combines into a cosine:

\[ x(t) = A_0e^{-\alpha t}\cos(\omega_d t + \phi), \qquad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2} \]

Characteristics: oscillates inside a shrinking envelope \( \pm A_0e^{-\alpha t} \). The damped period \( T_d = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega_d} \) is slightly longer than the undamped one since \( \omega_d < \omega_0 \). The only case that crosses the time axis.

Case 2 — Critically damped: \( \alpha = \omega_0 \), i.e. \( b^2 = 4mk \)

The root vanishes and both solutions collapse to \( \lambda = -\alpha \). For a repeated root the second independent solution carries a factor \( t \):

\[ x(t) = (C_1 + C_2t)e^{-\alpha t} \]

From \( \dfrac{b_c}{2m} = \sqrt{\dfrac{k}{m}} \):

\[ b_c = 2m\omega_0 = 2\sqrt{mk} \]

Characteristics: no oscillation, and it returns to equilibrium faster than any other case. The boundary between oscillating and not.

Case 3 — Overdamped: \( \alpha > \omega_0 \), i.e. \( b^2 > 4mk \)

\[ x(t) = C_1e^{\lambda_1t} + C_2e^{\lambda_2t}, \qquad \lambda_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2} \]

Characteristics: no oscillation, returns slower than critical damping. Only the slower root \( \lambda_1 \) is visible — the other term dies almost immediately.

⚠️ The counter-intuitive bit

More damping does not mean a faster return. Past critical damping, extra damping makes the system slower to settle: for heavy damping the slow root tends to \( -\dfrac{\omega_0^2}{2\alpha} \), which shrinks as \( \alpha \) grows.

B4. Amplitude decay, energy decay, relaxation time Quiz-2 too

Amplitude, then energy

From Case 1 the cosine runs between \( \pm 1 \), so the amplitude is the envelope \( A(t) = A_0e^{-\alpha t} \). Since \( E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 \), squaring gives:

\[ E(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}kA_0^2e^{-2\alpha t} = E_0e^{-2\alpha t} = E_0e^{-\frac{bt}{m}} \]

Energy decays at twice the rate of amplitude. Every factor-of-2 confusion in this topic traces back to that one line.

Relaxation time (lifetime)

Defined as the time for the energy to fall to \( 1/e \). Setting \( e^{-2\alpha\tau} = e^{-1} \):

Result
\[ \tau = \frac{1}{2\alpha} = \frac{m}{b} \]

⚠️ Two decay times — know which is asked

If a question asks when the amplitude falls to \( 1/e \), set \( e^{-\alpha t} = e^{-1} \) instead, giving \( t = \dfrac{1}{\alpha} = \dfrac{2m}{b} \) — twice as long.

Likewise "amplitude halves" is \( \dfrac{\ln 2}{\alpha} \) but "energy halves" is \( \dfrac{\ln 2}{2\alpha} \).

B5. Logarithmic decrement — \( \alpha \) from a graph Quiz-2 too

Ratio of successive peaks

Consecutive peaks are one damped period \( T_d \) apart and both touch the envelope:

\[ \frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}} = \frac{A_0e^{-\alpha t}}{A_0e^{-\alpha(t+T_d)}} = e^{\alpha T_d} \]

The ratio is the same for every pair — which is why a damped curve's peaks fall by a constant factor.

Take logs
\[ \delta = \ln\!\left(\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}\right) = \alpha T_d \]
Result — the graph-reading recipe
\[ \alpha = \frac{\delta}{T_d} = \frac{1}{T_d}\ln\!\left(\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}}\right), \qquad b = 2m\alpha \]

Read \( T_d \) as the peak-to-peak spacing, \( A_n \) and \( A_{n+1} \) as consecutive peak heights.

Part C — Forced Oscillations & Resonance

✅ Confirmed in scope

Part 1 of the “Waves and Oscillation” slide is listed as Simple harmonic oscillators · Damped oscillators · Driven oscillators · Resonance · The Wave Equation, so this part is definitely in. (That also answers the “DHO?” question — Driven Harmonic Oscillation.)

C1. The driven equation and its steady state Midterm

Add a driving force

Take the DHM equation of B1 and add a periodic external force \( F_0\sin\omega t \), where \( \omega \) is the driving frequency (not the natural one):

\[ m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = F_0\sin\omega t \]
Steady state

The homogeneous part (Part B) decays away as \( e^{-\alpha t} \). What survives is the particular solution, oscillating at the driving frequency but lagging it by a phase \( \phi_0 \):

\[ x(t) = A_0\sin(\omega t + \phi_0) \]

Substituting this in and collecting the \( \sin\omega t \) and \( \cos\omega t \) terms gives the amplitude:

Result
\[ A_0 = \frac{F_0}{m\sqrt{\left(\omega^2 - \omega_0^2\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{b\omega}{m}\right)^2}} \]
\[ \tan\phi_0 = \frac{b\omega}{m\left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\right)} \]

Note the amplitude depends on how far the driving frequency sits from \( \omega_0 \) — the \( (\omega^2-\omega_0^2)^2 \) term. That is what makes resonance happen.

C2. Resonance and the Q factor Midterm

Maximise the amplitude

\( A_0 \) is largest when the quantity under the root is smallest. Differentiate it with respect to \( \omega \) and set to zero:

\[ \frac{d}{d\omega}\left[\left(\omega^2-\omega_0^2\right)^2 + \frac{b^2\omega^2}{m^2}\right] = 4\omega\left(\omega^2-\omega_0^2\right) + \frac{2b^2\omega}{m^2} = 0 \]
Solve for the resonant frequency

Divide by \( 2\omega \) and rearrange, using \( \dfrac{b}{2m} = \alpha \) so \( \dfrac{b^2}{m^2} = 4\alpha^2 \):

\[ 2\left(\omega^2-\omega_0^2\right) + 4\alpha^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - 2\alpha^2 \]
Results
\[ \omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\alpha^2} \quad\text{(amplitude resonance)} \]

Velocity resonance occurs exactly at \( \omega = \omega_0 \) — unshifted by damping. The Q factor measures how sharp the peak is:

\[ Q = \frac{m\omega_0}{b} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\alpha} \]

Light damping → large \( Q \) → tall narrow peak, and \( \omega_r \to \omega_0 \). Heavy damping → small \( Q \) → broad flat peak. If \( 2\alpha^2 > \omega_0^2 \) there is no peak at all.

Part D — LC and RLC Circuits

D1. LC circuit — undamped electrical SHM Midterm

Kirchhoff's voltage law

A charged capacitor \( C \) discharging through an inductor \( L \), with no resistance. The two voltages sum to zero:

\[ L\frac{di}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \]
Write in terms of charge

With \( i = \dfrac{dQ}{dt} \), so \( \dfrac{di}{dt} = \dfrac{d^2Q}{dt^2} \):

\[ L\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{LC}Q = 0 \]

This is exactly the SHM equation of A1 with \( x \to Q \) and \( \omega_0^2 = \dfrac{1}{LC} \).

Result
\[ Q(t) = Q_0\cos(\omega_0t + \phi), \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \]
\[ f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{LC} \]

Energy view: \( \dfrac{Q^2}{2C} \) in the capacitor and \( \dfrac{1}{2}Li^2 \) in the inductor trade back and forth, total constant — the exact analogue of PE ↔ KE in A2. With no \( R \) there is nothing to dissipate it, so it never dies.

D2. Series RLC — the differential equation Quiz-2 too

Kirchhoff's voltage law

Now add a resistor. Around the loop, \( V_L + V_R + V_C = 0 \), with \( V_L = L\dfrac{di}{dt} \), \( V_R = iR \), \( V_C = \dfrac{Q}{C} \):

\[ L\frac{di}{dt} + iR + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \]
Express in charge and divide by L
\[ L\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + R\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{R}{L}\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}Q = 0 \]
Result — identical in form to B1

Compare term by term with \( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + 2\alpha\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega_0^2x = 0 \):

\[ \alpha = \frac{R}{2L}, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \]

Because the equation has the same form, every result from B2–B5 carries over unchanged — just swap in the new \( \alpha \) and \( \omega_0 \). Setting \( R = 0 \) recovers D1.

D3. Series RLC — damping conditions Quiz-2 too

Apply the B3 condition

Oscillatory when \( \alpha < \omega_0 \). Substituting the series values:

\[ \frac{R}{2L} < \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R < \frac{2L}{\sqrt{LC}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]
Results
\[ \text{oscillatory if } R < 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}, \qquad R_c = 2\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]
\[ f_d = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \left(\frac{R}{2L}\right)^2}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \]

D4. Parallel RLC — why the inequality flips Quiz-2 too

Kirchhoff's current law

With \( R \), \( L \), \( C \) all across the same node pair, the branch currents sum to zero:

\[ \frac{V}{R} + \frac{1}{L}\int V\,dt + C\frac{dV}{dt} = 0 \]
Differentiate to remove the integral, then divide by C
\[ \frac{d^2V}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{RC}\frac{dV}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}V = 0 \]
\[ \Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = \frac{1}{2RC}, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}\ \text{(unchanged)} \]
Apply the oscillatory condition
\[ \frac{1}{2RC} < \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R > \frac{\sqrt{LC}}{2C} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \]
Result — and the reason it flips
\[ \text{oscillatory if } R > \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}, \qquad f_d = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC} - \frac{1}{4R^2C^2}} \]

Why the sign reverses: in series \( R \) is in the numerator of \( \alpha = R/2L \), so raising \( R \) adds damping. In parallel it is in the denominator of \( \alpha = 1/2RC \), so raising \( R \) removes damping. Physically, a large parallel resistor draws almost no current, so it barely dissipates.

The two critical resistances differ by a factor of 4: \( 2\sqrt{L/C} \) versus \( \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{L/C} \).

D5. Mechanical ↔ electrical analogy Quiz-2 too

B1 and D2 produce the same differential equation, so the systems map term by term. The whole analogy rests on three swaps:

\[ m \to L, \qquad b \to R, \qquad k \to \frac{1}{C} \]

Everything else in the table follows by substituting those into a formula you already know.

⚠️ \( k \to 1/C \), and \( g \) is not in the table

Stiffness maps to the reciprocal of capacitance, so a stiff spring behaves like a small capacitor. The symbol \( g \) never appears in the analogy — it enters only through the pendulum, where \( g/L_{pend} \) plays the same role as \( k/m \) and \( 1/LC \):

\[ \omega_0^2 \;=\; \frac{k}{m} \;=\; \frac{g}{L_{pend}} \;=\; \frac{1}{LC} \]

Also beware that \( L \) is inductance in a circuit but length in a pendulum — same letter, unrelated quantities.

Finally, this substitution is for the series circuit only. Parallel RLC has \( \alpha = \dfrac{1}{2RC} \) (D4) and does not follow it.

Part E — Wave Equation

✅ Explicitly included

The contents taught in the Wave Equation section of the “Waves and Oscillations” slide are in the Mid Exam. Classification of Waves is not — so skip the types-of-waves slide, but know everything below.

E1. The progressive wave equation Midterm

Start at the origin

A particle at \( x=0 \) executes SHM as the wave passes: \( y = A\sin\omega t \).

A particle further along lags behind

A wave travelling left to right with speed \( v \) reaches \( x \) later, so that particle repeats the same motion but delayed — it lags in phase by

\[ \phi = \frac{2\pi x}{\lambda} \]

(one whole wavelength of separation = one full cycle of lag = \( 2\pi \)).

Subtract the lag
\[ y = A\sin\left(\omega t - \frac{2\pi x}{\lambda}\right) \]

Using \( \omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} \), factor out \( 2\pi \):

\[ y = A\sin 2\pi\left(\frac{t}{T} - \frac{x}{\lambda}\right) \]

and using \( v = \dfrac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda \), factor out \( \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda} \):

Result — the same wave, three ways
\[ y = A\sin(\omega t - kx) = A\sin 2\pi\left(\frac{t}{T} - \frac{x}{\lambda}\right) = A\sin\left[\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(vt - x)\right] \]

with \( k = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda} \) the wave number and \( v = \dfrac{\omega}{k} = f\lambda \) the wave speed. A minus sign before \( x \) means travelling in \( +x \); a plus sign means \( -x \).

E2. The differential equation of wave motion Midterm

Differentiate twice with respect to t

Starting from \( y = A\sin\left[\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(vt-x)\right] \):

\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = A\frac{2\pi v}{\lambda}\cos\left[\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(vt-x)\right] \]
\[ \frac{d^2y}{dt^2} = -A\frac{4\pi^2v^2}{\lambda^2}\sin\left[\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(vt-x)\right] \]
Differentiate twice with respect to x
\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = -A\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cos\left[\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(vt-x)\right] \]
\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -A\frac{4\pi^2}{\lambda^2}\sin\left[\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(vt-x)\right] \]
Compare

Both second derivatives contain the same sine factor. Dividing one by the other, everything cancels except \( v^2 \):

Result — the wave equation
\[ \frac{d^2y}{dt^2} = v^2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \]

Any function of the form \( y = f(vt \mp x) \) satisfies this — not just sines. That is why it is the wave equation: the coefficient of \( d^2y/dx^2 \) is always the square of the wave speed.

E3. Particle velocity vs. wave velocity Midterm

Two different things called "velocity"
  • Wave velocity \( v = f\lambda \) — how fast the pattern moves along \( x \). Constant.
  • Particle velocity \( \dfrac{dy}{dt} \) — how fast one particle moves up and down. Oscillates.
Relate them

Take the two first derivatives from E2 and divide:

\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = A\frac{2\pi v}{\lambda}\cos[\cdot], \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = -A\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cos[\cdot] \]
Result
\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = -v\frac{dy}{dx} \]

Particle velocity = −(wave velocity) × (slope of the waveform). Its maximum is \( v_{max} = A\omega \), which has nothing to do with the wave speed — a common exam trap. Maximum particle acceleration is \( A\omega^2 \).

E4. Stationary (standing) waves Midterm

Superpose two opposite-travelling waves

Same amplitude and frequency, opposite directions:

\[ y_1 = A\sin(\omega t - kx), \qquad y_2 = A\sin(\omega t + kx) \]
Add using the sum-to-product identity

\( \sin P + \sin Q = 2\sin\dfrac{P+Q}{2}\cos\dfrac{P-Q}{2} \). Here \( \dfrac{P+Q}{2} = \omega t \) and \( \dfrac{P-Q}{2} = -kx \), and cosine is even:

\[ y = y_1 + y_2 = 2A\sin(\omega t)\cos(kx) \]
Result — and why it does not travel
\[ y = \underbrace{2A\cos kx}_{\text{amplitude, depends on }x}\ \cdot\ \underbrace{\sin\omega t}_{\text{same for all }x} \]

The \( x \) and \( t \) dependence have separated. Every particle oscillates in step; only the amplitude varies with position. So the profile does not move, and no energy is transported.

  • Nodes (always still): \( \cos kx = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{\lambda}{4}, \dfrac{3\lambda}{4}, \dfrac{5\lambda}{4},\ldots \)
  • Antinodes (maximum swing \( 2A \)): \( \cos kx = \pm 1 \Rightarrow x = 0, \dfrac{\lambda}{2}, \lambda,\ldots \)

Adjacent nodes are \( \lambda/2 \) apart; a node and its neighbouring antinode are \( \lambda/4 \) apart.

E5. Superposition & Lissajous figures Midterm

Two perpendicular SHMs

A particle driven simultaneously by two SHMs at right angles traces a Lissajous figure:

\[ x = A_1\cos\omega t, \qquad y = A_2\cos(\omega t + \phi) \]
Case \( \phi = 0 \) — in phase

Then \( \dfrac{x}{A_1} = \dfrac{y}{A_2} \), so \( y = \dfrac{A_2}{A_1}x \) — a straight line through the origin.

Case \( \phi = \pi/2 \) — quarter cycle apart

\( y = A_2\cos(\omega t + \pi/2) = -A_2\sin\omega t \). Then \( \dfrac{x}{A_1} = \cos\omega t \) and \( \dfrac{y}{A_2} = -\sin\omega t \). Squaring and adding kills the time dependence:

\[ \frac{x^2}{A_1^2} + \frac{y^2}{A_2^2} = \cos^2\omega t + \sin^2\omega t = 1 \]

An ellipse — and a circle if \( A_1 = A_2 \).

Summary

The shape depends on the ratio of the two periods, the phase difference, and the two amplitudes. For equal frequencies: \( \phi = 0 \) or \( \pi \) gives a line, \( \phi = \pi/2 \) gives an ellipse or circle, and anything between gives a tilted ellipse.

Lissajous figures were excluded from Quiz-2 but are back in for the midterm — they sit in the Waves and Oscillation slide, not in the excluded “Classification of Waves” section.

Part F — Phase

F1. Phase difference and phase shift Quiz-2 too

The phase
\[ y = A\sin(\omega t - kx + \phi_0), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda},\ \ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} \]

The whole bracket is the phase. Anything that changes it changes where in the cycle the particle sits.

Two points at the same instant (path → phase)

Freeze time, move a distance \( \Delta x \). Only \( -kx \) changes:

\[ \Delta\phi = k\,\Delta x = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x \]
One point at two instants (time → phase)

Stay put, let time advance by \( \Delta t \). Only \( \omega t \) changes:

\[ \Delta\phi = \omega\,\Delta t = \frac{2\pi}{T}\Delta t \]
The two you actually use
\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{T}\Delta t \]

Phase shift \( \phi_0 \) slides the whole curve sideways: \( +\phi_0 \) shifts it left (leads), \( -\phi_0 \) shifts it right (lags). A shift of \( \phi_0 \) is a horizontal move of \( \dfrac{\phi_0}{2\pi}T \) on a \(y\)–\(t\) graph or \( \dfrac{\phi_0}{2\pi}\lambda \) on a \(y\)–\(x\) graph.

🎯 The spine of the whole syllabus

Almost every derivation above is one equation wearing a different costume:

\[ \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + 2\alpha\frac{dx}{dt} + \omega_0^2 x = 0 \]
  • Set \( \alpha = 0 \) → SHM (A1) and the LC circuit (D1)
  • Keep \( \alpha \) → DHM (B1) and the RLC circuit (D2)
  • Put a driving term on the right → forced oscillation and resonance (C1)
  • Swap \( m\to L,\ b\to R,\ k\to 1/C \) → every mechanical result becomes an electrical one (D5)

The sign of \( \alpha^2 - \omega_0^2 \) decides underdamped / critical / overdamped, every single time. Waves (Part E) are the one genuinely separate strand.
