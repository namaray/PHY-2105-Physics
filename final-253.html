Q1 (a) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

Step 1. With \(r \to 2r\):

\[ F' = \frac{kq^2}{(2r)^2} = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{kq^2}{r^2} = \frac{F}{4} \]
🧭 Why? Coulomb's law is an inverse-square law: the force depends on 1/r², so doubling r divides the force by 2² = 4. The charges are still alike, so the force stays repulsive — only its size changes.
Answer: The force becomes one quarter of its original value (still repulsive).

Q1 (b) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ W = q\,\Delta V = q(V_B - V_A) \]

work done to move q (slowly) from A to B

Step 1. \(V_A = V_B = V\), so

\[ W = q(V - V) = 0 \]
🧭 Why? A and B lie on the same equipotential surface. The field is perpendicular to an equipotential, so moving along it the force has no component along the motion — and the work depends only on the end points, so any path from A to B gives zero.
Answer: Zero work, because ΔV = 0 between points at the same potential (W = qΔV).

Q2 Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +5\ \mu\)C, \(q_2 = -10\ \mu\)C, 10 cm apart
  • \(q_0 = +1\ \mu\)C at the midpoint → 5 cm = 0.05 m from each
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F = F_1 + F_2 // (\text{same direction}) \]
\[ F_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(5\times10^{-6})(1\times10^{-6})}{(0.05)^2} = 18.0\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10\times10^{-6})(1\times10^{-6})}{(0.05)^2} = 36.0\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? q₀ is positive. +5 μC repels it → pushes it away from +5 μC, toward −10 μC. −10 μC attracts it → pulls it toward −10 μC. Both forces point the same way, so they add. (Between unlike charges forces never cancel.)
\[ F = 18.0 + 36.0 = 53.9\ \text{N} \]
Answer: 53.9 N, directed from the +5 μC charge toward the −10 μC charge.

Q3 Math 5 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = 5\) C at distance \(d = 2\) m to the left of P; \(q_2 = 10\) C at distance \(d\) from P, 60° above that line
  • \(q_0 = 1\) nC at P; charges taken as positive
  • Axes: P at the origin, +x to the right
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F_x = \sum F\cos\theta,\quad F_y = \sum F\sin\theta,\quad F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(5)(10^{-9})}{2^2} = 11.24\ \text{N} \\ F_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10)(10^{-9})}{2^2} = 22.48\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? All positive → both forces are repulsions, pointing away from each source. q₁ is directly left of P → F₁ points along +x. q₂ is up-left at 60° → F₂ points down-right, 60° below +x.

Step 1. (i) Components.

🧮 How to get the components Put the force (or field) on a right triangle. If θ is its angle with the horizontal:
\[ F_x = F\cos\theta,\qquad F_y = F\sin\theta \]
Then give each part a sign from the way it points: right / up = +, left / down = −. cos θ and sin θ come straight from the geometry: cos θ = (horizontal side) / (hypotenuse), sin θ = (vertical side) / (hypotenuse).

F₁ lies along +x, so it is all horizontal:

\[ \begin{gathered} F_{1x} = +11.24\ \text{N} \\ F_{1y} = 0 \end{gathered} \]

F₂ points 60° below +x (straight away from q₂, which sits 60° above the line to q₁):

\[ \begin{gathered} F_{2x} = +F_2\cos60^\circ = +(22.48)(0.5) = +11.24\ \text{N} \\ (\text{right}) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F_{2y} = -F_2\sin60^\circ = -(22.48)(0.866) = -19.46\ \text{N} \\ (\text{down}) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F_x = F_{1x} + F_{2x} = 11.24 + 11.24 = 22.48\ \text{N} \\ F_y = 0 + (-19.46) = -19.46\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. (ii) Resultant:

\[ \begin{gathered} F = \sqrt{22.48^2 + 19.46^2} = 29.7\ \text{N} \\ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{19.46}{22.48} = 40.9^\circ\ \text{below } +x \end{gathered} \]
Answer: (i) \(F_x = +22.5\) N, \(F_y = -19.5\) N. (ii) \(F = 29.7\) N at 40.9° below the +x axis (319.1°).

Q4 Math 5 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = -10\) C at \(x = -3\) m, \(q_2 = +5\) C at \(x = +3\) m
  • P at (0, 4) m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge — away from +, toward −

\[ \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 \]

Step 1. Distances: \(r = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\) m for both.

\[ \begin{gathered} E_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10)}{25} = 3.60\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \\ E_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(5)}{25} = 1.80\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? q₁ is negative → E₁ points toward q₁: down-left, along (−0.6, −0.8). q₂ is positive → E₂ points away from q₂: up-left, along (−0.6, +0.8). Both lean left; their vertical parts partly cancel.
🧮 How to get the components Put the force (or field) on a right triangle. If θ is its angle with the horizontal:
\[ F_x = F\cos\theta,\qquad F_y = F\sin\theta \]
Then give each part a sign from the way it points: right / up = +, left / down = −. cos θ and sin θ come straight from the geometry: cos θ = (horizontal side) / (hypotenuse), sin θ = (vertical side) / (hypotenuse).

From P to either charge is 3 m sideways and 4 m down, length 5 m, so cos θ = 3/5 = 0.6 and sin θ = 4/5 = 0.8 (θ = 53.1° from the horizontal).

\[ \begin{gathered} E_{1x} = -E_1\cos\theta = -(3.60\times10^9)(0.6) = -2.16\times10^{9} \\ (\text{left}) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} E_{1y} = -E_1\sin\theta = -(3.60\times10^9)(0.8) = -2.88\times10^{9} \\ (\text{down}) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} E_{2x} = -E_2\cos\theta = -(1.80\times10^9)(0.6) = -1.08\times10^{9} \\ (\text{left}) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} E_{2y} = +E_2\sin\theta = +(1.80\times10^9)(0.8) = +1.44\times10^{9} \\ (\text{up}) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} E_x = -2.16 - 1.08 = -3.24\times10^{9} \\ E_y = -2.88 + 1.44 = -1.44\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} E = \sqrt{3.24^2 + 1.44^2}\times10^9 = 3.54\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \\ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{1.44}{3.24} = 24.0^\circ \end{gathered} \]
Answer: (i) \(3.54\times10^9\) N/C; (ii) 24.0° below the −x axis (204° from +x), i.e. down and to the left.

Q5 Math 6 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(\vec p = (5\hat i + 2\hat j)\times10^{-30}\) C·m
  • \(\vec E = (5000\hat i + 6000\hat j)\) N/C
📐 Formula
\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

torque

\[ U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

potential energy

Step 1. (i) Both vectors lie in the xy-plane, so only the k̂ part survives:

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec\tau = (p_xE_y - p_yE_x)\,\hat k \\ = [(5)(6000) - (2)(5000)]\times10^{-30}\,\hat k = 2.0\times10^{-26}\,\hat k\ \text{N·m} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? +k̂ is out of the page: the torque turns p counter-clockwise, toward E (p is at 21.8°, E at 50.2°, so p has to rotate up by 28.4° to line up).

Step 2. (ii)

\[ U = -[(5)(5000) + (2)(6000)]\times10^{-30} = -3.7\times10^{-26}\ \text{J} \]
Answer: (i) \(\vec\tau = (2.0\times10^{-26}\ \text{N·m})\,\hat k\) — magnitude \(2.0\times10^{-26}\) N·m, along +z (out of the page). (ii) \(U = -3.7\times10^{-26}\) J.

Q8 (a) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution
🧠 The idea before the maths

A dipole is a +q and a −q a small distance d apart. At a point P on the axis beyond +q, the +q is closer, so it pushes P away strongly; the −q is farther, so it pulls back a bit more weakly. The two fields point opposite ways and nearly cancel.

So the plan is: write each field with Coulomb's law, subtract them, tidy the fraction, and finally use "P is far away" (z ≫ d) to simplify.

Because the fields nearly cancel, what's left falls off as 1/z³ — faster than one charge's 1/z².

Step 1. Set up. Put the dipole on the z-axis with its centre at the origin: +q at z = +d/2, −q at z = −d/2. P is on the axis at distance z. Distances from P:

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{to } +q:\ z - \frac d2 \\ \text{to } -q:\ z + \frac d2 \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Field of +q. Coulomb's law with distance z − d/2. P is beyond +q, so this field points away from +q, along +z:

\[ E_{(+)} = \frac{kq}{(z - \frac d2)^2} \]

Step 3. Field of −q. Distance z + d/2. A negative charge's field points toward it, so along −z:

\[ E_{(-)} = \frac{kq}{(z + \frac d2)^2} \]

Step 4. Subtract. They point in opposite directions, so the net field is the difference:

\[ E = E_{(+)} - E_{(-)} = kq\left[\frac{1}{(z - \frac d2)^2} - \frac{1}{(z + \frac d2)^2}\right] \]

Step 5. Common denominator. Put both fractions over one denominator:

\[ E = kq\,\frac{(z + \frac d2)^2 - (z - \frac d2)^2}{(z - \frac d2)^2\,(z + \frac d2)^2} \]

Step 6. Expand the top. \((z \pm \tfrac d2)^2 = z^2 \pm zd + \tfrac{d^2}{4}\). Subtracting, the \(z^2\) and \(d^2/4\) terms cancel:

\[ (z^2 + zd + \tfrac{d^2}{4}) - (z^2 - zd + \tfrac{d^2}{4}) = 2zd \]

Step 7. Simplify the bottom. Difference of two squares: \((z - \tfrac d2)(z + \tfrac d2) = z^2 - \tfrac{d^2}{4}\), so the bottom is that squared:

\[ E = kq\,\frac{2zd}{(z^2 - \frac{d^2}{4})^2}\qquad(\text{exact}) \]

Step 8. Far away (z ≫ d). \(d^2/4\) is tiny next to \(z^2\), so the bottom is about \((z^2)^2 = z^4\):

\[ E \approx kq\,\frac{2zd}{z^4} = \frac{2kqd}{z^3} \]

Step 9. Use p = qd and k = 1/4πε₀. \(2k = \dfrac{2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\):

\[ E = \frac{2kp}{z^3} = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} \]
Result
\[ E = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} \]

Points along the axis in the direction of p (from − to +). 1/z³: it fades faster than a single charge because the two fields almost cancel.

Answer: \(E = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qd}{z^3}\), along the dipole axis in the direction of \(\vec p\).

Q8 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution
🧠 The idea before the maths

In a uniform field the two ends feel equal and opposite forces (+qE and −qE), so the dipole doesn't slide anywhere — no net force.

But the two forces act on different lines, a bit like two hands turning a steering wheel. That makes a twist (torque) that turns p toward E.

Torque = force × lever arm (the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line). The lever arm here is (d/2) sin θ, which is where the sin θ comes from.

Step 1. Forces on the ends. The +q end is pushed along E, the −q end against E, each with size qE:

\[ \begin{gathered} F_{(+)} = qE \\ F_{(-)} = qE\ \ (\text{opposite directions}) \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Net force.

\[ F_{net} = qE - qE = 0 \]

Step 3. Lever arm. Take the centre as the pivot. Each end is d/2 from it. The rod makes angle θ with E, so the perpendicular distance from the pivot to each force's line is the side opposite θ:

\[ \text{lever arm} = \frac d2\sin\theta \]

Step 4. Torque of each force. Torque = force × lever arm:

\[ \begin{gathered} \tau_{(+)} = qE\cdot\frac d2\sin\theta \\ \tau_{(-)} = qE\cdot\frac d2\sin\theta \end{gathered} \]

Step 5. Add them. Both turn the dipole the same way (toward E), so they add:

\[ \tau = 2\cdot qE\,\frac d2\sin\theta = qEd\sin\theta \]

Step 6. Use p = qd.

\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta \]

Step 7. Vector form. The torque points perpendicular to the plane of \(\vec p\) and \(\vec E\), by the right-hand rule turning \(\vec p\) toward \(\vec E\). That is exactly the cross product, whose size is \(|\vec a\times\vec b| = ab\sin\theta\):

\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

Step 8. Conditions. \(\sin\theta\) is biggest (1) at 90° and zero at 0° and 180°:

\[ \begin{gathered} \tau_{max} = pE\ \ (\theta = 90^\circ) \\ \tau = 0\ \ (\theta = 0^\circ,\ 180^\circ) \end{gathered} \]
Result
\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E,\qquad \tau = pE\sin\theta \]

0° is stable (a nudge comes back); 180° is unstable (a nudge flips it).

Answer: \(\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E\), \(|\tau| = pE\sin\theta\): maximum pE when p ⊥ E; zero when p is parallel or antiparallel to E.

Q9 (a) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution
🧠 The idea before the maths

The field pushes on a charge, so it does work as the charge moves. Potential difference is defined from that work: ΔV = −(work done by the field) ÷ (charge).

Along a path, the field may change and the path may bend, so we add up the work in tiny steps ds — that's the integral. The dot product E · ds keeps only the part of the force along the motion (the only part that does work).

Step 1. Force on a test charge. A positive test charge \(q_0\) in the field feels

\[ \vec F = q_0\vec E \]

Step 2. Work over a tiny step. Work = force · displacement. For a small step \(d\vec s\):

\[ dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s = q_0\,\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 3. Work over the whole path. Add up all the small steps from i to f:

\[ W = \int_i^f dW = q_0\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 4. Definition of potential difference. \(\Delta U = -W\) and \(\Delta V = \Delta U/q_0\), so

\[ V_f - V_i = \frac{\Delta U}{q_0} = -\frac{W}{q_0} \]

Step 5. Substitute W. The \(q_0\) cancels:

\[ V_f - V_i = -\frac{q_0\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s}{q_0} = -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 6. Uniform field. Moving a distance d straight along a uniform E: \(\vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,ds\) (cos 0° = 1) and E comes out of the integral:

\[ V_f - V_i = -E\int_i^f ds = -Ed \]
Result
\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

The minus sign means V decreases as you move along E. The answer doesn't depend on the path (the electric force is conservative).

Answer: \(V_f - V_i = -\displaystyle\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s\).

Q9 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution
🧠 The idea before the maths

Potential at P = the work per unit charge to bring a test charge in from far away (where V = 0) to P. We get it from the field by adding up E · ds along a path between P and infinity — that's the formula Vf − Vi = −∫E·ds (derivation 7).

The easiest path is the straight radial line, because then E and ds point the same way and E · ds is just E dr.

For several charges: potential is a scalar, so their potentials simply add.

Step 1. Start formula.

\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 2. Choose the end points. Start at P (distance R from q) and finish at infinity. At infinity the potential is zero:

\[ \begin{gathered} V_i = V \\ V_f = V(\infty) = 0 \end{gathered} \]

Step 3. Choose the path. Go straight out along a radial line. E points radially too, so the angle between them is 0 and \(\vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,dr\):

\[ E = \frac{kq}{r^2} \]

Step 4. Substitute.

\[ 0 - V = -\int_R^{\infty}\frac{kq}{r^2}\,dr = -kq\int_R^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^2} \]

Step 5. Integrate. \(\int r^{-2}\,dr = -\dfrac1r\). Evaluate from R to ∞ (1/∞ = 0):

\[ \int_R^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^2} = \left[-\frac1r\right]_R^{\infty} = 0 - \left(-\frac1R\right) = \frac1R \]

Step 6. Solve for V.

\[ -V = -\frac{kq}{R}\ \Longrightarrow\ V = \frac{kq}{R} \]

Step 7. Group of n charges. Each charge gives its own kq/r; potentials are scalars, so just add (keep each sign):

\[ V = V_1 + V_2 + \cdots + V_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r},\qquad V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_i\frac{q_i}{r_i} \]

V has the same sign as q and is zero at infinity.

Answer: \(V = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac qr\).
