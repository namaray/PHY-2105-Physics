Q1 (a) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

Step 1. With \(r \to 2r\):

\[ F' = \frac{kq^2}{(2r)^2} = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{kq^2}{r^2} = \frac{F}{4} \]
🧭 Why? Coulomb's law is an inverse-square law: the force depends on 1/r², so doubling r divides the force by 2² = 4. The charges are still alike, so the force stays repulsive — only its size changes.
Answer: The force becomes one quarter of its original value (still repulsive).

Q1 (b) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ W = q\,\Delta V = q(V_B - V_A) \]

work done to move q (slowly) from A to B

Step 1. \(V_A = V_B = V\), so

\[ W = q(V - V) = 0 \]
🧭 Why? A and B lie on the same equipotential surface. The field is perpendicular to an equipotential, so moving along it the force has no component along the motion — and the work depends only on the end points, so any path from A to B gives zero.
Answer: Zero work, because ΔV = 0 between points at the same potential (W = qΔV).

Q1 (c) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q = -3.2\times10^{-15}\) C
  • \(e = -1.6\times10^{-19}\) C (electron)
📐 Formula
\[ q = Ne\ \Longrightarrow\ N = \frac{q}{e} \]

quantization of charge

\[ N = \frac{-3.2\times10^{-15}}{-1.6\times10^{-19}} = 2.0\times10^{4} \]
Answer: \(2.0\times10^4\) (twenty thousand) excess electrons.

Q2 Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +5\ \mu\)C, \(q_2 = -10\ \mu\)C, 10 cm apart
  • \(q_0 = +1\ \mu\)C at the midpoint → 5 cm = 0.05 m from each
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F = F_1 + F_2\ \ (\text{same direction}) \]
\[ F_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(5\times10^{-6})(1\times10^{-6})}{(0.05)^2} = 18.0\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10\times10^{-6})(1\times10^{-6})}{(0.05)^2} = 36.0\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? q₀ is positive. +5 μC repels it → pushes it away from +5 μC, toward −10 μC. −10 μC attracts it → pulls it toward −10 μC. Both forces point the same way, so they add. (Between unlike charges forces never cancel.)
\[ F = 18.0 + 36.0 = 53.9\ \text{N} \]
Answer: 53.9 N, directed from the +5 μC charge toward the −10 μC charge.

Q3 Math 5 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = 5\) C at distance \(d = 2\) m to the left of P; \(q_2 = 10\) C at distance \(d\) from P, 60° above that line
  • \(q_0 = 1\) nC at P; charges taken as positive
  • Axes: P at the origin, +x to the right
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F_x = \sum F\cos\theta,\quad F_y = \sum F\sin\theta,\quad F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(5)(10^{-9})}{2^2} = 11.24\ \text{N} \\ F_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10)(10^{-9})}{2^2} = 22.48\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? All positive → both forces are repulsions, pointing away from each source. q₁ is directly left of P → F₁ points along +x. q₂ is up-left at 60° → F₂ points down-right, 60° below +x.

Step 1. (i) Components:

\[ \begin{gathered} F_x = 11.24 + 22.48\cos60^\circ = 22.48\ \text{N} \\ F_y = 0 - 22.48\sin60^\circ = -19.46\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. (ii) Resultant:

\[ \begin{gathered} F = \sqrt{22.48^2 + 19.46^2} = 29.7\ \text{N} \\ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{19.46}{22.48} = 40.9^\circ\ \text{below } +x \end{gathered} \]
Answer: (i) \(F_x = +22.5\) N, \(F_y = -19.5\) N. (ii) \(F = 29.7\) N at 40.9° below the +x axis (319.1°).

Q4 Math 5 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = -10\) C at \(x = -3\) m, \(q_2 = +5\) C at \(x = +3\) m
  • P at (0, 4) m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge — away from +, toward −

\[ \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 \]

Step 1. Distances: \(r = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\) m for both.

\[ \begin{gathered} E_1 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(10)}{25} = 3.60\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \\ E_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(5)}{25} = 1.80\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? q₁ is negative → E₁ points toward q₁: down-left, along (−0.6, −0.8). q₂ is positive → E₂ points away from q₂: up-left, along (−0.6, +0.8). Both lean left; their vertical parts partly cancel.
\[ \begin{gathered} E_x = -0.6(3.60 + 1.80)\times10^9 = -3.24\times10^{9} \\ E_y = (-0.8)(3.60) + (0.8)(1.80) = -1.44\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} E = \sqrt{3.24^2 + 1.44^2}\times10^9 = 3.54\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \\ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{1.44}{3.24} = 24.0^\circ \end{gathered} \]
Answer: (i) \(3.54\times10^9\) N/C; (ii) 24.0° below the −x axis (204° from +x), i.e. down and to the left.

Q5 Math 6 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(\vec p = (5\hat i + 2\hat j)\times10^{-30}\) C·m
  • \(\vec E = (5000\hat i + 6000\hat j)\) N/C
📐 Formula
\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

torque

\[ U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

potential energy

Step 1. (i) Both vectors lie in the xy-plane, so only the k̂ part survives:

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec\tau = (p_xE_y - p_yE_x)\,\hat k \\ = [(5)(6000) - (2)(5000)]\times10^{-30}\,\hat k = 2.0\times10^{-26}\,\hat k\ \text{N·m} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? +k̂ is out of the page: the torque turns p counter-clockwise, toward E (p is at 21.8°, E at 50.2°, so p has to rotate up by 28.4° to line up).

Step 2. (ii)

\[ U = -[(5)(5000) + (2)(6000)]\times10^{-30} = -3.7\times10^{-26}\ \text{J} \]
Answer: (i) \(\vec\tau = (2.0\times10^{-26}\ \text{N·m})\,\hat k\) — magnitude \(2.0\times10^{-26}\) N·m, along +z (out of the page). (ii) \(U = -3.7\times10^{-26}\) J.

Q6 Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q = 6\) nC, \(d = 2\) m
  • from the figure: +q at d, −q at d, −q at 2d, +q at d
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum\frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]

scalar sum — signs matter, directions don't

\[ \begin{gathered} V = kq\left(\frac1d - \frac1d - \frac1{2d} + \frac1d\right) = \frac{kq}{2d} \\ = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(6\times10^{-9})}{2(2)} = 13.5\ \text{V} \end{gathered} \]
Answer: \(V = 13.5\) V.

Q7 Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = q_2 = +2e\), \(q_3 = +3e\)
  • \(a = 5\ \mu\)m
  • P is the midpoint of the hypotenuse (foot of the perpendicular from particle 3)
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum\frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]
\[ r = \frac{a}{\sqrt2} \]

the midpoint of the hypotenuse is equidistant from all three corners

\[ r = \frac{5\times10^{-6}}{\sqrt2} = 3.54\times10^{-6}\ \text{m} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} V = \frac{k(2e + 2e + 3e)}{r} \\ = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(7)(1.6\times10^{-19})}{3.54\times10^{-6}} = 2.85\times10^{-3}\ \text{V} \end{gathered} \]
Answer: \(V = 2.85\times10^{-3}\) V.

Q8 (a) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Put the dipole on the z-axis with its centre at the origin: +q at z = +d/2, −q at z = −d/2. P is on the axis at distance z.

Step 1. Fields at P (both along the axis; + pushes away, − pulls toward):

\[ E = E_{(+)} - E_{(-)} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{(z - \frac d2)^2} - \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{(z + \frac d2)^2} \]

Step 2. Factor out \(z^2\):

\[ E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2}\left[\left(1 - \frac{d}{2z}\right)^{-2} - \left(1 + \frac{d}{2z}\right)^{-2}\right] \]

Step 3. For \(z \gg d\), use \((1 \pm x)^{-2} \approx 1 \mp 2x\):

\[ E \approx \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2}\left[\left(1 + \frac dz\right) - \left(1 - \frac dz\right)\right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2}\cdot\frac{2d}{z} \]
\[ E = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{z^3} = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} \]
Answer: \(E = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qd}{z^3}\), along the dipole axis (in the direction of \(\vec p\)); it falls off as 1/z³.

Q8 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Step 1. In a uniform field the forces on the ends are \(+q\vec E\) and \(-q\vec E\): equal and opposite, so the net force is zero.

Step 2. Each force has a lever arm \((d/2)\sin\theta\) about the centre, and both turn the dipole the same way:

\[ \tau = F\frac d2\sin\theta + F\frac d2\sin\theta = qE\,d\sin\theta = pE\sin\theta \]

Step 3. The torque is perpendicular to the plane of \(\vec p\) and \(\vec E\) and turns \(\vec p\) toward \(\vec E\) — exactly the cross product:

\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

Step 4. Conditions:

\[ \begin{gathered} \tau_{max} = pE\ \ (\theta = 90^\circ) \\ \tau = 0\ \ (\theta = 0^\circ \text{ or } 180^\circ) \end{gathered} \]
Answer: \(\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E\), \(|\tau| = pE\sin\theta\): maximum pE when p ⊥ E; zero when p is parallel or antiparallel to E.

Q9 (a) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Step 1. A positive test charge \(q_0\) moves through \(d\vec s\) in a field \(\vec E\). The field's force is \(q_0\vec E\), so the work done on it is

\[ dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s = q_0\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 2. Add up the work along the whole path from i to f:

\[ W = q_0\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 3. The potential difference is the work per unit charge with a minus sign, \(V_f - V_i = -W/q_0\):

\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s \]
Answer: \(V_f - V_i = -\displaystyle\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s\). Moving along \(\vec E\) makes the integral positive, so V decreases in the direction of the field.

Q9 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Step 1. Start from \(V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s\). Take the test charge from P (distance R) straight out to infinity, along the field line:

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,dr \\ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2} \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Set \(V_f = V(\infty) = 0\) and \(V_i = V\):

\[ 0 - V = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_R^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[-\frac1r\right]_R^\infty = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac1R \]

Step 3. So, renaming R as r:

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r} \]
Answer: \(V = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac qr\) — positive for a positive charge, negative for a negative one, and zero at infinity.
