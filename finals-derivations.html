1. Electric field on the axis of a dipole 8/13 papers

Asked in 8 of 13 papers: 203212213A213B231232251253
How it was worded:
  • Find out the electric field due to an electric dipole. (203, 213A)
  • Derive expression for electric field due to a dipole. Modify the expression when z ≫ d. (212, 213B)
  • Show that the electric field on the axis of an electric dipole is E = qd / 2πε₀z³. (251, 253)
  • Show that E = 2kp / z³ (232)
📌 Start from these
  • T2 Field of a point charge:
    \( \displaystyle E = \frac{kq}{r^2} \)
    points away from +, toward −
  • T5 Superposition:
    \( \displaystyle \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots \)
    \( \displaystyle V = V_1 + V_2 + \cdots \)
    fields add as arrows, potentials add as numbers
  • T12 Far-away approximation:
    \( \displaystyle d \ll z\ \Rightarrow\ z \pm \tfrac d2 \approx z \)
    when P is far, the small size d of the dipole hardly changes the distances
  • T13 Dipole moment:
    \( \displaystyle p = qd \)
    points from −q to +q
🧠 The idea before the maths

A dipole is a +q and a −q a small distance d apart. At a point P on the axis beyond +q, the +q is closer, so it pushes P away strongly; the −q is farther, so it pulls back a bit more weakly. The two fields point opposite ways and nearly cancel.

So the plan is: write each field with Coulomb's law, subtract them, tidy the fraction, and finally use "P is far away" (z ≫ d) to simplify.

Because the fields nearly cancel, what's left falls off as 1/z³ — faster than one charge's 1/z².

Step 1. Set up. Put the dipole on the z-axis with its centre at the origin: +q at z = +d/2, −q at z = −d/2. P is on the axis at distance z. Distances from P:

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{to } +q:\ z - \frac d2 \\ \text{to } -q:\ z + \frac d2 \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Field of +q (T2). Coulomb's law with distance z − d/2. P is beyond +q, so this field points away from +q, along +z:

\[ E_{(+)} = \frac{kq}{(z - \frac d2)^2} \]

Step 3. Field of −q (T2). Distance z + d/2. A negative charge's field points toward it, so along −z:

\[ E_{(-)} = \frac{kq}{(z + \frac d2)^2} \]

Step 4. Subtract (T5). They point in opposite directions, so the net field is the difference:

\[ E = E_{(+)} - E_{(-)} = kq\left[\frac{1}{(z - \frac d2)^2} - \frac{1}{(z + \frac d2)^2}\right] \]

Step 5. Common denominator. Put both fractions over one denominator:

\[ E = kq\,\frac{(z + \frac d2)^2 - (z - \frac d2)^2}{(z - \frac d2)^2\,(z + \frac d2)^2} \]

Step 6. Expand the top. \((z \pm \tfrac d2)^2 = z^2 \pm zd + \tfrac{d^2}{4}\). Subtracting, the \(z^2\) and \(d^2/4\) terms cancel:

\[ (z^2 + zd + \tfrac{d^2}{4}) - (z^2 - zd + \tfrac{d^2}{4}) = 2zd \]

Step 7. Simplify the bottom. Difference of two squares: \((z - \tfrac d2)(z + \tfrac d2) = z^2 - \tfrac{d^2}{4}\), so the bottom is that squared:

\[ E = kq\,\frac{2zd}{(z^2 - \frac{d^2}{4})^2}\qquad(\text{exact}) \]

Step 8. Far away, z ≫ d (T12). \(d^2/4\) is tiny next to \(z^2\), so the bottom is about \((z^2)^2 = z^4\):

\[ E \approx kq\,\frac{2zd}{z^4} = \frac{2kqd}{z^3} \]

Step 9. Use p = qd (T13) and k = 1/4πε₀. \(2k = \dfrac{2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\):

\[ E = \frac{2kp}{z^3} = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} \]
Result
\[ E = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} \]

Points along the axis in the direction of p (from − to +). 1/z³: it fades faster than a single charge because the two fields almost cancel.

✍️ In the exam Draw the diagram first. If asked to "modify when z ≫ d", write the exact line (step 7) and the approximation (step 8).

2. Electric potential of a dipole 7/13 papers

Asked in 7 of 13 papers: 211213A213B231251252261
How it was worded:
  • Find out the electric potential due to an electric dipole. Rewrite the case if the electric charge is continuous. (211)
  • Find the electrical potential for a dipole. (213B)
  • Show that, at a distance r from an electric dipole with dipole moment p = qd, V = (1/4πε₀) p cos θ / r². Draw proper diagram. (231, 251, 252, 261)
📌 Start from these
  • T4 Potential of a point charge:
    \( \displaystyle V = \frac{kq}{r} \)
    a plain number; keeps the sign of q
  • T5 Superposition:
    \( \displaystyle \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots \)
    \( \displaystyle V = V_1 + V_2 + \cdots \)
    fields add as arrows, potentials add as numbers
  • T10 Right-triangle trig:
    \( \displaystyle \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \)
  • T12 Far-away approximation:
    \( \displaystyle d \ll z\ \Rightarrow\ z \pm \tfrac d2 \approx z \)
    when P is far, the small size d of the dipole hardly changes the distances
  • T13 Dipole moment:
    \( \displaystyle p = qd \)
    points from −q to +q
🧠 The idea before the maths

Potential is a scalar (just a number with a sign), so we simply add the potential of +q and of −q — no arrows, no components.

The two distances r₊ and r₋ are almost the same, so the two potentials almost cancel. The tiny difference between the distances is what matters, and from far away that difference is d cos θ — the part of d that points toward P.

Plan: add the two potentials, put them over one denominator, then use "far away" to replace the distances.

Step 1. Set up. Dipole centre at the origin, +q above and −q below, a distance d apart. P is a distance r from the centre at angle θ from the dipole axis. Call its distances to the charges r₊ and r₋.

Step 2. Potential of each charge (T4). V = kq/r for each; the −q gives a negative potential:

\[ \begin{gathered} V_{(+)} = \frac{kq}{r_{(+)}} \\ V_{(-)} = -\frac{kq}{r_{(-)}} \end{gathered} \]

Step 3. Add them as numbers (T5).

\[ V = kq\left(\frac{1}{r_{(+)}} - \frac{1}{r_{(-)}}\right) \]

Step 4. Common denominator.

\[ V = kq\,\frac{r_{(-)} - r_{(+)}}{r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)}} \]

Step 5. The top, far away (T10, T12). When P is far (r ≫ d) the lines from the two charges to P are almost parallel. Draw a perpendicular from +q onto the line from −q: the extra distance is the side of a right triangle with hypotenuse d and angle θ, i.e. the adjacent side:

\[ r_{(-)} - r_{(+)} \approx d\cos\theta \]

Step 6. The bottom, far away (T12). Both distances are almost equal to r:

\[ r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)} \approx r\cdot r = r^2 \]

Step 7. Substitute.

\[ V = kq\,\frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2} \]

Step 8. Use p = qd (T13).

\[ V = \frac{kp\cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} \]

Checks: on the axis (θ = 0) V = kp/r²; on the perpendicular bisector (θ = 90°) V = 0 — every point there is equally far from +q and −q; behind (θ = 180°) V = −kp/r².

✍️ In the exam Mark r₊, r₋, θ and the little d cos θ segment on your diagram — that projection is the step the marker looks for.

3. Torque on a dipole: τ = p × E 5/13 papers

Asked in 5 of 13 papers: 203213A232243253
How it was worded:
  • Establish a relation between torque τ, electric dipole moment p and electric field E. (203, 213A)
  • If two charges behave like a dipole, show that the moment of force is τ = p × E. Calculate the angles for maximum and minimum torque. (232)
  • Show that the torque experienced by a dipole in the electric field is given by the cross product. Draw and discuss the condition for maximum torque. (243, 253)
📌 Start from these
  • T3 Force from a field:
    \( \displaystyle F = qE \)
    a + charge is pushed along E, a − charge against E
  • T8 Torque:
    \( \displaystyle \tau = F\times(\text{lever arm}) \)
    lever arm = perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line
  • T10 Right-triangle trig:
    \( \displaystyle \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \)
  • T13 Dipole moment:
    \( \displaystyle p = qd \)
    points from −q to +q
  • T9 Dot and cross product:
    \( \displaystyle \vec a\cdot\vec b = ab\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle |\vec a\times\vec b| = ab\sin\theta \)
    θ = angle between the two vectors
🧠 The idea before the maths

In a uniform field the two ends feel equal and opposite forces (+qE and −qE), so the dipole doesn't slide anywhere — no net force.

But the two forces act on different lines, a bit like two hands turning a steering wheel. That makes a twist (torque) that turns p toward E.

Torque = force × lever arm (the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line). The lever arm here is (d/2) sin θ, which is where the sin θ comes from.

Step 1. Forces on the ends (T3). The +q end is pushed along E, the −q end against E, each with size qE:

\[ \begin{gathered} F_{(+)} = qE \\ F_{(-)} = qE\ \ (\text{opposite directions}) \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Net force.

\[ F_{net} = qE - qE = 0 \]

Step 3. Lever arm (T10). Take the centre as the pivot. Each end is d/2 from it. The rod makes angle θ with E, so the perpendicular distance from the pivot to each force's line is the side opposite θ:

\[ \text{lever arm} = \frac d2\sin\theta \]

Step 4. Torque of each force (T8). Torque = force × lever arm:

\[ \begin{gathered} \tau_{(+)} = qE\cdot\frac d2\sin\theta \\ \tau_{(-)} = qE\cdot\frac d2\sin\theta \end{gathered} \]

Step 5. Add them. Both turn the dipole the same way (toward E), so they add:

\[ \tau = 2\cdot qE\,\frac d2\sin\theta = qEd\sin\theta \]

Step 6. Use p = qd (T13).

\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta \]

Step 7. Vector form (T9). The torque points perpendicular to the plane of \(\vec p\) and \(\vec E\), by the right-hand rule turning \(\vec p\) toward \(\vec E\). That is exactly the cross product, whose size is \(|\vec a\times\vec b| = ab\sin\theta\):

\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

Step 8. Conditions. \(\sin\theta\) is biggest (1) at 90° and zero at 0° and 180°:

\[ \begin{gathered} \tau_{max} = pE\ \ (\theta = 90^\circ) \\ \tau = 0\ \ (\theta = 0^\circ,\ 180^\circ) \end{gathered} \]
Result
\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E,\qquad \tau = pE\sin\theta \]

0° is stable (a nudge comes back); 180° is unstable (a nudge flips it).

✍️ In the exam Two diagrams earn the marks: the dipole with both forces and the lever arm, and the three orientations 0°, 90°, 180°.

4. Potential energy of a dipole: U = −p · E 5/13 papers

Asked in 5 of 13 papers: 231233251252261
How it was worded:
  • Show that a potential energy U is associated with the orientation of the dipole moment in the field, U = −p·E. (231, 233, 252, 261)
  • Calculate the condition for maximum and zero potential energy. (233, 252, 261)
  • Show that U = −p·E. Draw the diagram and discuss the conditions of maximum potential energy. (251)
📌 Start from these
  • T6 Work:
    \( \displaystyle W = Fd\cos\theta\qquad(\text{tiny step: } dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s) \)
    θ = angle between the force and the motion
  • T7 Energy and potential:
    \( \displaystyle \Delta U = -W \)
    \( \displaystyle \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} \)
    W = work done by the field
  • T11 Integrals you need:
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac1r \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}} = \ln\!\left(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\right) \)
    ∫ just means "add up lots of tiny pieces"
  • T9 Dot and cross product:
    \( \displaystyle \vec a\cdot\vec b = ab\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle |\vec a\times\vec b| = ab\sin\theta \)
    θ = angle between the two vectors
  • Torque on a dipole \( \tau = pE\sin\theta \) — derivation 3
🧠 The idea before the maths

What U means: energy the dipole stores just because of which way it points. Think of a ball on a hill: pointing along E is the bottom (lowest energy, it stays put); pointing against E is the top (highest energy, the slightest nudge flips it).

Why it changes with angle: the field twists the dipole toward E with torque pE sin θ. Turning it away means fighting that twist — you do work, and that work is stored as potential energy (like lifting a ball).

Where the cos comes from: work = torque added up over the angle (an integral), and \( \int\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta \).

Why zero at 90°: like choosing "ground level" for height, the zero is a choice; 90° is picked because cos 90° = 0.

The "= −p·E" part is the same thing in vector shorthand: \( \vec a\cdot\vec b = ab\cos\theta \), so \( \vec p\cdot\vec E = pE\cos\theta \).

Step 1. Torque from the field (derivation 3). The field twists the dipole so as to decrease θ — hence the minus sign:

\[ \tau = -pE\sin\theta \]

Step 2. Work done by the field (T6). Work = torque added up over the angle turned, from θ₀ to θ:

\[ W = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\tau\,d\theta = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}(-pE\sin\theta)\,d\theta \]

Step 3. Take the constant out.

\[ W = -pE\int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta \]

Step 4. Integrate (T11). \(\int\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta\), so between the limits:

\[ \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta + \cos\theta_0 \]
\[ W = -pE(-\cos\theta + \cos\theta_0) = pE(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0) \]

Step 5. Potential energy = minus the field's work (T7).

\[ U(\theta) - U(\theta_0) = -W = -pE(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0) \]

Step 6. Choose the zero. Take \(\theta_0 = 90^\circ\) with \(U(90^\circ) = 0\). Since \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\), both reference terms vanish:

\[ U(\theta) - 0 = -pE(\cos\theta - 0) \]
\[ U = -pE\cos\theta \]

Step 7. Dot-product form (T9). \(pE\cos\theta\) is \(\vec p\cdot\vec E\):

\[ U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Step 8. Conditions.

\[ \theta = 0^\circ:\ U = -pE\ \ (\text{minimum, stable}) \]
\[ \theta = 90^\circ:\ U = 0 \]
\[ \theta = 180^\circ:\ U = +pE\ \ (\text{maximum, unstable}) \]
Result
\[ U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Work an outside agent must do to turn the dipole: W = Uf − Ui; 0° → 180° costs 2pE.

✍️ In the exam Say why θ₀ = 90° (it makes U = 0 there) and sketch the U–θ curve for the conditions.

5. Potential of a point charge (and of a group of charges) 5/13 papers

Asked in 5 of 13 papers: 203213B231232253
How it was worded:
  • Find out the electric potential due to a group of point charges. (203)
  • Find the electrical potential for a point charge. (213B)
  • Show that the electric potential due to a single charged particle at a distance r is V = (1/4πε₀) q/r. Write down the formula for a group of point charges. (231, 253)
  • Find out the electric potential due to a single charge. Modify your derived equation for n number of charges. (232)
📌 Start from these
  • T2 Field of a point charge:
    \( \displaystyle E = \frac{kq}{r^2} \)
    points away from +, toward −
  • T9 Dot and cross product:
    \( \displaystyle \vec a\cdot\vec b = ab\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle |\vec a\times\vec b| = ab\sin\theta \)
    θ = angle between the two vectors
  • T11 Integrals you need:
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac1r \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}} = \ln\!\left(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\right) \)
    ∫ just means "add up lots of tiny pieces"
  • \( V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s \) — derivation 7
🧠 The idea before the maths

Potential at P = the work per unit charge to bring a test charge in from far away (where V = 0) to P. We get it from the field by adding up E · ds along a path between P and infinity — that's the formula Vf − Vi = −∫E·ds (derivation 7).

The easiest path is the straight radial line, because then E and ds point the same way and E · ds is just E dr.

For several charges: potential is a scalar, so their potentials simply add.

Step 1. Start formula (derivation 7).

\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 2. Choose the end points. Start at P (distance R from q) and finish at infinity. At infinity the potential is zero:

\[ \begin{gathered} V_i = V \\ V_f = V(\infty) = 0 \end{gathered} \]

Step 3. Choose the path (T2, T9). Go straight out along a radial line. E points radially too, so the angle between them is 0 and \(\vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,dr\):

\[ E = \frac{kq}{r^2} \]

Step 4. Substitute.

\[ 0 - V = -\int_R^{\infty}\frac{kq}{r^2}\,dr = -kq\int_R^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^2} \]

Step 5. Integrate (T11). \(\int r^{-2}\,dr = -\dfrac1r\). Evaluate from R to ∞ (1/∞ = 0):

\[ \int_R^{\infty}\frac{dr}{r^2} = \left[-\frac1r\right]_R^{\infty} = 0 - \left(-\frac1R\right) = \frac1R \]

Step 6. Solve for V.

\[ -V = -\frac{kq}{R}\ \Longrightarrow\ V = \frac{kq}{R} \]

Step 7. Group of n charges (T5). Each charge gives its own kq/r; potentials are scalars, so just add (keep each sign):

\[ V = V_1 + V_2 + \cdots + V_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r},\qquad V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_i\frac{q_i}{r_i} \]

V has the same sign as q and is zero at infinity.

✍️ In the exam If asked to "modify for n charges", the one-line superposition step is the whole answer.

  • T5 Superposition:

  • T10 Right-triangle trig:
    \( \displaystyle \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \)
  • T11 Integrals you need:
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac1r \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}} = \ln\!\left(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\right) \)
    ∫ just means "add up lots of tiny pieces"
🧠 The idea before the maths

Why not just kq/r²? That's for charge at one point; the ring's charge is spread round a circle.

Plan — chop, find, add: chop the ring into tiny pieces dq (each is a point charge). Every piece is the same distance from P: \( r = \sqrt{z^2 + R^2} \) by Pythagoras. Each piece's field is a slanted arrow; split it into an axial part (dE cos θ) and a sideways part (dE sin θ).

Symmetry: the piece directly opposite gives a mirror-image arrow — same axial part, opposite sideways part — so all the sideways parts cancel. Only the axial parts survive, and they all point the same way, so they simply add.

Where (z² + R²)3/2 comes from: 1/r² from Coulomb × cos θ = z/r from the triangle = z/r³.

Step 1. One piece. A tiny piece of length ds carries \(dq = \lambda\,ds\), where \(\lambda = q/2\pi R\) is the charge per length:

\[ dq = \lambda\,ds \]

Step 2. Its distance from P (T10). Go z along the axis and R out to the ring:

\[ r = \sqrt{z^2 + R^2} \]

Step 3. Its field (T2). Coulomb's law for a point charge:

\[ dE = \frac{k\,dq}{r^2} = \frac{k\lambda\,ds}{z^2 + R^2} \]

Step 4. Keep only the axial part (T10). Sideways parts cancel in pairs (symmetry), so we need dE cos θ. From the triangle, adjacent side z, hypotenuse r:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac zr = \frac{z}{(z^2 + R^2)^{1/2}} \]

Step 5. Multiply. Axial part of one piece = dE × cos θ — this is where z comes in:

\[ dE\cos\theta = \frac{k\lambda\,ds}{z^2 + R^2}\times\frac{z}{(z^2 + R^2)^{1/2}} \]

Step 6. Combine the powers. \((z^2+R^2)^1\times(z^2+R^2)^{1/2} = (z^2+R^2)^{3/2}\):

\[ dE\cos\theta = \frac{k\,z\,\lambda\,ds}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]

Step 7. Add round the ring (T11). Only ds changes from piece to piece; k, z, R and λ are the same for all, so they come outside the sum (∮ = "all the way round"):

\[ E = \oint dE\cos\theta = \frac{k\,z\,\lambda}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}}\oint ds \]

Step 8. Length of the ring. All the ds add up to the circumference, \(\oint ds = 2\pi R\), and \(\lambda\cdot2\pi R = q\):

\[ E = \frac{k\,z\,(\lambda\,2\pi R)}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{kqz}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]

Step 9. Checks.

\[ z = 0:\ E = 0\quad(\text{every piece cancelled by its opposite}) \]
\[ \begin{gathered} z \gg R:\ (z^2+R^2)^{3/2} \approx z^3 \\ \Rightarrow\ E \approx \frac{kq}{z^2}\quad(\text{looks like a point charge}) \end{gathered} \]
Result
\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qz}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]

Along the axis, away from the ring for q > 0.

✍️ In the exam Write the symmetry sentence ("sideways parts cancel") explicitly — it carries marks. The papers' wording "infinite line charge or ring charge" still means the ring formula.

7. Potential difference from the field: V_f − V_i = −∫ E · ds 3/13 papers

Asked in 3 of 13 papers: 233251253
How it was worded:
  • Show that the electric potential difference between two points i and f is V_f − V_i = −∫ E·ds = −Ed. (233)
  • Draw proper diagram and show that V_f − V_i = −∫ E·ds. (251, 253)
📌 Start from these
  • T3 Force from a field:
    \( \displaystyle F = qE \)
    a + charge is pushed along E, a − charge against E
  • T6 Work:
    \( \displaystyle W = Fd\cos\theta\qquad(\text{tiny step: } dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s) \)
    θ = angle between the force and the motion
  • T7 Energy and potential:
    \( \displaystyle \Delta U = -W \)
    \( \displaystyle \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} \)
    W = work done by the field
  • T9 Dot and cross product:
    \( \displaystyle \vec a\cdot\vec b = ab\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle |\vec a\times\vec b| = ab\sin\theta \)
    θ = angle between the two vectors
🧠 The idea before the maths

This is just work → energy → potential, using T3, T6 and T7.

Part A does the simple case: a uniform field and a straight path. There it's one line of work, W = Fd, and you get ΔV = −Ed.

Part B is the same idea when the field changes or the path bends: chop the path into tiny straight steps, do Part A on each step, and add them up. "Add up tiny pieces" is all the ∫ sign means.

Step 1. Part A — force on the test charge (T3). Put a small positive test charge \(q_0\) in a uniform field E. It is pushed along E:

\[ F = q_0E \]

Step 2. Part A — work done by the field (T6). Let it move a distance d straight along E. Force and motion point the same way (θ = 0°, cos 0° = 1):

\[ W = Fd\cos0^\circ = q_0Ed \]

Step 3. Part A — change in potential energy (T7).

\[ \Delta U = -W = -q_0Ed \]

Step 4. Part A — change in potential (T7). Divide by the charge; \(q_0\) cancels:

\[ \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q_0} = \frac{-q_0Ed}{q_0} = -Ed \]

Step 5. Part B — chop the path into tiny steps. If E changes or the path bends, take a tiny step \(d\vec s\). Over such a short step E is constant and the step is straight, so Part A works on it. The work is force × step × cos θ, which is a dot product (T9):

\[ dW = q_0E\,ds\cos\theta = q_0\,\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 6. Part B — add up all the steps. Adding every tiny piece from i to f is what \(\int_i^f\) means:

\[ W = q_0\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 7. Part B — potential difference (T7). Exactly as in Part A, divide by \(-q_0\):

\[ V_f - V_i = -\frac{W}{q_0} = -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

Step 8. Check. For a uniform E along a straight path, the integral is just E × d, giving back Part A:

\[ -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s = -E\,d \]
Result
\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s\qquad(\text{uniform field: } \Delta V = -Ed) \]

The minus sign: V drops as you move along E (the field does positive work, so energy falls). The answer doesn't depend on the path.

✍️ In the exam Write Part A first — it shows you understand the idea — then generalise. Draw the field lines, the path and one small ds with F = q₀E on it.

8. Potential of a line of charge (rod of length L) 3/13 papers

Asked in 3 of 13 papers: 212213B261
How it was worded:
  • Find the electrical potential due to a line of charges. (212)
  • Find out electric potential at a point P, distance r from a charged wire of length L. (213B)
  • Show that the electric potential V due to a continuous charge distribution of length L is … (261)
📌 Start from these
  • T4 Potential of a point charge:
    \( \displaystyle V = \frac{kq}{r} \)
    a plain number; keeps the sign of q
  • T5 Superposition:
    \( \displaystyle \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots \)
    \( \displaystyle V = V_1 + V_2 + \cdots \)
    fields add as arrows, potentials add as numbers
  • T10 Right-triangle trig:
    \( \displaystyle \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle \sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \)
    \( \displaystyle c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \)
  • T11 Integrals you need:
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac1r \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}} = \ln\!\left(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\right) \)
    ∫ just means "add up lots of tiny pieces"
🧠 The idea before the maths

Same chop-and-add idea as the ring, but simpler because potential is a scalar — no components, no cancelling arrows. Each little piece of the rod is a point charge with potential k dq / r.

The difference from the ring: here every piece is a different distance from P, so r stays inside the integral and we need a standard integral to finish.

Step 1. Set up. Rod along the x-axis from x = 0 to x = L, uniform charge per length λ. P is a distance d from the left end, straight above it.

Step 2. One piece. The piece of length dx at position x carries

\[ dq = \lambda\,dx \]

Step 3. Its distance from P (T10). Right triangle with sides x and d:

\[ r = \sqrt{x^2 + d^2} \]

Step 4. Its potential (T4). Point-charge potential:

\[ dV = \frac{k\,dq}{r} = \frac{k\lambda\,dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + d^2}} \]

Step 5. Add all the pieces (T5). Potentials are scalars, so just integrate along the rod; kλ is constant:

\[ V = k\lambda\int_0^L\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + d^2}} \]

Step 6. Standard integral (T11).

\[ \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + d^2}} = \ln\left(x + \sqrt{x^2 + d^2}\right) \]

Step 7. Put in the limits. Top (x = L) minus bottom (x = 0); at x = 0 the log is \(\ln(0 + \sqrt{d^2}) = \ln d\):

\[ V = k\lambda\left[\ln\left(L + \sqrt{L^2 + d^2}\right) - \ln d\right] \]

Step 8. Combine the logs. \(\ln a - \ln b = \ln(a/b)\):

\[ V = k\lambda\,\ln\left[\frac{L + \sqrt{L^2 + d^2}}{d}\right] \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\left[\frac{L + (L^2 + d^2)^{1/2}}{d}\right] \]

You may quote the standard integral; you aren't expected to derive it.

✍️ In the exam Label x, dx, d and r on the diagram — the triangle gives r = √(x² + d²).

9. Electric field of a point charge (from Coulomb's law) 1/13 papers

Asked in 1 of 13 papers: 232
How it was worded:
  • Derive the equation of electric field at a certain distance from a point charge. Write the vector form of your derived equation. (232)
📌 Start from these
  • T1 Coulomb's law:
    \( \displaystyle F = \frac{k\,q_1q_2}{r^2} \)
    \( \displaystyle k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \)
    force between two point charges; like repel, unlike attract
  • Definition of the field: \( \vec E = \vec F / q_0 \) (force per unit positive test charge)
🧠 The idea before the maths

The field is defined as force per unit positive test charge. So put a test charge near q, find the Coulomb force on it, and divide the test charge out.

Step 1. Coulomb force on a test charge (T1). A small positive test charge \(q_0\) a distance r from q, with \(\hat r\) the unit vector pointing from q to the test charge:

\[ \vec F = \frac{k\,q\,q_0}{r^2}\,\hat r \]

Step 2. Definition of the field.

\[ \vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q_0} \]

Step 3. Divide. The \(q_0\) cancels — the field doesn't depend on the test charge:

\[ \vec E = \frac{kq}{r^2}\,\hat r \]
Result
\[ \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat r,\qquad E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

Away from a positive q, toward a negative q. Several charges: add the fields as vectors.

✍️ In the exam Coulomb's law → divide by q₀ → vector form with r̂ → direction rule.

10. Potential energy of a system of point charges From the slides

📌 Start from these
  • T4 Potential of a point charge:
    \( \displaystyle V = \frac{kq}{r} \)
    a plain number; keeps the sign of q
  • T6 Work:
    \( \displaystyle W = Fd\cos\theta\qquad(\text{tiny step: } dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s) \)
    θ = angle between the force and the motion
  • T7 Energy and potential:
    \( \displaystyle \Delta U = -W \)
    \( \displaystyle \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} \)
    W = work done by the field
  • Work to bring a charge q to a point at potential V: \( W = qV \)
🧠 The idea before the maths

The energy stored in a group of charges = the work needed to bring them together, one at a time, from far away. Each new charge has to be pushed against the charges already there — that gives one term for every pair.

Step 1. First charge. Bring \(q_1\) in from infinity. Nothing else is there yet, so no force and no work:

\[ W_1 = 0 \]

Step 2. Second charge (W = qV, T4). Bring \(q_2\) to a distance \(r_{12}\) from \(q_1\). The work is \(q_2\) times the potential of \(q_1\) at that spot:

\[ W_2 = q_2V_1 = q_2\,\frac{kq_1}{r_{12}} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r_{12}} \]

Step 3. Third charge. \(q_3\) is pushed against both \(q_1\) and \(q_2\):

\[ W_3 = q_3(V_1 + V_2) = \frac{kq_1q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{kq_2q_3}{r_{23}} \]

Step 4. Total = stored energy.

\[ U = W_1 + W_2 + W_3 = k\left(\frac{q_1q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2q_3}{r_{23}}\right) \]
Result
\[ U = \sum_{\text{pairs}}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}} \]

Like charges → positive terms (work needed to push them together); unlike → negative (the system is bound).

✍️ In the exam 3 charges → 3 pairs, 4 charges → 6 pairs. Keep the signs of the charges.

11. Field from potential (potential gradient) From the slides

📌 Start from these
  • T6 Work:
    \( \displaystyle W = Fd\cos\theta\qquad(\text{tiny step: } dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s) \)
    θ = angle between the force and the motion
  • T7 Energy and potential:
    \( \displaystyle \Delta U = -W \)
    \( \displaystyle \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} \)
    W = work done by the field
🧠 The idea before the maths

This runs derivation 7 backwards: instead of getting V from E, get E from V. Over a tiny step, the work done by the field can be written two ways — using the force, and using the potential change. Setting them equal gives E.

Step 1. Work using the force (T6). A test charge \(q_0\) moves a tiny distance \(ds\) at angle θ to \(\vec E\):

\[ dW = q_0E\cos\theta\,ds \]

Step 2. Work using the potential (T7). \(dW = -q_0\,dV\):

\[ dW = -q_0\,dV \]

Step 3. Set them equal.

\[ q_0E\cos\theta\,ds = -q_0\,dV \]

Step 4. Solve. Cancel \(q_0\), divide by ds; \(E\cos\theta\) is the component of E along s:

\[ E_s = E\cos\theta = -\frac{dV}{ds} \]
Result
\[ E_s = -\frac{dV}{ds} \]

E points the way V falls fastest. (The quizzes excluded gradient applications; the result itself is in the slides.)

✍️ In the exam State clearly that E cos θ is the component of E along the displacement.

12. Potential of any continuous charge distribution From the slides

📌 Start from these
  • T4 Potential of a point charge:
    \( \displaystyle V = \frac{kq}{r} \)
    a plain number; keeps the sign of q
  • T5 Superposition:
    \( \displaystyle \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots \)
    \( \displaystyle V = V_1 + V_2 + \cdots \)
    fields add as arrows, potentials add as numbers
  • T11 Integrals you need:
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac1r \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\sin\theta\,d\theta = -\cos\theta \)
    \( \displaystyle \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}} = \ln\!\left(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\right) \)
    ∫ just means "add up lots of tiny pieces"
🧠 The idea before the maths

Chop the charge into tiny pieces, treat each as a point charge, and add their potentials. Because potential is a scalar, "add" really is just adding numbers.

Step 1. One piece (T4). Each tiny piece dq is a point charge:

\[ dV = \frac{k\,dq}{r} \]

Step 2. Add them all (T5, T11).

\[ V = \int dV = k\int\frac{dq}{r} \]

Step 3. What dq is. Line: \(dq = \lambda\,ds\). Surface: \(dq = \sigma\,dA\). Volume: \(dq = \rho\,dV\).

Step 4. Example: ring on its axis. Every piece is at \(r = \sqrt{z^2+R^2}\), so r comes outside and the dq's add to q:

\[ V = \frac{k}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\int dq = \frac{kq}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}} \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{r} \]

The rod of length L is worked in full as derivation 8.
