1. Electric field on the axis of a dipole 8/13 papers

Asked in 8 of 13 papers: 203212213A213B231232251253
How it was worded:
  • Find out the electric field due to an electric dipole. (203, 213A)
  • Derive expression for electric field due to a dipole. Modify the expression when z ≫ d. (212, 213B)
  • Show that the electric field on the axis of an electric dipole is E = qd / 2πε₀z³. (251, 253)
  • Show that E = 2kp / z³ (232)
Set up

Put the dipole on the z-axis with its centre at the origin: +q at z = +d/2 and −q at z = −d/2. P is on the axis a distance z from the centre.

Field of each charge at P

+q is closer (distance z − d/2) and pushes away from itself, along +z. −q is farther (z + d/2) and pulls toward itself, along −z:

\[ \begin{gathered} E_{(+)} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{(z - \frac d2)^2} \\ E_{(-)} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{(z + \frac d2)^2} \end{gathered} \]
Superpose (they point opposite ways)
\[ E = E_{(+)} - E_{(-)} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(z - \frac d2)^2} - \frac{1}{(z + \frac d2)^2}\right] \]
Common denominator
\[ E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{(z + \frac d2)^2 - (z - \frac d2)^2}{(z^2 - \frac{d^2}{4})^2} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2zd}{(z^2 - \frac{d^2}{4})^2} \]
Far away (z ≫ d)

\(d^2/4\) is negligible next to \(z^2\), so \((z^2 - d^2/4)^2 \approx z^4\):

\[ E \approx \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2zd}{z^4} = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qd}{z^3} \]
Result
\[ E = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} = \frac{2kp}{z^3},\qquad p = qd \]

Direction: along the axis, the same way as \(\vec p\) (from − to +). It falls off as 1/z³ — faster than a point charge's 1/z² because the two fields nearly cancel.

✍️ In the exam Draw the diagram first (it carries marks). If the question says "modify when z ≫ d", show the exact line and the approximation. An alternative last step: factor out z² and use (1 ± x)⁻² ≈ 1 ∓ 2x — both are accepted.

2. Electric potential of a dipole 7/13 papers

Asked in 7 of 13 papers: 211213A213B231251252261
How it was worded:
  • Find out the electric potential due to an electric dipole. Rewrite the case if the electric charge is continuous. (211)
  • Find the electrical potential for a dipole. (213B)
  • Show that, at a distance r from an electric dipole with dipole moment p = qd, V = (1/4πε₀) p cos θ / r². Draw proper diagram. (231, 251, 252, 261)
Superpose the two potentials

Potential is a scalar, so just add: +q at distance \(r_{(+)}\), −q at \(r_{(-)}\).

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q}{r_{(+)}} - \frac{q}{r_{(-)}}\right) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{r_{(-)} - r_{(+)}}{r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)}} \]
Far away (r ≫ d)

The two lines to P are almost parallel. The extra distance to −q is the projection of d on that line, and the product is about r²:

\[ \begin{gathered} r_{(-)} - r_{(+)} \approx d\cos\theta \\ r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)} \approx r^2 \end{gathered} \]
Substitute and use p = qd
\[ V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2} \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} \]

θ is measured from the dipole axis. On the axis (θ = 0): V = kp/r². On the perpendicular bisector (θ = 90°): V = 0. Behind (θ = 180°): V = −kp/r².

If the charge is continuous (211)

Split the charge into elements \(dq\), each a point charge, and add up the scalars:

\[ dV = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r}\ \Longrightarrow\ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{r} \]
✍️ In the exam State the approximation r ≫ d out loud and mark d cos θ on your diagram — that projection is the key step the marker looks for.

3. Torque on a dipole: τ = p × E 5/13 papers

Asked in 5 of 13 papers: 203213A232243253
How it was worded:
  • Establish a relation between torque τ, electric dipole moment p and electric field E. (203, 213A)
  • If two charges behave like a dipole, show that the moment of force is τ = p × E. Calculate the angles for maximum and minimum torque. (232)
  • Show that the torque experienced by a dipole in the electric field is given by the cross product. Draw and discuss the condition for maximum torque. (243, 253)
Forces

In a uniform field \(\vec E\), the +q end feels \(+q\vec E\) and the −q end feels \(-q\vec E\). Equal and opposite → no net force.

Lever arms

Take moments about the centre. Each force acts at distance d/2, and its perpendicular lever arm is \((d/2)\sin\theta\), θ being the angle between \(\vec p\) and \(\vec E\). Both turn the dipole the same way, so they add:

\[ \tau = F\frac d2\sin\theta + F\frac d2\sin\theta = (qE)\,d\sin\theta \]
Use p = qd
\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta \]
Vector form

The torque is perpendicular to the plane of \(\vec p\) and \(\vec E\) and turns \(\vec p\) toward \(\vec E\) — exactly the right-hand rule for a cross product:

Result
\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E,\qquad |\tau| = pE\sin\theta \]

Maximum τ = pE at θ = 90° (p ⊥ E). Zero at θ = 0° (stable) and θ = 180° (unstable).

✍️ In the exam Two diagrams win the marks: the dipole with both forces and the lever arm, and the three orientations (0°, 90°, 180°).

4. Potential energy of a dipole: U = −p · E 5/13 papers

Asked in 5 of 13 papers: 231233251252261
How it was worded:
  • Show that a potential energy U is associated with the orientation of the dipole moment in the field, U = −p·E. (231, 233, 252, 261)
  • Calculate the condition for maximum and zero potential energy. (233, 252, 261)
  • Show that U = −p·E. Draw the diagram and discuss the conditions of maximum potential energy. (251)
Torque tries to reduce θ
\[ \tau = -pE\sin\theta \]

(The minus sign: the torque acts to decrease the angle between p and E.)

Work done by the field as θ₀ → θ
\[ W = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\tau\,d\theta = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}(-pE\sin\theta)\,d\theta = pE\left(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0\right) \]
Potential energy

\(\Delta U = -W\). Choose the zero of energy at \(\theta_0 = 90^\circ\) (\(\cos 90^\circ = 0\)):

\[ U(\theta) - U(90^\circ) = -pE\cos\theta + pE\cos90^\circ\ \Longrightarrow\ U = -pE\cos\theta \]
Result
\[ U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Maximum +pE at θ = 180° (antiparallel, unstable). Zero at θ = 90°. Minimum −pE at θ = 0° (aligned, stable). Work by an external agent to turn it: W = Uf − Ui.

✍️ In the exam Say why θ₀ = 90° is chosen (it makes U = 0 there). Sketch the U–θ curve; it answers the 'conditions' part at a glance.

5. Potential of a point charge (and of a group of charges) 5/13 papers

Asked in 5 of 13 papers: 203213B231232253
How it was worded:
  • Find out the electric potential due to a group of point charges. (203)
  • Find the electrical potential for a point charge. (213B)
  • Show that the electric potential due to a single charged particle at a distance r is V = (1/4πε₀) q/r. Write down the formula for a group of point charges. (231, 253)
  • Find out the electric potential due to a single charge. Modify your derived equation for n number of charges. (232)
Start from the field
\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s \]
Choose the path

Move a test charge from P (distance R) straight out to infinity along a radial line, so \(\vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,dr\) with \(E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q}{r^2}\).

Integrate, with V(∞) = 0
\[ 0 - V = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_R^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2} = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[-\frac1r\right]_R^\infty = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R} \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r} \]

V has the same sign as q, and is zero at infinity.

Group of n point charges

Potential is a scalar, so the potentials of the individual charges simply add (superposition) — no components:

\[ V = \sum_{i=1}^{n} V_i = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{q_i}{r_i} \]
✍️ In the exam Keep the sign of each q in the group sum. If asked to 'modify for n charges', the one-line superposition step is the whole answer.

6. Electric field of a charged ring (on its axis) 4/13 papers

Asked in 4 of 13 papers: 213B243252261
How it was worded:
  • Derive expression for electric field for a charged ring. Modify the expression when z ≫ R and z = 0. (213B)
  • Show that the electric field due to a charged ring at large distance is … (243)
  • Show that the electric field at a point due to a uniformly distributed infinite line charge or ring charge is E = qz / 4πε₀(z² + R²)^{3/2}. (252, 261)
🧠 The idea before the maths

Why not just kq/r²? That formula is for charge sitting at one point. The ring's charge is spread around a circle, so there is no single distance to use.

The plan — chop, find, add:

  1. Chop the ring into tiny pieces dq. Each piece is small enough to be a point charge.
  2. Every piece is the same distance from P: go z along the axis and R out to the ring, so by Pythagoras \( r = \sqrt{z^2 + R^2} \).
  3. One piece gives \( dE = k\,dq/r^2 \), pointing away from that piece — a slanted arrow.
  4. Split each slanted arrow into an axial part \( dE\cos\theta \) (along the axis) and a sideways part \( dE\sin\theta \).
  5. Symmetry: the piece directly opposite makes a mirror-image arrow. Same axial part, opposite sideways part. So every sideways part is cancelled by its partner — only the axial parts survive, and they all point the same way, so they simply add.
  6. Add the axial parts of all the pieces. Because z and R are the same for every piece, they come out of the sum, and the dq's add up to the total charge q.

Where (z² + R²)3/2 comes from: one factor 1/r² from Coulomb's law, times cos θ = z/r from the triangle, gives z/r³ — and r³ = (z² + R²)3/2.

Element of the ring

Split the ring (radius R, charge q, \(\lambda = q/2\pi R\)) into elements \(ds\) with \(dq = \lambda\,ds\). Each is a point charge a distance \(r = \sqrt{z^2 + R^2}\) from P:

\[ dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,ds}{z^2 + R^2} \]
Symmetry

The element directly opposite gives an equal \(dE\) whose sideways part points the other way. All sideways (\(dE\sin\theta\)) parts cancel; only the axial parts \(dE\cos\theta\) add, with

\[ \cos\theta = \frac zr = \frac{z}{(z^2 + R^2)^{1/2}} \]
Add round the ring

(a) Axial part of one piece = (its field from Step 1) × (cos θ from Step 2). This is where z comes in — it is the adjacent side of the triangle in cos θ = z/r:

\[ dE\cos\theta = \underbrace{\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,ds}{z^2 + R^2}}_{dE\ \text{(Step 1)}}\times\underbrace{\frac{z}{(z^2 + R^2)^{1/2}}}_{\cos\theta\ \text{(Step 2)}} \]

(b) Combine the powers of \((z^2+R^2)\): \(\ (z^2+R^2)^{1}\times(z^2+R^2)^{1/2} = (z^2+R^2)^{3/2}\):

\[ dE\cos\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,\lambda\,ds}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]

(c) Add this up for every piece round the ring (∮ means “sum all the way round”). Only \(ds\) changes from piece to piece — z, R and λ are the same for all of them — so everything except ds comes outside the sum:

\[ E = \oint dE\cos\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,\lambda}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}}\oint ds \]

(d) All the little lengths ds added together make the whole circumference: \(\oint ds = 2\pi R\).

\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,\lambda\,(2\pi R)}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]

and \(\lambda(2\pi R) = q\) (charge per length × length of the ring = total charge).

Result
\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qz}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]
Limits
\[ \begin{gathered} z = 0:\ \ E = 0 \\ (\text{every element is cancelled by the opposite one}) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} z \gg R:\ \ (z^2 + R^2)^{3/2} \approx z^3\ \\ \Rightarrow\ E \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{z^2} \\ (\text{looks like a point charge}) \end{gathered} \]
✍️ In the exam The symmetry sentence (sideways parts cancel) is worth marks on its own — write it explicitly. Note the papers' wording "infinite line charge or ring charge": the formula is the ring one.

7. Potential difference from the field: V_f − V_i = −∫ E · ds 3/13 papers

Asked in 3 of 13 papers: 233251253
How it was worded:
  • Show that the electric potential difference between two points i and f is V_f − V_i = −∫ E·ds = −Ed. (233)
  • Draw proper diagram and show that V_f − V_i = −∫ E·ds. (251, 253)
Work on a test charge

A positive test charge \(q_0\) moves through \(d\vec s\) in a field \(\vec E\). The field's force is \(q_0\vec E\), so

\[ dW = \vec F\cdot d\vec s = q_0\,\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]
Whole path
\[ W = q_0\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]
Potential difference = −work per unit charge
\[ V_f - V_i = -\frac{W}{q_0} \]
Result
\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f\vec E\cdot d\vec s \]
Uniform field (233 asks for this too)

Move a distance d along a uniform \(\vec E\): \(\vec E\cdot d\vec s = E\,ds\), and E comes out of the integral:

\[ V_f - V_i = -E\int_i^f ds = -Ed \]
✍️ In the exam Mention that the result is path-independent (the electrostatic force is conservative), and that V decreases along the direction of E.

8. Potential of a line of charge (rod of length L) 3/13 papers

Asked in 3 of 13 papers: 212213B261
How it was worded:
  • Find the electrical potential due to a line of charges. (212)
  • Find out electric potential at a point P, distance r from a charged wire of length L. (213B)
  • Show that the electric potential V due to a continuous charge distribution of length L is … (261)
Set up

A thin rod of length L along the x-axis from 0 to L carries uniform charge density λ. P is a distance d from the left end, on the perpendicular through that end.

Element

The element \(dx\) at x carries \(dq = \lambda\,dx\) and is \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + d^2}\) from P:

\[ dV = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + d^2}} \]
Integrate along the rod

Using \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}} = \ln\left(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\right)\):

\[ \begin{gathered} V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Big[\ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2+d^2}\big)\Big]_0^L \\ = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Big[\ln\big(L + \sqrt{L^2+d^2}\big) - \ln d\Big] \end{gathered} \]
Result
\[ V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\left[\frac{L + (L^2+d^2)^{1/2}}{d}\right] \]
✍️ In the exam No components are needed — potential is a scalar. Quote the standard integral; you aren't expected to derive it.

9. Electric field of a point charge (from Coulomb's law) 1/13 papers

Asked in 1 of 13 papers: 232
How it was worded:
  • Derive the equation of electric field at a certain distance from a point charge. Write the vector form of your derived equation. (232)
Coulomb's law on a test charge

Place a small positive test charge \(q_0\) a distance r from q:

\[ \vec F = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q\,q_0}{r^2}\,\hat r \]
Definition of the field
\[ \vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q_0} \]
Result
\[ \vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat r,\qquad E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

\(\hat r\) points from q to the field point: away from a positive q, toward a negative q. For several charges, \(\vec E = \sum\vec E_i\) (vector sum).

✍️ In the exam Short but easy marks: Coulomb's law → divide by q₀ → vector form with r̂ → direction rule.

10. Potential energy of a system of point charges From the slides

Assemble the charges from infinity

Bring \(q_1\) in first — no field yet, no work. Then bring \(q_2\) from infinity to a distance r from \(q_1\). The external agent's work is \(q_2\) times the potential of \(q_1\) there:

\[ W = q_2V_1 = q_2\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r} \]
That work is stored as U
\[ U = W = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{r} \]
More charges

Each new charge does work against every charge already there, so add one term per pair:

\[ U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q_1q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2q_3}{r_{23}}\right) \]
Result
\[ U = \sum_{\text{pairs}}\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}} \]

Like charges → U > 0 (work was needed to push them together); unlike → U < 0 (the system is bound).

11. Field from potential (potential gradient) From the slides

Move between two close equipotentials

A test charge \(q_0\) moves \(ds\) at angle θ to \(\vec E\). The field's work, written two ways:

\[ \begin{gathered} dW = q_0E\cos\theta\,ds\qquad\text{and} \\ dW = -q_0\,dV \end{gathered} \]
Equate
\[ q_0E\cos\theta\,ds = -q_0\,dV\ \Longrightarrow\ E\cos\theta = -\frac{dV}{ds} \]
Result
\[ E_s = -\frac{dV}{ds} \]

The component of E in any direction is minus the rate of change of V in that direction; E points the way V falls fastest. (The quizzes excluded gradient applications; the result itself is in the slides.)

12. Potential of any continuous charge distribution From the slides

Treat each element as a point charge
\[ dV = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r} \]
Add (integrate) the scalars
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{r} \]

with \(dq = \lambda\,ds\) (line), \(\sigma\,dA\) (surface) or \(\rho\,dV\) (volume). Example: a ring of charge q, every element at \(r = \sqrt{z^2+R^2}\), gives \(V = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\); the rod of length L is worked in derivation 8.

Result
\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{r} \]
