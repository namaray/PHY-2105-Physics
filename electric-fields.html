1. The Electric Field (22-2)

Temperature at every point in a room has a definite value — that distribution is a scalar field. Air pressure is another. The electric field works the same way, but it is a vector field: it consists of a distribution of vectors, one for each point in the region around a charged object.

Definition via a test charge

To define the field at a point \( P \):

  1. Place a small positive test charge \( q_0 \) at \( P \)
  2. Measure the electrostatic force \( \vec F \) acting on it
  3. Divide out the test charge
\[ \vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q_0} \qquad\text{(electric field)} \]

The magnitude is \( E = F/q_0 \), and the direction of \( \vec E \) is the direction of the force on a positive test charge. The SI unit is the newton per coulomb (N/C).

2. Electric Field Lines (22-3)

Michael Faraday introduced the idea in the 19th century, imagining the space around a charged body as filled with lines of force.

★ The rule to quote

Electric field lines extend away from positive charge (where they originate) and toward negative charge (where they terminate).

The relation between field lines and field vectors:

  1. At any point, the direction of a straight field line — or the tangent to a curved one — gives the direction of \( \vec E \) at that point.
  2. The lines are drawn so the number of lines per unit area (measured perpendicular to the lines) is proportional to the magnitude of \( \vec E \).

So \( E \) is large where field lines are close together and small where they are far apart.

3. Field Due to a Point Charge (22-4)

Put a positive test charge \( q_0 \) a distance \( r \) from a point charge \( q \). From Coulomb's law the force is \( F = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qq_0}{r^2} \). Dividing by \( q_0 \):

\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r^2} = \frac{kq}{r^2} \qquad\text{(point charge)} \]

The direction is directly away from the point charge if \( q \) is positive, and toward it if \( q \) is negative.

Superposition of fields

For \( n \) point charges, the net force on a test charge is \( \vec F_0 = \vec F_{01} + \vec F_{02} + \cdots \). Dividing through by \( q_0 \) gives the same rule for fields:

\[ \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots + \vec E_n \]

Here \( \vec E_i \) is the field that point charge \( i \) would set up acting alone. The principle of superposition applies to electric fields exactly as it does to electrostatic forces.

4. Worked Problems from the Slides

Problem-1 — Net field at an empty point

Three particles with charges \( q_1 = +2Q \), \( q_2 = -2Q \), \( q_3 = -4Q \), each a distance \( d \) from the origin. What net field \( \vec E \) is produced at the origin?

Key idea: each charge produces its own field vector at the origin; the net field is the vector sum \( \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \vec E_3 \). Find magnitudes and directions first.

\[ E_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2Q}{d^2} \]

and similarly for \( E_2 \) and \( E_3 \). Then resolve each into components using the given angles (30° in the figure) and add.

Direction trap

The field from a positive charge points away from it; from a negative charge it points toward it. Get these directions right on the diagram before you start adding components.

Problem-2 — Charged arc (integration)

A plastic rod with uniformly distributed charge \( -Q \) is bent into a 120° circular arc of radius \( r \), with the axis of symmetry along \( x \) and the origin at the centre of curvature \( P \). What is \( \vec E \) at \( P \)?

The rod is not a particle — but each small element \( ds \) of it can be treated as one. With linear charge density \( \lambda \):

\[ dq = \lambda\,ds \]
\[ dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,ds}{r^2} \]

Then integrate over the arc. By symmetry the components perpendicular to the axis cancel, leaving only the component along the axis of symmetry.

Problem-3 — The water molecule dipole ★

A neutral water molecule (H₂O) in its vapour state has a dipole moment of magnitude \( 6.2\times10^{-30} \) C·m.

(a) How far apart are the centres of positive and negative charge?

There are 10 electrons and 10 protons in a neutral water molecule, so the dipole moment is \( p = qd = (10e)(d) \):

\[ d = \frac{p}{10e} = \frac{6.2\times10^{-30}}{(10)(1.60\times10^{-19})} = 3.9\times10^{-12}\ \text{m} = 3.9\ \text{pm} \]

Not only is this small — it is smaller than the radius of a hydrogen atom.

(b) Maximum torque in a field of \( 1.5\times10^{4} \) N/C?

Torque is maximum when \( \theta = 90^\circ \), so \( \tau_{max} = pE \):

\[ \tau_{max} = (6.2\times10^{-30})(1.5\times10^{4}) = 9.3\times10^{-26}\ \text{N·m} \]

5. Electric Dipole in a Field

A dipole is two equal and opposite charges \( +q \) and \( -q \) rigidly separated by a distance \( d \). Its dipole moment is

\[ p = qd \]

pointing from the negative toward the positive charge.

Why it feels torque but no net force

Electrostatic forces act on the charged ends: \( F = qE \) on each. Because the field is uniform, those forces act in opposite directions with the same magnitude, so:

  • The net force is zero — the centre of mass does not move
  • But they do produce a net torque about the centre of mass, twisting the dipole into alignment

With the centre of mass a distance \( x \) from one end and \( d-x \) from the other:

\[ \tau = Fx\sin\theta + F(d-x)\sin\theta = Fd\sin\theta \]

and since \( F = qE \) and \( p = qd \):

\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta \qquad\text{or}\qquad \vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E \]

Potential energy

Choosing \( U = 0 \) at \( \theta = 90^\circ \), the potential energy of the dipole is

\[ U = -pE\cos\theta \qquad\text{or}\qquad U = -\vec p \cdot \vec E \]
