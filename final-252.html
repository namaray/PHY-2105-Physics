Q1 (a) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of each charge: away from +, toward −

🧭 Why? Take any point between +q and −q. The field of +q points away from +q — toward −q. The field of −q points toward −q — also toward −q. Both point the same way, so they add; their sum can never be zero there.
Answer: No. Between two opposite charges the two fields always point the same way (from + toward −), so E ≠ 0 at every point between them. A zero-field point can exist only on the axis outside the pair, beyond the charge of smaller magnitude (and nowhere if the magnitudes are equal).

Q1 (b) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E,\qquad U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

torque and energy of a dipole in a field

Step 1. A water molecule is a permanent dipole: its electrons crowd toward the oxygen, so the O side is negative and the H side positive (bond angle ~105°).

Step 2. The oven fills the food with an electric field that reverses direction about 2.45 billion times a second (it oscillates).

Step 3. Each time the field changes, it exerts a torque τ = pE sin θ that turns every water dipole toward the new field direction — so the molecules keep flipping back and forth.

Step 4. The rotating molecules jostle their neighbours and keep breaking and re-forming hydrogen bonds. That energy is shared out as random molecular motion — i.e. thermal energy — so the temperature rises.

Answer: The microwave's rapidly reversing field keeps exerting torques on the water dipoles, making them rotate back and forth; the energy of this agitation turns into heat (random motion), warming the sandwich.

Q1 (c) Short 2 marks

📝 Solution
📐 Formula
\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta \]
\[ \tau = 0\ \ \text{when}\ \sin\theta = 0,\ \text{i.e. } \theta = 0^\circ \text{ or } 180^\circ \]
🧭 Why? At 0° and 180° the forces +qE and −qE act along the same line (the dipole axis), so they cancel without twisting. 0° is stable (U = −pE, a nudge brings it back); 180° is unstable (U = +pE, a nudge flips it).
Answer: When the dipole moment is parallel (θ = 0°) or antiparallel (θ = 180°) to the field.

Q2 (a) Math 2 marks

  • \(q = +3.2\times10^{-15}\) C
  • \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C

\[ N = \frac{q}{e} \]

each electron removed leaves +e behind

Q2 (b) Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +20\ \mu\)C at 0, \(q_2 = -10\ \mu\)C at 4 m, \(q_3 = +40\ \mu\)C at 9 m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F = \sum F_i\ \text{(signs along x)} \]
\[ F_{12} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(20\times10^{-6})(10\times10^{-6})}{4^2} = 0.112\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{13} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(20\times10^{-6})(40\times10^{-6})}{9^2} = 0.0888\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? q₂ is negative → it attracts q₁ toward itself → +x. q₃ is positive like q₁ → it repels q₁ away from itself → −x.
\[ F = +0.112 - 0.0888 = +0.0236\ \text{N} \]
Answer: (i) \(2.36\times10^{-2}\) N; (ii) along +x (toward q₂) — the closer attraction beats the farther repulsion.

Q2 (c) Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +Q\) at \((-d, 0)\), \(q_2 = +2Q\) at \((0, d)\), \(q_3 = -8Q\) at \((0, -d)\)
  • \(Q = 5\) nC, \(d = 4\) m, \(q_0 = +1\) nC at O
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike attract.

\[ F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F_1 = \frac{k(5\times10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{16} = 2.81\times10^{-9} \\ F_2 = 2F_1 = 5.62\times10^{-9} \\ F_3 = 8F_1 = 2.25\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? q₁ (+, on the left) repels q₀ → pushes it right (+x). q₂ (+, above) repels → pushes it down. q₃ (−, below) attracts → pulls it down too. So F₂ and F₃ add.

Each force lies along an axis, so one component is the whole force and the other is zero:

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec F_1 = (+2.81\times10^{-9},\ 0) \\ \vec F_2 = (0,\ -5.62\times10^{-9}) \\ \vec F_3 = (0,\ -2.25\times10^{-8})\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F_x = +2.81\times10^{-9}\ \text{N} \\ F_y = -(5.62 + 22.5)\times10^{-9} = -2.81\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} F = \sqrt{(0.281)^2 + (2.81)^2}\times10^{-8} = 2.82\times10^{-8}\ \text{N} \\ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{2.81}{0.281} = 84.3^\circ \end{gathered} \]
Answer: (i) \(F = 2.82\times10^{-8}\) N; (ii) 84.3° below the +x axis (275.7°) — almost straight down, slightly to the right.

Q3 (a) Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(\pm5e\), \(d = 0.4\) nm
  • \(E = 8\times10^{-9}\) N/C
  • \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ p = qd \]
\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta \]
\[ p = (5)(1.6\times10^{-19})(4\times10^{-10}) = 3.2\times10^{-28}\ \text{C·m} \]

Step 1. (i) Parallel, θ = 0°:

\[ \tau = pE\sin0^\circ = 0 \]

Step 2. (ii) Perpendicular, θ = 90°:

\[ \tau = pE = (3.2\times10^{-28})(8\times10^{-9}) = 2.56\times10^{-36}\ \text{N·m} \]
Answer: (i) 0; (ii) \(2.56\times10^{-36}\) N·m (the maximum possible torque).

Q3 (b) Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(p = 5.98\times10^{-30}\) C·m
  • NH₄OH: 1 N (7 protons), 5 H (1 each), 1 O (8) → 20 protons (and 20 electrons)
  • (ii) \(\vec p = 5.98\times10^{-28}\,\hat k\) C·m, \(\vec E = 24\,\hat i\) N/C
  • \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ d = \frac{p}{Ne} \]

slide method: total charge Ne of one sign, separated by d

\[ U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Step 1. (i)

\[ d = \frac{5.98\times10^{-30}}{(20)(1.6\times10^{-19})} = 1.87\times10^{-12}\ \text{m} \]

Step 2. (ii) p is along k̂ and E along î, which are perpendicular:

\[ U = -(5.98\times10^{-28}\,\hat k)\cdot(24\,\hat i) = -(5.98\times10^{-28})(24)(\hat k\cdot\hat i) = 0 \]
Part (ii) gives p = 5.98×10⁻²⁸ (not ×10⁻³⁰ as in the first line). It doesn't matter here: the dot product is zero for any size of p.
Answer: (i) \(d = 1.87\times10^{-12}\) m; (ii) \(U = 0\) (p ⊥ E).

Q3 (c) Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • Corners in order around the square: +2q, +4q, −2q, −3q; side \(a = 4\) cm
  • \(q = 2\) nC
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge — away from +, toward −

\[ r = \frac{a}{\sqrt2} \]

corner to centre

Step 1. \(r = 0.04/\sqrt2 = 0.0283\) m, so one \(q\) at a corner gives

\[ E_0 = \frac{kq}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(2\times10^{-9})}{(0.0283)^2} = 2.25\times10^{4}\ \text{N/C} \]
🧭 Why? Positive corners push the field away, negative corners pull it in. +2q (top-left) and −2q (bottom-right) both give a field pointing toward the bottom-right corner: 2E₀ + 2E₀ = 4E₀. +4q (top-right) and −3q (bottom-left) both point toward the bottom-left corner: 4E₀ + 3E₀ = 7E₀. The two diagonals are at 90°.
\[ E = \sqrt{(4E_0)^2 + (7E_0)^2} = \sqrt{65}\,E_0 = 1.81\times10^{5}\ \text{N/C} \]

The same answer with x and y components (both diagonals are 45° from the horizontal, cos 45° = sin 45° = 0.707):

🧮 How to get the components Put the force (or field) on a right triangle. If θ is its angle with the horizontal:
\[ F_x = F\cos\theta,\qquad F_y = F\sin\theta \]
Then give each part a sign from the way it points: right / up = +, left / down = −. cos θ and sin θ come straight from the geometry: cos θ = (horizontal side) / (hypotenuse), sin θ = (vertical side) / (hypotenuse).
\[ \begin{gathered} 4E_0 = 8.99\times10^4\ \text{(down-right)} \\ x:\ (+8.99\times10^4)(0.707) = +6.36\times10^4 \\ y:\ -(8.99\times10^4)(0.707) = -6.36\times10^4 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} 7E_0 = 1.57\times10^5\ \text{(down-left)} \\ x:\ -(1.57\times10^5)(0.707) = -1.11\times10^5 \\ y:\ -(1.57\times10^5)(0.707) = -1.11\times10^5 \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} E_x = 6.36\times10^4 - 1.11\times10^5 = -4.77\times10^{4} \\ E_y = -6.36\times10^4 - 1.11\times10^5 = -1.75\times10^{5}\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]
\[ E = \sqrt{(4.77\times10^4)^2 + (1.75\times10^5)^2} = 1.81\times10^{5}\ \text{N/C} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{angle from the } -2q\text{ diagonal} = \tan^{-1}\frac{7}{4} = 60.3^\circ \\ \Rightarrow\ 254.7^\circ \text{ from } +x \end{gathered} \]
The paper doesn't fix which corner holds which charge. They are placed in the order given, going round the square — state this.
Answer: (ii) \(E = 1.81\times10^5\) N/C, pointing down and to the left at 254.7° from +x (15.3° to the left of straight down), toward the side between the −2q and −3q corners.

Q4 (a) Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • Charges \(-q\) with \(q = 2\times10^{-5}\) C at A \((-1, 0)\) and B \((1, 0)\)
  • P at (0, 4) m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum\frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]

scalar sum

Step 1. Distance from each charge: \(r = \sqrt{1^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{17} = 4.12\) m.

Step 2. (i)

\[ V = 2\cdot\frac{k(-2\times10^{-5})}{\sqrt{17}} = -8.72\times10^{4}\ \text{V}\ \Rightarrow\ |V| = 8.72\times10^{4}\ \text{V} \]

Step 3. (ii) For V = 0 the new charge must cancel B's contribution. Both are at the same distance, so

\[ \frac{kQ}{\sqrt{17}} + \frac{k(-q)}{\sqrt{17}} = 0\ \Longrightarrow\ Q = +q = +2\times10^{-5}\ \text{C} \]
Answer: (i) \(|V| = 8.72\times10^4\) V (V itself is negative); (ii) \(Q = +2\times10^{-5}\) C.

Q4 (b) Math 3 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • 20, −30, 30, −20 nC, all on a circle of radius r = 2 m
  • \(k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)

\[ V = \frac{k\sum q_i}{r} \]

all at the same distance from the centre → add the charges

Q5 (a) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Same derivation as Final 261 Q4 (a).

The formula given is the field of a ring of radius R and charge q at a point P on its axis, a distance z from the centre.

Step 1. Split the ring into small elements of length \(ds\), each carrying \(dq = \lambda\,ds\) with \(\lambda = q/2\pi R\). Treat each as a point charge:

\[ dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,ds}{z^2 + R^2} \]

Step 2. Symmetry: the element directly opposite gives a \(d\vec E\) with the same axial part and an equal but opposite sideways part. So all the sideways (\(dE\sin\theta\)) parts cancel and only the axial parts \(dE\cos\theta\) survive, with

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{z}{r} = \frac{z}{(z^2 + R^2)^{1/2}} \]

Step 3. Add up the axial parts around the whole ring (z and R are the same for every element):

\[ E = \oint dE\cos\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,\lambda}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\oint ds = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{z\,\lambda\,(2\pi R)}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}} \]

Step 4. Since \(\lambda(2\pi R) = q\):

\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{qz}{(z^2 + R^2)^{3/2}} \]
Answer: \(E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{qz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}\), directed along the axis (away from the ring for q > 0). Checks: E = 0 at the centre (z = 0); E → kq/z² far away.

Q5 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Same derivation as Final 261 Q4 (b).

Step 1. A dipole at angle θ to \(\vec E\) feels a torque that tries to decrease θ:

\[ \tau = -pE\sin\theta \]

Step 2. Work done by the field as the dipole turns from \(\theta_0\) to θ:

\[ W = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}\tau\,d\theta = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta}(-pE\sin\theta)\,d\theta = pE(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0) \]

Step 3. Potential energy change \(\Delta U = -W\). Choose the reference \(U = 0\) at \(\theta_0 = 90^\circ\) (\(\cos90^\circ = 0\)):

\[ U(\theta) - 0 = -pE\cos\theta + pE\cos90^\circ\ \Longrightarrow\ U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

Step 4. Conditions:

\[ \begin{gathered} U_{max} = +pE\ \ \text{at } \theta = 180^\circ \\ U = 0\ \ \text{at } \theta = 90^\circ \\ U_{min} = -pE\ \ \text{at } \theta = 0^\circ \end{gathered} \]
Answer: \(U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E\). Maximum (+pE) when p is antiparallel to E (180°, unstable); zero when p ⊥ E (90°); minimum (−pE) when aligned (0°, stable).

Q6 (a) Math 4 marks

📝 Solution
Given
  • Corners: \(+2q_1,\ -3q_1,\ -q_1,\ +2q_1\); middles of the long sides: \(+4q_2\) each
  • \(q_1 = q\), \(q_2 = 2q\); rectangle \(2a\times a\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum\frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]

Step 1. Corners are all \(\sqrt{a^2 + (a/2)^2} = \tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}a\) from the centre, and their charges add to

\[ 2q_1 - 3q_1 - q_1 + 2q_1 = 0\ \Longrightarrow\ V_{corners} = 0 \]

Step 2. The two middle charges are \(a/2\) away:

\[ V = 2\cdot\frac{k(4q_2)}{a/2} = \frac{16kq_2}{a} = \frac{16k(2q)}{a} = \frac{32kq}{a} = \frac{8q}{\pi\varepsilon_0 a} \]
Answer: \(V = \dfrac{32kq}{a} = \dfrac{8q}{\pi\varepsilon_0\,a}\) (only the two \(+4q_2\) charges contribute).

Q6 (b) Show that 4 marks

📝 Solution

Same derivation as Final 261 Q5 (b).

Step 1. Superpose the potentials of \(+q\) and \(-q\), at distances \(r_{(+)}\) and \(r_{(-)}\) from P:

\[ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q}{r_{(+)}} - \frac{q}{r_{(-)}}\right) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{r_{(-)} - r_{(+)}}{r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)}} \]

Step 2. Far away (\(r \gg d\)) the two lines to P are almost parallel. Then

\[ \begin{gathered} r_{(-)} - r_{(+)} \approx d\cos\theta \\ r_{(-)}\,r_{(+)} \approx r^2 \end{gathered} \]

Step 3. Substitute, and use \(p = qd\):

\[ V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{d\cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} \]
Answer: \(V = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}\). On the axis (θ = 0) V = kp/r²; on the bisector (θ = 90°) V = 0.
