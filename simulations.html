Coulomb's Law

1. Two charges: how big is the force, and which way?

Drag either charge. Change their signs and sizes with the sliders. Press Release to let them move.

  • Unlike signs (+ and −): the arrows point toward each other — attraction. Like signs: they point away — repulsion.
  • Make q₁ = +5 and q₂ = −1. The two arrows stay exactly the same length — the big charge pulls the small one as hard as the small one pulls it (Newton's 3rd law).
  • Drag them twice as far apart: the force drops to ¼, not ½. Halve the distance: ×4. The graph shows the 1/r² curve.
  • Set one charge to 0 — no force at all.
Explains: Assignment-2 Q1, Coulomb Example-2, any "attract or repel?" short question.

2. Several charges: the net force on one of them

The charge with the gold ring is the one we're finding the force on. Each other charge gives it one yellow force; the green arrow is their vector sum. Click a charge to select it, then change its value.

Presets:
  • Triangle: the two pulls are mirror images — their sideways parts cancel, so the net points straight down. Drag one corner and watch the symmetry break.
  • Square: the two +2q corners push in opposite directions and cancel; +4q pushes and −2q pulls the same way, so they add.
  • Null point: the net force is zero only at that one spot — nudge the target left or right and the bigger push wins.
  • Three in a line: the target is negative, so both positives pull it — in opposite directions. The closer one wins.
Explains: A2 Q2, Q4, Q6, Coulomb Example-5, P6.

Electric Field

3. Field lines lab

Drag the charges, or click anywhere to move the white probe. The probe shows the field there; put a test charge on it to see the force.

Presets:
  • Lines start on + and end on −, and never cross.
  • Set a charge to ±4: it gets twice as many lines as a ±2 charge.
  • Two +: put the probe exactly midway — E is zero there (a null point). Lines from the two charges bend away from each other.
  • Put a −1 μC test charge on the probe: the force arrow flips, but the field arrow doesn't — the field belongs to the place, not to the test charge.
  • Parallel plates: between them the lines are straight and evenly spaced — a uniform field.
  • Tick "each charge's E": the yellow arrows add head-to-tail into the green one — superposition.
Explains: field-line drawings (Quiz-4 graphs), A2 Q7, Q10, PPS-4 #5.

4. Dipole in a uniform field: torque, energy, equilibrium

Drag the dipole to any angle, then press Play. The field points right. p (green) points from − to +.

  • The two yellow forces are equal and opposite — net force zero, so the dipole doesn't drift. But they don't act along the same line, so they twist it (torque).
  • At 90° the torque is biggest (pE). At 0° and 180° it's zero.
  • Set θ = 179° and press Play with low damping: a tiny nudge sends it swinging round — unstable. At 0° it just sits — stable (lowest U).
  • Watch U on the graph: −pE at 0°, +pE at 180°. Turning it from 0° to 180° costs 2pE of work.
Explains: Field slide P3, A2 Q8, the "stable/unstable equilibrium" and "torque but no net force" short questions.

5. A charged particle fired between two plates

A particle enters a uniform field between two plates (10 cm long, 4 cm apart). Press Fire.

  • The electron bends toward the + plate — its force is opposite to E. Flip the plates and it bends the other way.
  • Switch to a proton with the same settings: it barely bends. Same force (same |q|), but 1836× the mass → 1836× less acceleration.
  • Raise E or lower v₀ until the electron hits the plate. Deflection y = ½at² with t = L/v₀, so halving v₀ quadruples it.
  • The path is a parabola (constant sideways force, like a thrown ball), then a straight line once it leaves the plates.
Explains: PPS-4 #17, #18, and F = qE, a = qE/m in general.

Electric Potential

6. Potential map: V is a number, E is an arrow

Colour shows the potential: red = positive V, blue = negative V, neutral = near zero. Gold lines are equipotentials. Move the probe to read V and E.

Presets:
  • Dipole: probe at the midpoint — V = 0 but E ≠ 0. The V = 0 line runs straight down the middle.
  • Two +: probe at the midpoint — E = 0 but V ≠ 0.
  • Drag the probe along a gold line: V doesn't change → no work to move a charge along it.
  • Turn on field lines: they cross the gold lines at 90° everywhere, and point from red (high V) to blue (low V).
  • Tick "each charge's contribution": the E's need arrows, the V's are just added as numbers with signs.
  • Parallel plates: equipotentials become flat, evenly spaced lines — V changes steadily (V = Ed).
Explains: Potential slide P3, PPS-5 #10, #18, equipotential drawings.

7. Moving a charge in a uniform field: ΔV, ΔU and work

The top plate is at high potential, the bottom at 0 V; E points down. Drag the test charge anywhere — the readout compares where you started with where you are. Or press Release to let the field move it.

  • Drag the + charge sideways along a dashed line: ΔV = 0, so no work.
  • Drag it up toward the + plate: you're moving against the force, so the field does negative work and U rises.
  • Switch to −1 μC and drag it up: V rises but U falls (ΔU = qΔV with q < 0). That's exactly the electron in slide Problem-1.
  • Press Release: the charge accelerates along its force, U drops and K rises by the same amount (energy conservation).
Explains: Potential slide P1, PPS-5 #6, #13, and W = −ΔU, ΔV = ΔU/q.

8. Closest approach: kinetic energy turns into potential energy

Two charges approach head-on. Watch the energy bars: K drains into U as they slow, then turns back into K as they fly apart.

  • Double the speed: K goes up 4×, so rmin = kq₁q₂/K drops to about ¼.
  • The total bar never changes — energy is conserved the whole way.
  • Proton + alpha: at the closest point they do not both stop — they're still moving together (momentum is conserved), so less K becomes U and they don't get quite as close as the slide formula says.
  • Unlike charges: there's no turning point — U keeps falling, K keeps rising, and they collide.
Explains: Potential slide P4, PPS-5 #8, #9, and the U-vs-r graph.
