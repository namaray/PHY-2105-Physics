Q1 — Null point and force at the midpoint

Problem: Two charges +16 nC and +9 nC are 50 cm apart. (i) At what point on the line joining them is the electric force zero? (ii) What is the electrostatic force at the midpoint if a positive test charge of +2 nC is placed there?

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +16\) nC \(= 16\times10^{-9}\) C, \(q_2 = +9\) nC
  • separation \(d = 50\) cm \(= 0.50\) m
  • test charge \(q_0 = +2\times10^{-9}\) C
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — the size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

\[ \frac{kq_1q_0}{x^2} = \frac{kq_2q_0}{(d-x)^2} \]

null point: the two forces on a test charge are equal and opposite

(i) Null point

🧭 Why? A test charge placed between two like charges is pushed one way by each, so the forces can cancel there. Outside them both pushes point the same way, so they can't. The point sits nearer the smaller charge (9 nC), because the bigger charge's force must be weakened by more distance.

Step 1. Let \(x\) be the distance from the 16 nC charge. Cancel \(kq_0\) from both sides:

\[ \frac{16}{x^2} = \frac{9}{(0.50 - x)^2} \]

Step 2. Take the square root of both sides (no quadratic needed):

\[ \frac{4}{x} = \frac{3}{0.50 - x}\ \Longrightarrow\ 4(0.50 - x) = 3x\ \Longrightarrow\ x = \frac{2.0}{7} = 0.286\ \text{m} \]

(ii) Force at the midpoint

Step 3. Each charge is \(r = 0.25\) m from the midpoint.

\[ F_{16} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(16\times10^{-9})(2\times10^{-9})}{(0.25)^2} = 4.60\times10^{-6}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{9} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(9\times10^{-9})(2\times10^{-9})}{(0.25)^2} = 2.59\times10^{-6}\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? All three charges are positive, so both forces on the +2 nC are repulsive: the 16 nC pushes it toward the 9 nC (away from itself), and the 9 nC pushes it toward the 16 nC. They point in opposite directions, so subtract.

Step 4. Net force:

\[ F = F_{16} - F_9 = 4.60\times10^{-6} - 2.59\times10^{-6} = 2.01\times10^{-6}\ \text{N} \]
Answer: (i) 0.286 m (28.6 cm) from the +16 nC charge, i.e. 21.4 cm from the +9 nC charge. (ii) \(2.01\times10^{-6}\) N, directed toward the +9 nC charge.

Q2 — Equilateral triangle: force on B

Problem: Charges of magnitude 10 μC are at the corners A, B, C of an equilateral triangle of side 3 m. A and C are negative, B is positive. (i) Magnitude and direction of the total force on B; (ii) draw the force direction diagram; (iii) x- and y-components of the resultant; (iv) express all forces as vectors.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(|q| = 10\ \mu\)C \(= 1.0\times10^{-5}\) C each; A, C negative, B positive
  • side \(r = 3\) m, all angles 60°
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
  • Axes: A at (0, 0), C at (3, 0), B at (1.5, 2.60) m — base along x, B on top
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — the size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

\[ F_x = F\cos\theta,\quad F_y = F\sin\theta \]

resolve each force into components

\[ F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} \]

magnitude of the resultant

Step 1. Size of each force (same charges, same distance):

\[ F_{BA} = F_{BC} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(1.0\times10^{-5})^2}{3^2} = 0.0999\ \text{N} \approx 0.100\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? B is positive and A, C are negative, so both are attractions: B is pulled toward A and toward C. From B, the line to A goes down-left at 60° below the horizontal (the triangle's angle), and the line to C goes down-right at 60°.

Step 2. (ii) Force direction diagram:

Step 3. (iii) Components (cos 60° = 0.5, sin 60° = 0.866):

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec F_{BA}:\ F_x = -0.0999(0.5) = -0.0500 \\ F_y = -0.0999(0.866) = -0.0865\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \vec F_{BC}:\ F_x = +0.0999(0.5) = +0.0500 \\ F_y = -0.0999(0.866) = -0.0865\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \sum F_x = -0.0500 + 0.0500 = 0 \\ \sum F_y = -0.0865 - 0.0865 = -0.173\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? The two pulls are mirror images about the vertical line through B, so their sideways (x) parts cancel and only the downward parts survive.

Step 4. (i) Resultant:

\[ F = \sqrt{0^2 + (-0.173)^2} = 0.173\ \text{N}\quad(\text{shortcut: } 2F\cos30^\circ = \sqrt3\,F) \]

Step 5. (iv) As vectors:

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec F_{BA} = (-0.0500\,\hat i - 0.0865\,\hat j)\ \text{N} \\ \vec F_{BC} = (0.0500\,\hat i - 0.0865\,\hat j)\ \text{N} \\ \vec F = (-0.173\,\hat j)\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
Answer: (i) 0.173 N, straight down (−y), from B toward the midpoint of AC. (iii) \(F_x = 0\), \(F_y = -0.173\) N. (iv) as above.

Q3 — Pennies with the electrons removed

Problem: What is (i) the Coulomb force between two 4 g pennies 1 m apart if we remove all the electrons from the \(^{23}_{11}\)Mg atoms? (ii) What is their acceleration as they separate?

📝 Solution
Given
  • mass of each penny \(m = 4\) g = 0.004 kg
  • \(^{23}_{11}\)Mg as printed: mass number A = 23, atomic number Z = 11
  • \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\) /mol
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ N = \frac{m}{M}N_A \]

number of atoms (M = molar mass = 23 g/mol)

\[ q = N\,Z\,e \]

charge left after removing all Z electrons from each atom

\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — the size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

\[ a = \frac{F}{m} \]

Newton's second law

The symbol is printed as \(^{23}_{11}\)Mg. Magnesium's atomic number is really 12 (Z = 11 is sodium, which does have A = 23). Below uses Z = 11 as printed; with Z = 12 the answers become \(F = 3.64\times10^{20}\) N and \(a = 9.09\times10^{22}\) m/s².

Step 1. Atoms in one penny:

\[ N = \frac{4}{23}(6.022\times10^{23}) = 1.047\times10^{23}\ \text{atoms} \]

Step 2. Remove all 11 electrons from each atom — the penny keeps a charge of +Ze per atom:

\[ q = (1.047\times10^{23})(11)(1.6\times10^{-19}) = 1.84\times10^{5}\ \text{C} \]

Step 3. Force between the two identical pennies:

\[ F = \frac{kq^2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(1.84\times10^5)^2}{1^2} = 3.05\times10^{20}\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? Both pennies are left positive, so the force is repulsive — they push each other apart, which is why the question says "as they separate".

Step 4. Acceleration (electron mass is negligible, so m stays 0.004 kg):

\[ a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{3.05\times10^{20}}{0.004} = 7.64\times10^{22}\ \text{m/s}^2 \]
Answer: (i) \(F \approx 3.05\times10^{20}\) N (repulsive); (ii) \(a \approx 7.64\times10^{22}\) m/s², each penny accelerating away from the other. (With Z = 12: \(3.64\times10^{20}\) N and \(9.09\times10^{22}\) m/s².)

Q4 — Three charges on the x-axis: force on q₂

Problem: \(q_1 = +25\) nC at the origin, \(q_2 = -12\) nC at x = 2 m, \(q_3 = +18\) nC at x = 3 m. What is the net force on \(q_2\), its direction, and draw all the force directions.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +25\times10^{-9}\) C at 0; \(q_2 = -12\times10^{-9}\) C at 2 m; \(q_3 = +18\times10^{-9}\) C at 3 m
  • \(r_{12} = 2\) m, \(r_{32} = 1\) m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — the size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

\[ F_{net} = \sum F\ \text{(with signs along x)} \]

all forces lie on one line: take +x as positive

Step 1. Force from q₁:

\[ F_{12} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(25\times10^{-9})(12\times10^{-9})}{2^2} = 6.74\times10^{-7}\ \text{N} \]

Step 2. Force from q₃:

\[ F_{32} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(18\times10^{-9})(12\times10^{-9})}{1^2} = 1.94\times10^{-6}\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? q₂ is negative and both others are positive, so both forces are attractions. q₁ sits to the left, so it pulls q₂ left (−x); q₃ sits to the right, so it pulls q₂ right (+x). Opposite directions → subtract.

Step 3. Net force (+x positive):

\[ F = +1.94\times10^{-6} - 6.74\times10^{-7} = +1.27\times10^{-6}\ \text{N} \]

All forces on q₂ (arrows to scale).

Answer: \(1.27\times10^{-6}\) N in the +x direction (toward q₃). q₃ wins even though it's the smaller charge because it is twice as close (force ∝ 1/r²).

Q5 — Charges in 1 kg

Problem: How many charges are available in (i) 1 kg of electrons? (ii) 1 kg of AlCl₂?

📝 Solution
Given
  • electron mass \(m_e = 9.11\times10^{-31}\) kg
  • \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
  • Al: Z = 13, M = 27 g/mol; Cl: Z = 17, M = 35.5 g/mol
  • \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\) /mol
📐 Formula
\[ N = \frac{\text{total mass}}{\text{mass of one particle}} \]

number of particles

\[ q = Ne \]

total charge carried by N elementary charges

\[ n = \frac{m}{M},\quad N_{units} = nN_A \]

moles, then formula units

(i) 1 kg of electrons

Step 1.

\[ N = \frac{1}{9.11\times10^{-31}} = 1.10\times10^{30}\ \text{electrons} \]

Step 2.

\[ q = Ne = (1.10\times10^{30})(1.6\times10^{-19}) = 1.76\times10^{11}\ \text{C}\quad(\text{negative}) \]

(ii) 1 kg of AlCl₂

Step 3. Molar mass and number of formula units:

\[ \begin{gathered} M = 27 + 2(35.5) = 98\ \text{g/mol} \\ N_{units} = \frac{1000}{98}(6.022\times10^{23}) = 6.14\times10^{24} \end{gathered} \]

Step 4. Electrons per formula unit = 13 + 2(17) = 47 (a neutral substance has as many protons):

\[ \begin{gathered} N_e = 47(6.14\times10^{24}) = 2.89\times10^{26} \\ q = (2.89\times10^{26})(1.6\times10^{-19}) = 4.62\times10^{7}\ \text{C} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? A neutral substance has equal numbers of electrons and protons, so its net charge is zero. "Charges available" means the electrons (or, equally, the protons) it contains — the same count either way.
AlCl₂ is unusual (aluminium chloride is AlCl₃). With AlCl₃ (M = 133.5 g/mol, 64 electrons per unit) you get the same answer to 3 s.f.: \(2.89\times10^{26}\) electrons, \(4.62\times10^{7}\) C — because almost every material has about one electron per two nucleons.
Answer: (i) \(1.10\times10^{30}\) electrons, total charge \(-1.76\times10^{11}\) C. (ii) \(2.89\times10^{26}\) electrons (and as many protons), i.e. \(4.62\times10^{7}\) C of each sign.

Q6 — Square: force on +q at the centre

Problem: \(q_1 = +2q\), \(q_2 = +4q\), \(q_3 = +2q\), \(q_4 = -2q\) at the corners of a square (figure). (i) Draw the arrangement; (ii) find the magnitude and direction of the force on a charge +1q at the intersection of the diagonals, side 10 cm, \(q = 3\times10^{-9}\) C.

Assignment figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q = 3\times10^{-9}\) C; side \(a = 0.10\) m
  • from the figure: q₄ at the origin O, q₁ above it, q₂ top-right, q₃ bottom-right
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Coulomb's law — the size of the force. Like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

\[ r = \frac{a}{\sqrt2} \]

corner-to-centre distance

\[ F_0 = \frac{k(q)(q)}{r^2} \]

unit force: one q at a corner acting on the +q at the centre

Step 1. Distance and the unit force:

\[ \begin{gathered} r = \frac{0.10}{\sqrt2} = 0.0707\ \text{m} \\ F_0 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(3\times10^{-9})^2}{(0.0707)^2} = 1.62\times10^{-5}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. So the four forces are 2F₀, 4F₀, 2F₀ and 2F₀. Now the directions:

🧭 Why?
  • q₁ (+2q, top-left): repels the +q → pushes it toward the bottom-right corner (q₃).
  • q₃ (+2q, bottom-right): repels → pushes toward the top-left (q₁). Equal size, opposite to q₁'s push → these two cancel.
  • q₂ (+4q, top-right): repels → pushes toward the bottom-left corner (q₄).
  • q₄ (−2q, bottom-left): attracts the +q → pulls it toward q₄, also bottom-left. Same direction as q₂'s push → these two add.

Step 3. (i) Arrangement and the forces:

Step 4. Net force, along the diagonal toward q₄:

\[ F = 4F_0 + 2F_0 = 6F_0 = 6(1.62\times10^{-5}) = 9.71\times10^{-5}\ \text{N} \]

Step 5. As a vector (the diagonal toward q₄ points at 225°, both components negative):

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec F = 9.71\times10^{-5}(\cos225^\circ\,\hat i + \sin225^\circ\,\hat j) \\ = (-6.87\,\hat i - 6.87\,\hat j)\times10^{-5}\ \text{N} \end{gathered} \]
Answer: \(9.71\times10^{-5}\) N along the diagonal toward q₄ (the −2q corner at the origin), i.e. at 225° from +x.

Q7 — Electric field at P from two charges

Problem: −q = −4 C at x = −4 m and q = +4 C at x = +4 m. (i) Magnitude and direction of the net field at P on the y-axis at y = 4 m. (ii) If the −q is changed to +4 C and a +4q charge is placed at O (0, 0), find the net field at P.

Assignment figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • charges ±4 C at (∓4, 0) m; P at (0, 4) m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge — points away from +, toward −

\[ r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \]

distance from each charge to P

\[ \vec E = \sum\vec E_i,\quad E_x = E\cos\theta,\ E_y = E\sin\theta \]

add the fields as vectors (components)

(i) −4 C and +4 C

Step 1. Distance and size of each field:

\[ \begin{gathered} r = \sqrt{4^2 + 4^2} = 4\sqrt2 = 5.66\ \text{m} \\ E = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(4)}{32} = 1.12\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C each} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? +4 C at (4, 0): field points away from it, i.e. from (4, 0) through P and beyond → up-left at 45°. −4 C at (−4, 0): field points toward it → from P down-left at 45°. One goes up, one goes down by the same amount, so the y-parts cancel; both go left, so the x-parts add.

Step 2. Components (cos 45° = 0.707):

\[ \begin{gathered} E_x = -2(1.12\times10^9)(0.707) = -1.59\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C} \\ E_y = (1.12 - 1.12)\times10^9(0.707) = 0 \end{gathered} \]

(ii) Both side charges +4 C, plus +4q = +16 C at O

🧭 Why? Now everything is positive, so every field points away from its charge. The left charge's field at P points up-right, the right charge's up-left: x-parts cancel, y-parts add. The +16 C at O is directly below P, so its field points straight up. Everything adds along +y.

Step 3. Side charges together:

\[ E_{sides} = 2(1.12\times10^9)(0.707) = 1.59\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C}\ (+y) \]

Step 4. Charge at O (r = 4 m):

\[ E_O = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(16)}{4^2} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N/C}\ (+y) \]

Step 5. Total:

\[ E = 1.59\times10^9 + 8.99\times10^9 = 1.06\times10^{10}\ \text{N/C} \]
Answer: (i) \(1.59\times10^{9}\) N/C in the −x direction (parallel to the axis, pointing from the + side toward the − side). (ii) \(1.06\times10^{10}\) N/C in the +y direction (straight up).

Q8 — Dipole in a field — vector form

Problem: \(\vec p = (4\hat i + 5\hat j + 7\hat k)\) C·m in \(\vec E = (4000\hat i + 2000\hat j + 3000\hat k)\) N/C. (i) Potential energy; (ii) torque; (iii) work by an agent turning it to \(\vec p = (5\hat i - 10\hat j)\) C·m; (iv) maximum potential energy; (v) work if the dipole rotates from 45° to 90°.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(\vec p = (4, 5, 7)\) C·m
  • \(\vec E = (4000, 2000, 3000)\) N/C
  • \(\vec p_f = (5, -10, 0)\) C·m
📐 Formula
\[ U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E = -(p_xE_x + p_yE_y + p_zE_z) \]

potential energy (dot product)

\[ \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

torque (cross product)

\[ W_{agent} = U_f - U_i \]

work done by an external agent

\[ U = -pE\cos\theta,\qquad U_{max} = +pE\ (\theta = 180^\circ) \]

magnitude form

(i) Potential energy

\[ \begin{gathered} U = -[(4)(4000) + (5)(2000) + (7)(3000)] \\ = -(16000 + 10000 + 21000) = -4.70\times10^{4}\ \text{J} \end{gathered} \]

(ii) Torque

Step 1. Write the determinant:

\[ \vec\tau = \begin{vmatrix}\hat i & \hat j & \hat k\\ 4 & 5 & 7\\ 4000 & 2000 & 3000\end{vmatrix} \]

Step 2. Expand (watch the minus sign on the ĵ term):

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat i\,(5\cdot3000 - 7\cdot2000) \\ - \hat j\,(4\cdot3000 - 7\cdot4000) + \hat k\,(4\cdot2000 - 5\cdot4000) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \vec\tau = (1000\,\hat i + 16000\,\hat j - 12000\,\hat k)\ \text{N·m} \\ |\tau| = \sqrt{1000^2 + 16000^2 + 12000^2} = 2.00\times10^{4}\ \text{N·m} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? For the ĵ term: \(-(4\cdot3000 - 7\cdot4000) = -(12000 - 28000) = +16000\). The minus in front of ĵ is part of the determinant rule — dropping it is the most common slip.

(iii) Work by the agent

\[ \begin{gathered} U_f = -[(5)(4000) + (-10)(2000) + (0)(3000)] \\ = -(20000 - 20000) = 0 \end{gathered} \]
\[ W_{agent} = U_f - U_i = 0 - (-4.70\times10^4) = +4.70\times10^{4}\ \text{J} \]
🧭 Why? U rises from −47 000 J to 0, so the agent has to put energy in — positive work. (The field itself does −4.70×10⁴ J.)

(iv) Maximum potential energy

\[ \begin{gathered} p = \sqrt{4^2 + 5^2 + 7^2} = \sqrt{90} = 9.49\ \text{C·m} \\ E = \sqrt{4000^2 + 2000^2 + 3000^2} = 5385\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]
\[ U_{max} = pE = (9.49)(5385) = 5.11\times10^{4}\ \text{J} \]
🧭 Why? \(U = -pE\cos\theta\) is largest when \(\cos\theta = -1\), i.e. θ = 180° — the dipole points exactly against the field (unstable equilibrium).

(v) Rotating from 45° to 90°

\[ \begin{gathered} W_{agent} = U_f - U_i = (-pE\cos90^\circ) - (-pE\cos45^\circ) \\ = pE\cos45^\circ = (5.11\times10^4)(0.707) = 3.61\times10^{4}\ \text{J} \end{gathered} \]
In (iii) the new \(\vec p\) has a different magnitude (\(\sqrt{125}\) vs \(\sqrt{90}\)), so it isn't a pure rotation — just apply \(W = U_f - U_i\) as asked.
Answer: (i) \(U = -4.70\times10^4\) J; (ii) \(\vec\tau = (1000\hat i + 16000\hat j - 12000\hat k)\) N·m, \(|\tau| = 2.00\times10^4\) N·m; (iii) \(+4.70\times10^4\) J; (iv) \(5.11\times10^4\) J; (v) \(+3.61\times10^4\) J by the agent (the field does \(-3.61\times10^4\) J).

Q9 — Electron near a dipole

Problem: An electron on the axis of an electric dipole is 5 nm from its centre. Find the field and the force on the electron if \(p = 5.6\times10^{-15}\) C·m (5 nm ≫ the charge separation).

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(z = 5\) nm \(= 5\times10^{-9}\) m
  • \(p = 5.6\times10^{-15}\) C·m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} = \frac{2kp}{z^3} \]

dipole field far away, on its axis (from the slides)

\[ F = |q|E = eE \]

force on the electron

Step 1. Field:

\[ \begin{gathered} E = \frac{2(8.99\times10^9)(5.6\times10^{-15})}{(5\times10^{-9})^3} \\ = \frac{1.007\times10^{-4}}{1.25\times10^{-25}} = 8.06\times10^{20}\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]

Step 2. Force:

\[ F = eE = (1.6\times10^{-19})(8.06\times10^{20}) = 129\ \text{N} \]
🧭 Why? On the dipole's axis the field points along \(\vec p\) (from − toward +, and onward). The electron is negative, so its force is opposite to \(\vec E\) — pointing back along −\(\vec p\).
\"Y axis\" here is read as the dipole's own axis — that's the case the slides derive. If it means the perpendicular bisector instead, use \(E = kp/z^3 = 4.03\times10^{20}\) N/C and \(F = 64.4\) N (half the values).
Answer: \(E = 8.06\times10^{20}\) N/C, \(F = 129\) N, directed opposite to \(\vec p\).

Q10 — Field at the centre of the shape

Problem: From the figure, calculate the net electric field at the centre of the shape where a +2.2 μC charge is brought. The electrons are 0.1 cm and the protons 0.6 cm from the centre.

Assignment figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • 4 protons (+e) at 0.6 cm = 0.006 m, on the axes
  • 4 electrons (−e) at 0.1 cm = 0.001 m, symmetric about the centre
  • \(q_0 = +2.2\times10^{-6}\) C
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge — points away from +, toward −

\[ \vec F = q_0\vec E \]

force on the charge brought to the centre

Step 1. Size of each field at the centre:

\[ \begin{gathered} E_p = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(1.6\times10^{-19})}{(0.006)^2} = 4.00\times10^{-5}\ \text{N/C} \\ E_e = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(1.6\times10^{-19})}{(0.001)^2} = 1.44\times10^{-3}\ \text{N/C} \end{gathered} \]
🧭 Why? Protons: top and bottom are equal and on opposite sides. The top one's field points away from it (down), the bottom one's points away from it (up) → they cancel. Same for left and right. Electrons: each one is matched by an electron diametrically opposite. Each field points toward its electron, so the pair point in opposite directions with equal size → they cancel.

Step 2. Every field is cancelled by its partner, so:

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec E_{net} = 0 \\ \vec F = q_0\vec E = (2.2\times10^{-6})(0) = 0 \end{gathered} \]
Field and force are zero here, but the potential is not: \(V = 4ke(1/0.006 - 1/0.001) = -4.80\times10^{-6}\) V (see PPS-5 #18).
Answer: Net field at the centre = 0 (so the +2.2 μC charge feels no force there).
