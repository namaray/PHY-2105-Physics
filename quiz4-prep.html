A2 — The field itself

1. Definition

\[ \vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q_0} \]

Unit N/C. \(q_0\) is a positive test charge.

2. Point charge

\[ E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q|}{r^2} = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

Away from +, toward −.

3. Superposition

\[ \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots + \vec E_n \]

A vector sum — resolve into components.

4. Resultant

\[ E = \sqrt{E_x^2+E_y^2},\quad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{E_y}{E_x} \]

Check the quadrant from the signs of \(E_x\), \(E_y\).

5. Two equal fields at angle φ

\[ E_{net} = 2E\cos\frac{\varphi}{2} \]

60° apart → \( \sqrt3\,E \); 90° apart → \( \sqrt2\,E \).

6. Null point between like charges

\[ \frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{q_2}{(d-x)^2} \]

Take square roots — no quadratic needed.

A3 — Force and motion in a field

7. Force on a charge

\[ \vec F = q\vec E \]

Electron: \( \vec F \) is opposite to \( \vec E \).

8. Acceleration

\[ a = \frac{|q|E}{m} \]

Then \( v = v_0 + at \), \( x = v_0t + \tfrac12at^2 \). (Not in the slides.)

A4 — Continuous charge

9. Linear charge density

\[ dq = \lambda\,ds,\quad dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,ds}{r^2} \]

10. Ring, on its axis

\[ E = \frac{kqz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}} \]

\( E = 0 \) at the centre; \( E \to kq/z^2 \) for \( z \gg R \).

A5 — Electric dipole

11. Dipole moment

\[ p = qd \]

Points from the negative to the positive charge. Unit C·m.

12. Dipole field (far, on axis)

\[ E = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} \]

Falls as \( 1/z^3 \), faster than a point charge.

13. Torque

\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta,\quad \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

Maximum \( pE \) at 90°.

14. Potential energy

\[ U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

\(-pE\) at 0° (stable), \(+pE\) at 180° (unstable).

15. Work

\[ W_{field} = -\Delta U,\qquad W_{agent} = U_f - U_i \]

0° → 180° costs \( 2pE \); 0° → θ costs \( pE(1-\cos\theta) \).

16. Molecule dipole (slide method)

\[ d = \frac{p}{Ne} \]

\(N\) = electrons in the molecule (10 for H₂O).

💬 B. Short-Question Bank

20–30% of the marks. All of these come straight off the slides.

Q. Define the electric field. What is its unit?

Place a positive test charge \( q_0 \) at a point and measure the force on it; the field is \( \vec E = \vec F/q_0 \). Its direction is the direction of the force on the positive test charge. SI unit: N/C.

Q. Is the electric field a scalar or a vector field? Give examples of each.

A vector field — a vector at every point. Temperature in a room and air pressure in the atmosphere are scalar fields.

Q. State the rules relating field lines to the field.

(1) The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of \( \vec E \) there. (2) The number of lines per unit area, measured perpendicular to the lines, is proportional to the magnitude of \( \vec E \) — so \(E\) is large where lines are close together. Lines extend away from positive charge (where they start) and toward negative charge (where they end).

Q. Why can two field lines never cross?

At a crossing the field would have two directions at one point, which is impossible — \( \vec E \) has one value everywhere.

Q. Who introduced field lines?

Michael Faraday, in the 19th century — he pictured the space around a charged body as filled with "lines of force".

Q. Describe the field lines of a uniformly charged sphere and of a large charged sheet.

Sphere: radial — outward if positive, inward if negative. Large uniformly charged sheet: parallel, evenly spaced lines perpendicular to the sheet, so the field is uniform.

Q. What is an electric dipole and its dipole moment?

Two charges of equal magnitude and opposite sign, \( \pm q \), separated by \( d \). The dipole moment \( \vec p \) has magnitude \( p = qd \) and points from the negative to the positive charge. Unit: C·m.

Q. Why does a dipole's field fall off faster than a point charge's?

Far away, the fields of \( +q \) and \( -q \) almost cancel, leaving only the small difference: \( E \propto 1/z^3 \) instead of \( 1/r^2 \).

Q. A dipole sits in a uniform field. Why is there a torque but no net force?

The forces on the two ends are \( qE \) each, equal and opposite, so the net force is zero and the centre of mass doesn't move. But they act along different lines, so they form a couple: \( \tau = pE\sin\theta \), which rotates \( \vec p \) toward \( \vec E \).

Q. When is a dipole in stable and in unstable equilibrium?

Stable at \( \theta = 0^\circ \) (\( \vec p \) along \( \vec E \)), where \( U = -pE \) is least. Unstable at \( \theta = 180^\circ \), where \( U = +pE \) is greatest. Torque is zero at both. The slides compare it to a pendulum, which has least potential energy at its lowest point.

Q. Why is a water molecule a dipole? How does a microwave oven heat food?

In H₂O the electrons crowd toward the oxygen, so the oxygen side is negative and the hydrogen side positive; the two O–H bonds make 105°. \( \vec p \) points from the oxygen side to the hydrogen side. A microwave oven sets up a rapidly oscillating field; the torque keeps flipping the water dipoles, breaking hydrogen bonds between molecules, and that energy ends up as heat. No dipoles, no microwave ovens.

Q. What is the field at the centre of a uniformly charged ring, and far away from it?

Zero at the centre — the field of each element is cancelled by the element opposite it. Far away (\( z \gg R \)) the ring "looks like" a point charge: \( E \to kq/z^2 \).

Q. What is linear charge density?

Charge per unit length, \( \lambda \) (C/m). A continuous charge is split into elements \( dq = \lambda\,ds \), each treated as a point charge, and their fields are added (integrated).

Q. Give some typical field strengths.

Surface of a uranium nucleus \( 3\times10^{21} \) N/C; inside a hydrogen atom \( 5\times10^{11} \); electric breakdown of air \( 3\times10^{6} \); near a photocopier drum \( 10^{5} \); near a charged comb \( 10^{3} \); lower atmosphere \( 10^{2} \); inside household copper wire \( 10^{-2} \) N/C.

⚡ E. Last-Minute Traps

Things that cost easy marks

  • Draw every arrow before calculating. Away from +, toward −. Most wrong answers here are direction errors, not arithmetic.
  • Look for symmetry first. Equal charges at equal distances on opposite sides cancel.
  • Resolve, then add. Never add magnitudes of fields at an angle.
  • Electron force is opposite to \(\vec E\).
  • Dipole angle is from \(\vec E\). If the figure measures from the horizontal and \(\vec E\) is vertical, convert (PPS 22).
  • External agent: \( W = U_f - U_i \). The field's own work has the opposite sign.
  • Convert units: cm → m, nC → \(10^{-9}\), μC → \(10^{-6}\), nm → \(10^{-9}\), fm → \(10^{-15}\).
  • Give direction as well as magnitude — an angle from \(+x\), a unit vector, or "toward the ... charge".
