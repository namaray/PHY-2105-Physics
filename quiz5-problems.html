SECTION 1: Slide Problems

Slide Problem-1 — Electron lifted 520 m against the field Slide

Problem: An electron is moved upward 520 m by the electric force, in a downward field of 150 N/C. Find the change in its electric potential energy and in the electric potential.

Slide figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(E = 150\) N/C, pointing down
  • displacement \(d = 520\) m, up
  • electron: \(q = -e = -1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
📐 Formula
\[ W = Fd\cos\theta,\qquad F = |q|E \]

work done by the electric force (θ = angle between force and displacement)

\[ \Delta U = -W \]

change in potential energy

\[ \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} \]

change in potential (divide by the charge, sign included)

Step 1. Force on the electron. A negative charge is pushed opposite to \(\vec E\), so the force is up — the same way as the displacement (θ = 0°).

\[ F = |q|E = (1.6\times10^{-19})(150) = 2.4\times10^{-17}\ \text{N} \]

Step 2. Work done by the field:

\[ W = Fd\cos0^\circ = (2.4\times10^{-17})(520)(1) = 1.25\times10^{-14}\ \text{J} \approx 1.2\times10^{-14}\ \text{J} \]

Step 3. Change in potential energy:

\[ \Delta U = -W = -1.2\times10^{-14}\ \text{J} \]

Step 4. Change in potential:

\[ \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} = \frac{-1.2\times10^{-14}}{-1.6\times10^{-19}} = 7.5\times10^{4}\ \text{V} \]
The slide prints ΔV = 4.5×10⁴ V (45 kV) — that's an arithmetic slip: 1.2/1.6 = 0.75, so it is 7.5×10⁴ V. Unrounded it is exactly \(Ed = 150\times520 = 7.8\times10^4\) V. Show the division line so your working stands either way.
Answer: \(\Delta U = -1.2\times10^{-14}\) J (energy falls); \(\Delta V = +7.5\times10^4\) V — the electron moves to a higher potential (upward, against \(\vec E\)).

Slide Problem-2 — Potential at the centre of a square Slide

Problem: \(q_1 = 12\) nC, \(q_2 = -24\) nC, \(q_3 = 31\) nC, \(q_4 = 17\) nC at the corners of a square of side \(d = 1.3\) m. What is \(V\) at the centre P?

Slide figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = +12\), \(q_2 = -24\), \(q_3 = +31\), \(q_4 = +17\) nC (1 nC = \(10^{-9}\) C)
  • side \(d = 1.3\) m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

\[ r = \frac{d}{\sqrt2} \]

distance from a corner of a square to its centre (half the diagonal)

Step 1. Every corner is the same distance from P:

\[ r = \frac{1.3}{\sqrt2} = 0.919\ \text{m} \]

Step 2. Same r for all four, so add the charges first:

\[ q_1+q_2+q_3+q_4 = 12 - 24 + 31 + 17 = 36\ \text{nC} = 36\times10^{-9}\ \text{C} \]

Step 3. Potential:

\[ V = \frac{k(36\times10^{-9})}{r} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(36\times10^{-9})}{0.919} = 352\ \text{V} \]
Answer: ≈ 350 V (the slide's answer).

Slide Problem-3 — Field AND potential at the origin Slide

Problem: −1.0 μC at (0, 1) and +1.0 μC at (1, 0) (metres). At the origin find E (magnitude, direction), V, and the energy to bring +1.0 μC there from infinity.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = -1.0\ \mu\)C at (0, 1); \(q_2 = +1.0\ \mu\)C at (1, 0)
  • both are \(r = 1.0\) m from the origin
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge — a vector: away from +, toward −

\[ V = \frac{kq}{r} \]

potential of a point charge — a scalar, keeps the sign of q

\[ W = qV \]

work to bring charge q from infinity (where V = 0) to a point at potential V

Step 1. Field of each charge (same |q|, same r):

\[ E_1 = E_2 = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(1.0\times10^{-6})}{1^2} \approx 9000\ \text{N/C} \]

Step 2. Directions: away from +q at (1, 0) → points left; toward −q at (0, 1) → points up. They are at 90°:

\[ E = \sqrt{E_1^2 + E_2^2} = \sqrt2\,(9000) = 1.27\times10^{4}\ \text{N/C},\qquad \theta = 135^\circ \]

Step 3. Potential — just add with signs:

\[ V = \frac{k(-1.0\times10^{-6})}{1} + \frac{k(+1.0\times10^{-6})}{1} = -9000 + 9000 = 0\ \text{V} \]

Step 4. Work:

\[ W = qV = (1.0\times10^{-6})(0) = 0\ \text{J} \]

E needs vectors; V doesn't.

Answer: \(E = 1.27\times10^4\) N/C at 135° (up and left); \(V = 0\); no work needed. Key idea: E can be non-zero where V is zero.

Slide Problem-4 — Proton and alpha: closest approach Slide

Problem: A proton (mass m, charge +e) and an alpha particle (mass 4m, charge +2e) approach each other with the same speed v from far away. How close do they get?

📝 Solution
Given
  • proton: \(m\), \(+e\), speed \(v\)
  • alpha: \(4m\), \(+2e\), speed \(v\)
  • start: very far apart (U = 0)
📐 Formula
\[ K = \tfrac12 mv^2 \]

kinetic energy

\[ U = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r} \]

potential energy of two charges a distance r apart

\[ K_{initial} = U_{closest} \]

energy conservation: at closest approach all the kinetic energy has become potential energy (slide method)

Step 1. Total kinetic energy at the start:

\[ K = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12(4m)v^2 = \tfrac52 mv^2 \]

Step 2. Potential energy at closest approach r:

\[ U = \frac{k(e)(2e)}{r} = \frac{2ke^2}{r} \]

Step 3. Set them equal and solve for r:

\[ \frac{2ke^2}{r} = \frac52 mv^2\ \Longrightarrow\ r = \frac{4ke^2}{5mv^2} \]

Before and at closest approach.

This is the slide's method. Strictly, momentum is conserved so they can't both stop; the exact answer is \(5ke^2/4mv^2\). Use the slide's version in the exam.
Answer: \(r = \dfrac{4ke^2}{5mv^2}\).

Slide Problem-6 — Potential energy of three charges Slide

Problem: \(q_1 = +q\), \(q_2 = -4q\), \(q_3 = +2q\) at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side \(d = 12\) cm, \(q = 150\) nC. Find the potential energy U of the system.

Slide figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q = 150\times10^{-9}\) C
  • side \(d = 0.12\) m (all three pairs are d apart)
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ U = \sum_{\text{pairs}} \frac{kq_iq_j}{r_{ij}} = k\left(\frac{q_1q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2q_3}{r_{23}}\right) \]

energy belongs to each pair of charges; three charges → three pairs. Keep the signs.

Step 1. List the pair products (every \(r_{ij} = d\)):

\[ q_1q_2 = (q)(-4q) = -4q^2,\quad q_1q_3 = (q)(2q) = 2q^2,\quad q_2q_3 = (-4q)(2q) = -8q^2 \]

Step 2. Add and substitute:

\[ U = \frac{k}{d}(-4 + 2 - 8)q^2 = -\frac{10kq^2}{d} = -\frac{10(8.99\times10^9)(150\times10^{-9})^2}{0.12} = -1.7\times10^{-2}\ \text{J} \]
Answer: \(U = -1.7\times10^{-2}\) J = −17 mJ. Negative: an external agent would have to do 17 mJ of work to pull the system apart.

P1 — Square: +2q, +4q, +2q, −2q Practice

Problem: Four charges +2q, +4q, +2q and −2q are at the corners of a square of side 10 cm. (i) Draw the arrangement; (ii) find the net potential where the diagonals cross, with \(q = 57\times10^{-9}\) C.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q = 57\times10^{-9}\) C
  • side = 10 cm = 0.10 m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

\[ r = \frac{\text{side}}{\sqrt2} \]

corner-to-centre distance of a square

Step 1. (i) The drawing. Put the charges on the corners in order, A → B → C → D, and mark the centre O where the diagonals cross:

Arrangement for (i). Any order of the corners gives the same V at O, because every corner is the same distance away.

Step 2. Distance from each corner to O:

\[ r = \frac{0.10}{\sqrt2} = 0.0707\ \text{m} \]

Step 3. Same r for all four, so add the charges first:

\[ 2q + 4q + 2q - 2q = 6q = 6(57\times10^{-9}) = 3.42\times10^{-7}\ \text{C} \]

Step 4. Potential at O:

\[ V = \frac{k(6q)}{r} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(3.42\times10^{-7})}{0.0707} = 4.35\times10^{4}\ \text{V} \]
Answer: (i) as drawn; (ii) \(V = 4.35\times10^4\) V. (Compare PPS-4 #6: the field there needed vectors; potential just adds.)

P2 — Potential and work Practice

Problem: Calculate (i) the potential at a point 0.09 m from a charge of \(4\times10^{-7}\) C; (ii) the work done in bringing a charge of \(2\times10^{-9}\) C from infinity to that point.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q = 4\times10^{-7}\) C, \(r = 0.09\) m
  • \(q_0 = 2\times10^{-9}\) C
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \frac{kq}{r} \]

potential of a point charge (V = 0 at infinity)

\[ W = q_0V \]

work to bring \(q_0\) from infinity to a point at potential V

Step 1. (i) Potential:

\[ V = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(4\times10^{-7})}{0.09} = 4.0\times10^{4}\ \text{V} \]

Step 2. (ii) Work:

\[ W = q_0V = (2\times10^{-9})(4.0\times10^4) = 8.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{J} \]
Answer: (i) \(4.0\times10^4\) V; (ii) \(8.0\times10^{-5}\) J.

P3 — Thunderstorm in electron-volts Practice

Problem: The potential difference between ground and a cloud is \(1.2\times10^9\) V. In electron-volts, what is the magnitude of the change in potential energy of an electron moving between them?

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(|\Delta V| = 1.2\times10^9\) V
  • electron: \(|q| = e\)
📐 Formula
\[ \Delta U = q\,\Delta V \]

change in potential energy of a charge q moved through ΔV

\[ 1\ \text{eV} = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} \]

energy gained by charge e moving through 1 V

Step 1. With the charge written as "1 e", volts × e gives eV directly:

\[ |\Delta U| = e\,|\Delta V| = (1\,e)(1.2\times10^9\ \text{V}) = 1.2\times10^{9}\ \text{eV} = 1.2\ \text{GeV} \]

Step 2. (In joules, if asked:)

\[ |\Delta U| = (1.6\times10^{-19})(1.2\times10^9) = 1.92\times10^{-10}\ \text{J} \]
Answer: \(1.2\times10^9\) eV.

P4 — Two equal charges, potential at P Practice

Problem: Two equal charges of \(10\times10^{-5}\) C (drawn as −q) sit 10 cm either side of the origin; P is 10 cm up the y-axis. What is the magnitude of the potential at P?

Sheet figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q = -10\times10^{-5} = -1.0\times10^{-4}\) C each (shown as −q)
  • each charge is 0.10 m from the origin; P is 0.10 m above it
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

\[ r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \]

distance from each charge to P (Pythagoras)

Step 1. Distance from each charge to P:

\[ r = \sqrt{0.10^2 + 0.10^2} = 0.141\ \text{m} \]

Step 2. Two equal charges at the same distance:

\[ V = 2\cdot\frac{kq}{r} = 2\cdot\frac{(8.99\times10^9)(-1.0\times10^{-4})}{0.141} = -1.27\times10^{7}\ \text{V} \]
Answer: \(|V| = 1.27\times10^7\) V (the charges are negative, so V itself is negative).

P5 — Potential from the field Practice

Problem: A charged particle produces a field of 5.0 N/C at a point 60 cm away. What is the magnitude of its potential there, and its charge?

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(E = 5.0\) N/C
  • \(r = 60\) cm = 0.60 m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ E = \frac{kq}{r^2} \]

field of a point charge

\[ V = \frac{kq}{r} \]

potential of a point charge

\[ \frac{V}{E} = r\ \Longrightarrow\ V = Er \]

divide one by the other — a useful shortcut

Step 1. Charge, from the field formula:

\[ q = \frac{Er^2}{k} = \frac{(5.0)(0.60)^2}{8.99\times10^9} = 2.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{C} \]

Step 2. Potential:

\[ V = Er = (5.0)(0.60) = 3.0\ \text{V} \]
Answer: V = 3.0 V, q = 2.0×10⁻¹⁰ C (0.20 nC).

P6 — Ionosphere voltage Practice

Problem: A fair-weather field of 100 N/C acts downward through the 100 km up to the ionosphere. What is the ionosphere's voltage?

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(E = 100\) N/C (uniform)
  • \(d = 100\) km = \(1.0\times10^5\) m
📐 Formula
\[ \Delta V = Ed \]

uniform field: potential difference across a distance d along the field

Step 1.

\[ V = Ed = (100)(1.0\times10^{5}) = 1.0\times10^{7}\ \text{V} \]
Answer: \(1.0\times10^7\) V (10 MV). The field points down, so the ionosphere is at the higher potential.

P7 — Ammonia dipole Practice

Problem: NH₃ has dipole moment 1.47 D (1 D = \(3.34\times10^{-30}\) C·m). Find the potential 52.0 nm away along the dipole axis (V = 0 at infinity).

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(p = 1.47\) D
  • \(r = 52.0\) nm = \(52.0\times10^{-9}\) m
  • on the axis → \(\theta = 0^\circ\)
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \frac{kp\cos\theta}{r^2} \]

potential of a dipole far away (r ≫ separation); θ measured from the dipole axis

Step 1. Convert the dipole moment:

\[ p = 1.47 \times 3.34\times10^{-30} = 4.91\times10^{-30}\ \text{C·m} \]

Step 2. Potential:

\[ V = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(4.91\times10^{-30})\cos0^\circ}{(52.0\times10^{-9})^2} = 1.63\times10^{-5}\ \text{V} \]
Answer: \(1.63\times10^{-5}\) V.

P8 — Proton and alpha, v = 2×10⁸ m/s On the syllabus

Problem: A proton (mass \(m_p\), charge +e) and an alpha particle (mass \(4m_p\), charge +2e) approach each other with the same speed \(v = 2\times10^8\) m/s from far away. How close do they get before turning around?

📝 Solution
Given
  • proton: \(m_p = 1.67\times10^{-27}\) kg, \(+e\)
  • alpha: \(4m_p\), \(+2e\)
  • \(v = 2\times10^8\) m/s each
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\)
📐 Formula
\[ K = \tfrac12 mv^2 \]

kinetic energy of each particle

\[ U = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r} \]

potential energy of the pair

\[ K_{total} = U_{closest} \]

far apart U = 0; at closest approach the kinetic energy has all turned into U (slide method)

Step 1. Total kinetic energy:

\[ K = \tfrac12 m_pv^2 + \tfrac12(4m_p)v^2 = \tfrac52 m_pv^2 \]

Step 2. Potential energy at closest distance r:

\[ U = \frac{k(e)(2e)}{r} = \frac{2ke^2}{r} \]

Step 3. Equate and solve:

\[ \frac{2ke^2}{r} = \tfrac52 m_pv^2\ \Longrightarrow\ r = \frac{4ke^2}{5m_pv^2} \]

Step 4. Numbers:

\[ r = \frac{4(8.99\times10^9)(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{5(1.67\times10^{-27})(2\times10^8)^2} = \frac{9.21\times10^{-28}}{3.34\times10^{-10}} = 2.76\times10^{-18}\ \text{m} \]
Slide method (same as slide Problem-4). The strict momentum-conserving answer is \(5ke^2/4m_pv^2 = 4.31\times10^{-18}\) m — mention it only if asked.
Answer: \(r = 2.76\times10^{-18}\) m.

P9 — Alpha particle stopped by gold Practice

Problem: An alpha particle (2 protons) heads straight for a gold nucleus (79 protons) and momentarily stops at r = 9.23 fm from its centre. What was its initial kinetic energy?

Sheet figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_\alpha = 2e\), \(q_{Au} = 79e\)
  • \(r = 9.23\) fm = \(9.23\times10^{-15}\) m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\)
📐 Formula
\[ K_i = U_f = \frac{kq_\alpha q_{Au}}{r} \]

energy conservation: all K becomes U at the stopping point

\[ 1\ \text{eV} = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} \]

Step 1.

\[ K_i = \frac{k(2e)(79e)}{r} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(158)(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{9.23\times10^{-15}} = 3.94\times10^{-12}\ \text{J} \]

Step 2. In electron-volts:

\[ K_i = \frac{3.94\times10^{-12}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = 2.46\times10^{7}\ \text{eV} = 24.6\ \text{MeV} \]
Answer: \(3.94\times10^{-12}\) J ≈ 24.6 MeV.

P10 — Potential at the origin On the syllabus

Problem: −1.0 μC at (0, 1) and +3.0 μC at (3, 0) (metres). At the origin find (i) the potential (V = 0 at infinity), and (ii) the energy needed to bring a +3.0 μC charge there from infinity.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = -1.0\times10^{-6}\) C, \(r_1 = 1\) m
  • \(q_2 = +3.0\times10^{-6}\) C, \(r_2 = 3\) m
  • \(q_0 = +3.0\times10^{-6}\) C
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

\[ W = q_0V \]

energy to bring \(q_0\) from infinity

Step 1. (i) Potential at the origin:

\[ V = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2}\right) = (8.99\times10^9)\left(\frac{-1.0\times10^{-6}}{1} + \frac{3.0\times10^{-6}}{3}\right) = (8.99\times10^9)(0) = 0\ \text{V} \]

Step 2. (ii) Energy:

\[ W = q_0V = (3.0\times10^{-6})(0) = 0\ \text{J} \]
Answer: (i) 0 V; (ii) 0 J — the bigger charge is three times further away, so the two contributions cancel exactly. (The field at the origin is not zero.)

P11 — Three charges, potential at P Practice

Problem: \(q_1 = q_2 = +e\), \(q_3 = +2e\), \(a = 6.00\ \mu\)m (same figure as PPS-4 #13). Net potential at P?

Sheet figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(q_1 = q_2 = +e\), \(q_3 = +2e\)
  • \(a = 6.00\times10^{-6}\) m
  • P is the midpoint of the hypotenuse (foot of the perpendicular from 3)
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

\[ r = \frac{a}{\sqrt2} \]

midpoint of the hypotenuse is equidistant from all three corners

Step 1. All three charges are the same distance from P:

\[ r = \frac{a\sqrt2}{2} = \frac{6.00\times10^{-6}}{\sqrt2} = 4.24\times10^{-6}\ \text{m} \]

Step 2. Add the charges: \(e + e + 2e = 4e\).

\[ V = \frac{k(4e)}{r} = \frac{(8.99\times10^9)(4)(1.6\times10^{-19})}{4.24\times10^{-6}} = 1.36\times10^{-3}\ \text{V} \]
Answer: \(1.36\times10^{-3}\) V. (The fields of 1 and 2 cancelled at P; their potentials don't.)

P12 — Charges on an arc On the syllabus

Problem: Three protons and two electrons sit on a circular arc of radius r = 2.00 cm at the angles shown. What is the magnitude of the net potential at the centre of the arc?

Sheet figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • 3 protons (+e each), 2 electrons (−e each)
  • every charge is \(r = 0.0200\) m from the centre
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

P13 — Work along a field line Practice

Problem: An electron moves from A to B along a field line; the field does \(3.94\times10^{-19}\) J of work on it. Find \(V_B-V_A\), \(V_C-V_A\) and \(V_C-V_B\).

Sheet figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(W = +3.94\times10^{-19}\) J (by the field)
  • \(q = -e = -1.6\times10^{-19}\) C
  • B and C lie on the same equipotential
📐 Formula
\[ W = -\Delta U = -q\,\Delta V\ \Longrightarrow\ \Delta V = -\frac{W}{q} \]

potential difference from the field's work

\[ V = \text{same everywhere on an equipotential} \]

Step 1. (i)

\[ V_B - V_A = -\frac{W}{q} = -\frac{3.94\times10^{-19}}{-1.6\times10^{-19}} = +2.46\ \text{V} \]

Step 2. (ii) C is on B's equipotential, so \(V_C = V_B\):

\[ V_C - V_A = V_B - V_A = +2.46\ \text{V} \]

Step 3. (iii)

\[ V_C - V_B = 0 \]
The sheet says “two electron”, almost certainly a typo for “an electron”. If it really is charge −2e, halve the answers: 1.23 V.
Answer: (i) +2.46 V; (ii) +2.46 V; (iii) 0.

P14 — Work to bring Q near two charges Practice

Problem: How much work must we do to bring Q = +16e from infinity along the dashed line to the point shown, 2.00d from \(q_1 = +4e\) and d from \(q_2 = -q_1/3\)? d = 1.40 cm, θ₁ = 43°, θ₂ = 60°.

Sheet figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • \(Q = 16e\), \(q_1 = +4e\) at \(2d\), \(q_2 = -\tfrac43 e\) at \(d\)
  • \(d = 0.0140\) m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\), \(e = 1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

\[ W = QV \]

work by us = Q × (potential at the end point); the path and the angles don't matter

Step 1. Potential at the end point:

\[ V = k\left(\frac{4e}{2d} - \frac{4e/3}{d}\right) = \frac{ke}{d}\left(2 - \frac43\right) = \frac{2ke}{3d} \]

Step 2. Work:

\[ W = QV = (16e)\frac{2ke}{3d} = \frac{32ke^2}{3d} = \frac{32(8.99\times10^9)(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{3(0.0140)} = 1.75\times10^{-25}\ \text{J} \]
Answer: \(1.75\times10^{-25}\) J.

P17 — Rectangular array On the syllabus

Problem: A rectangular array of charges (multiples of \(q_1 = 3.40\) pC and \(q_2 = 6.00\) pC) with a = 39.0 cm, as shown. With V = 0 at infinity, what is the net potential at the rectangle's centre?

Sheet figure.

📝 Solution
Given
  • corners: \(+2q_1,\ -3q_1,\ -q_1,\ +2q_1\)
  • middles of the long sides: \(+4q_2,\ +4q_2\)
  • rectangle \(2a \times a\), \(a = 0.390\) m
  • \(k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2\)
📐 Formula
\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} = k\left(\frac{q_1}{r_1} + \frac{q_2}{r_2} + \cdots\right) \]

Potential is a scalar: put each charge in with its sign and add. No components, no angles.

Step 1. Distances from the centre C:

\[ \text{middles: } r = \frac a2,\qquad \text{corners: } r = \sqrt{a^2 + \left(\tfrac a2\right)^2} = \frac{\sqrt5}{2}a \]

Step 2. Corners — all at the same distance, so add their charges:

\[ 2q_1 - 3q_1 - q_1 + 2q_1 = 0\ \Longrightarrow\ V_{corners} = 0 \]

Step 3. The two middle charges:

\[ V = 2\cdot\frac{k(4q_2)}{a/2} = \frac{16kq_2}{a} = \frac{16(8.99\times10^9)(6.00\times10^{-12})}{0.390} = 2.21\ \text{V} \]

G1 — Equipotential surfaces and field lines Graph

Draw the equipotentials (dashed or gold) and field lines (arrowed) for a point charge, a uniform field, a dipole and two equal positive charges.

Rules to show in your drawing:

  • Equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines wherever they cross.
  • Field lines point from high V to low V (out of +, into −).
  • Equipotentials are closer together where the field is stronger (near the charges).
  • No work is done moving a charge along an equipotential (ΔV = 0, so W = qΔV = 0).
  • Point charge: concentric circles (spheres). Uniform field: parallel planes. Dipole: the perpendicular bisector is the V = 0 surface.

G2 — V and E against distance for a point charge Graph

  • \(V \propto 1/r\): double r → V halves.
  • \(E \propto 1/r^2\): double r → E falls to a quarter, so E drops off faster.
  • Both approach zero but never touch the axis. For a negative charge, V is drawn below the axis.

G3 — V along the line through two charges Graph

  • Dipole: V goes from +∞ (at +q) to −∞ (at −q), crossing zero at the midpoint — V = 0 but E ≠ 0 there (slide P3, PPS 10).
  • Two equal + charges: V dips to a minimum at the midpoint but stays positive — E = 0 but V ≠ 0 there (PPS 18).

G4 — Uniform field: V against distance Graph

  • \(\Delta V = -Ed\): a straight line sloping down along the field (slide P1, PPS 6).
  • E is constant, so its graph is a horizontal line.

G5 — Potential energy of two charges against separation Graph

  • \(U = kq_1q_2/r\): positive and rising for like charges, negative for unlike charges.
  • Closest approach: draw the total kinetic energy as a horizontal line; where it meets the U curve is \(r_{min}\) (slide P4, PPS 8, 9).
