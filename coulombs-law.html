⚡ Coulomb's Law & Electric Charge

PHY-2105 • Lecture on Electricity — Introduction to Electricity: Coulomb's Law

1. What is Charge?

Electrostatics is the study of electricity when charges are not in motion. We only know charge exists because, in experiments, electric forces cause objects to move.

The three particles

ParticleChargeMassSize
Electron\( -e = -1.6\times10^{-19} \) C\( m_e = 9.11\times10^{-31} \) kgpoint object, radius < \(10^{-18}\) m
Proton\( +e = +1.6\times10^{-19} \) C\( m_p = 1.67\times10^{-27} \) kgfinite, \( \approx 0.88\times10^{-15} \) m
Neutron0 (net)\( \approx m_p \)similar to proton

The neutron has zero total charge, but positive and negative charges exist inside it.

Neutral, charged, and excess charge

  • All ordinary matter contains both positive and negative charge.
  • An object is electrically neutral when it has equal amounts of both.
  • Objects can lose or gain charge. The net charge is also called excess charge.
  • A tiny imbalance in either type of charge is the cause of static electricity.
  • The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C). Always write the sign with the quantity.

Conservation of charge

Rubbing does not create charge — it is transferred from one object to another. Charge is conserved in every process:

  • Teflon becomes negative, silk positive; acrylic positive, silk negative
  • Pair production: \( 0 = e^+ + e^- \)
  • Radioactive decay: \( ^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow\ ^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \)
  • High-energy reaction: \( e^- + p^+ \rightarrow e^- + p^+ + n^0 \)

Quantization of charge

Discovered in 1911 by Robert A. Millikan in the oil-drop experiment. Charge comes in indivisible lumps of \( e \); the unit is so tiny we never notice the graininess.

\[ Q = N e \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N = \frac{Q}{e} \]

3. Methods of Charging

Normally atoms sit in the lowest energy state with equal electrons and protons, so material is neutral. There are three ways to change that:

MethodWhat happens
FrictionTwo neutral objects are rubbed; charge transfers from one to the other and they become oppositely charged, so they attract each other.
Contact (conduction)A charged object touches another; charge flows across and both end up with the same sign.
InductionA charged object is brought near without touching; charges inside rearrange. Can be demonstrated on spheres with an electrometer.

Key demonstration facts

  • Silk (+) on teflon (−); silk (−) on acrylic (+)
  • Wood doesn't charge
  • Charged objects always attract neutral objects
  • Not only chemical composition matters — surface structure matters too (a monolayer of molecules is involved; a quantum/nanotechnology effect)

Triboelectric series

An ordering of materials by how readily they give up electrons. Top of the list loses electrons easily and becomes positive; bottom gains electrons and becomes negative.

Positive end (lose electrons easily): air, human hands, asbestos, rabbit fur, glass, mica, acrylic, human hair, nylon, wool, steel, wood, amber, sealing wax, hard rubber, nickel, copper, brass, silver, gold, platinum, sulfur, acetate, rayon, polyester … (negative end)

The electroscope

The forces between the two kinds of charge can be observed with an electroscope. Charge can be transferred to it by conduction.

4. Conductors, Insulators & Semiconductors

All materials contain electrons. What differs is whether those electrons are free to move.

Semiconductor: has a few free electrons plus atoms with bound electrons that act as insulators — in between the two.

5. Current, Voltage & Power

Current

Current is the movement of electric charge through a substance.

\[ I = \frac{q}{t} \qquad\text{(amps = coulombs/second)} \]

More generally, current is the slope of the charge–time graph, and charge is the area under the current–time graph:

\[ i = \frac{dq}{dt}, \qquad Q = \int i\,dt \]

One ampere corresponds to \( 6.242\times10^{18} \) electrons per second, since \( (6.242\times10^{18})(1.602\times10^{-19}) = 1 \) C/s.

⚠️ Direction convention

The direction of current was historically defined as the direction positive charges move. Both positive and negative charges can carry current:

  • In conductive liquids (salt water) both signs carry current
  • In solid metal conductors only electrons move, so current is carried by the flow of negative electrons — which is why conventional current points opposite to the electron flow

AC and DC

  • DC — constant value
  • AC — changes sinusoidally in both value and direction, so no net charge is transferred

Voltage

A voltage source provides the energy (work) required to produce a current.

\[ V = \frac{dW}{dQ} \qquad\text{(volts = joules/coulomb)} \]
  • Voltage measures the potential energy that drives current through a transducer
  • It is always measured as a difference with respect to an arbitrary common point called ground
  • Outside engineering it is also called electromotive force (EMF)

Power

The rate at which energy is transferred from a source or used by a passive device:

\[ P = \frac{dW}{dt} = V\cdot I = \frac{dW}{dQ}\cdot\frac{dQ}{dt} \qquad\text{(watts = volts × amps)} \]
\[ W = \int P\,dt \qquad\text{(area under the power–time graph)} \]

The river analogy (how to think about circuits)

  • Voltage — the force pushing current round the circuit (gravity)
  • Resistance — friction impeding the flow (rocks in the river)
  • Current — the actual substance flowing through the wires (electrons)

The central concept is the closed circuit — current must have a complete path back to the source. Current flows from the higher-voltage terminal of the source into the higher-voltage terminal of the transducer before returning. The source expends energy and the transducer converts it into something useful.

6. Coulomb's Law

In 1785 Charles Augustin Coulomb reported to the Royal Academy that, using a torsion balance, two charged mulberry-pith balls repelled each other with a force inversely proportional to the square of the distance.

\[ F = \frac{k\,q_1q_2}{r^2}, \qquad k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2 \;\approx\; 10^{10}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2 \]

The slides use the rounded value \( k \approx 10^{10} \) for quick estimates. You may also see it written with the permittivity of free space:

\[ k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/\text{N·m}^2 \]
ChargesForce
+ and +Repulsion
+ and −Attraction
− and −Repulsion

Spheres behave as points

For uniformly charged metal spheres, the force is the same as if all the charge sat at the centre — so you may treat them as point charges separated by their centre-to-centre distance:

\[ F = \frac{kq^2}{r^2} \]

If instead the separation is measured between the near surfaces of two spheres of radius \(R\), the centre-to-centre distance is \( r + 2R \) and the force becomes \( F = \dfrac{kq^2}{(r+2R)^2} \).

Principle of superposition

The force between any two charges is the same as if all the others were not there. The total force on a charge is the vector sum of the individual forces:

\[ \vec{F}_{net} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \vec{F}_3 + \cdots \]

⚠️ Vector sum, not scalar sum

Add forces along a line with signs; add forces at angles by resolving into \(x\) and \(y\) components. Never just add the magnitudes unless the forces are genuinely parallel.

7. Worked Examples from the Slides

Example-1 — Quantization

We charge an object with a nanocoulomb (\(10^{-9}\) C). How many elementary units of charge is this?

\[ N = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{10^{-9}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} \approx 6\times10^{9} \]

Answer: about 6 billion elementary charges (6 billion electrons).

Example-2 — Two positive charges

Force between two charges of 1 nC each, 1 cm apart. Why is it so weak?

\[ F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{(10^{10})(10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{(10^{-2})^2} = 10^{-4}\ \text{N} \quad \text{(repulsion)} \]

That is equivalent to the weight of a mass of \(10^{-5}\) kg = 10 mg — about a long strand of hair. It is weak because a nanocoulomb is a tiny amount of charge.

Example-3 — Two pennies without electrons

Two 3 g copper pennies 1 m apart, with all electrons removed.

Copper has 29 protons per atom and molar mass 63.5 g/mol, so a 3 g penny holds

\[ N_{atoms} = \frac{3}{63.5}(6\times10^{23}) \approx 2.8\times10^{22} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q = 29 N_{atoms}\,e \approx 1.4\times10^{5}\ \text{C} \]
\[ F = \frac{kq^2}{r^2} = \frac{(10^{10})(1.4\times10^{5})^2}{1^2} \approx 2\times10^{20}\ \text{N} \]

An absurdly large force — which is exactly the point: matter is neutral to extraordinary precision.

Example-4 — Three charges in a line (superposition)

\( q_1 = +25 \) nC at the origin, \( q_2 = -12 \) nC at \( x = 2 \) m, \( q_3 = +18 \) nC at \( x = 3 \) m. What is the net force on \( q_1 \)?

Take \(+x\) as positive. \( q_2 \) is opposite in sign so it attracts \( q_1 \) toward \(+x\); \( q_3 \) has the same sign so it repels \( q_1 \) toward \(-x\).

\[ F = k q_1\left[\frac{|q_2|}{(2)^2} - \frac{q_3}{(3)^2}\right] \]
\[ F = (10^{10})(25\times10^{-9})\left[\frac{12\times10^{-9}}{4} - \frac{18\times10^{-9}}{9}\right] = +2.5\times10^{-7}\ \text{N} \]

Answer: \( 2.5\times10^{-7} \) N in the \(+x\) direction (using \( k = 8.99\times10^{9} \) gives \( 2.2\times10^{-7} \) N).

Example-5 — Three point charges in 2-D ★ the important one

\( q_1 = +1 \) nC at the origin, \( q_2 = +1 \) nC at 1 cm along \(+x\), \( q_3 = -2 \) nC positioned 1 cm across and 2 cm up (so \( r_{13} = \sqrt{5} \) cm). Find the net force on \( q_1 \).

Step 1 — force from \( q_2 \) (repulsive, along the \(x\) axis only):

\[ F_{12} = \frac{(10^{10})(10^{-9})^2}{(10^{-2})^2} = 1\times10^{-4}\ \text{N}, \qquad F_{12y}=0 \]

Step 2 — force from \( q_3 \) (attractive, at an angle):

\[ F_{13} = \frac{(10^{10})(2\times10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{(\sqrt5\times10^{-2})^2} = 0.40\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]

Resolve it using the 1 cm : 2 cm : \(\sqrt5\) cm triangle, so \( \cos\theta = 1/\sqrt5 \), \( \sin\theta = 2/\sqrt5 \):

\[ F_{13x} = 0.179\times10^{-4}\ \text{N}, \qquad F_{13y} = 0.358\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]

Step 3 — add components (the two \(x\) contributions oppose each other):

\[ F_{x,net} = (1 - 0.179)\times10^{-4} = 0.821\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{y,net} = (0 + 0.358)\times10^{-4} = 0.358\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]

Step 4 — magnitude and direction:

\[ F_{net} = \sqrt{(0.821)^2 + (0.358)^2}\times10^{-4} = \sqrt{0.802}\times10^{-4} = 0.896\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]
\[ \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{0.358}{0.821}\right) = 23.6^\circ \ \text{from the } -x \text{ axis} \]

⚠️ The slide has an arithmetic slip here

The slide prints \( F_{net} = 0.802\times10^{-4} \) N — but \( 0.802 \) is the value of \( (0.821)^2 + (0.358)^2 \), i.e. the sum of squares before taking the square root. The correct magnitude is \( \sqrt{0.802} = 0.896 \), so \( F_{net} = 0.896\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \approx 0.90\times10^{-4} \) N.

The angle \( 23.6^\circ \) on the slide is correct. Show your working for the square root and you are safe either way.

Example-6 — Electric force vs gravity

In an atom, can we neglect the gravitational force between electrons and protons? Find the ratio of the Coulomb force to the gravitational force for two electrons a distance \(r\) apart.

\[ F_c = \frac{ke^2}{r^2}, \qquad F_g = \frac{Gm^2}{r^2} \]

The \( r^2 \) cancels — the ratio does not depend on the separation at all:

\[ \frac{F_c}{F_g} = \frac{ke^2}{Gm_e^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{(6.67\times10^{-11})(9.11\times10^{-31})^2} \approx 4\times10^{42} \]

Answer: the electric force is about \( 10^{42} \) times stronger, so yes — gravity is completely negligible inside an atom.
