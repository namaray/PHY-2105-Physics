1. Charge & Coulomb's Law — notes: coulombs-law.html

Coulomb's law

\[ F = \frac{k|q_1q_2|}{r^2} \]

Like charges repel, unlike attract. Force acts along the line joining them.

Vector form

\[ \vec F_{12} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}\,\hat r_{12} \]

\(\hat r\) points from the source to the charge acted on.

Superposition

\[ \vec F_{net} = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \cdots \]

Vector sum: resolve, add components, recombine.

Resultant

\[ F = \sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2},\ \ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{F_y}{F_x} \]

Check the quadrant from the signs.

Quantization

\[ q = ne,\quad n = \frac{q}{e} \]

Charge comes in whole multiples of e.

Charge in a sample

\[ q = \frac{m}{M}N_A\,Z\,e \]

M = molar mass, Z = electrons (protons) per atom/unit.

Spheres (surface gap r)

\[ F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{(r + 2R)^2} \]

Always use centre-to-centre distance.

Electric vs gravity

\[ \frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{Gm_1m_2} \]

r cancels. Two electrons: \(4\times10^{42}\); e–p: \(2\times10^{39}\).

Newton's 2nd law

\[ a = \frac{F}{m} \]

"Acceleration as they separate".

Current

\[ I = \frac{dq}{dt},\quad Q = \int i\,dt \]

Q = area under the i–t graph.

Voltage

\[ V = \frac{dW}{dq} \]

1 V = 1 J/C.

Power

\[ P = VI = \frac{dW}{dt},\quad W = \int P\,dt \]

Energy = area under the P–t graph.

Key facts (short questions)
  • Two kinds of charge; like repel, unlike attract. Charge is conserved and quantized (\(q = ne\)).
  • Charging: friction (rubbing transfers electrons), conduction (contact shares charge), induction (nearby charge separates charge, then ground).
  • Conductors have free electrons (metals); insulators don't (glass, rubber); semiconductors are in between (Si, Ge).
  • An object is negative if it has gained electrons, positive if it has lost them — protons don't move.
  • The electric force is enormously stronger than gravity; gravity is negligible between particles.

2. Electric Field — notes: electric-fields.html

Definition

\[ \vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q_0} \]

N/C. Direction = force on a positive test charge.

Point charge

\[ E = \frac{k|q|}{r^2} \]

Away from +, toward −.

Force from a field

\[ \vec F = q\vec E \]

Electron: force opposite to \(\vec E\).

Superposition

\[ \vec E = \sum \vec E_i \]

Vector sum — components.

Two equal fields at angle φ

\[ E_{net} = 2E\cos\frac{\varphi}{2} \]

60° → \(\sqrt3E\); 90° → \(\sqrt2E\); 120° → \(E\).

Null point (like charges)

\[ \frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{q_2}{(d-x)^2} \]

Between them, nearer the smaller one. Square-root both sides. (Unlike charges: outside, beyond the smaller.)

Acceleration in a field

\[ a = \frac{|q|E}{m} \]

Then \(v = v_0 + at\), \(x = v_0t + \tfrac12at^2\).

Line charge element

\[ dq = \lambda\,ds,\quad dE = \frac{k\,dq}{r^2} \]

λ = charge per length (C/m).

Charged ring (axis)

\[ E = \frac{kqz}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}} \]

0 at the centre; \(\to kq/z^2\) far away.

Dipole moment

\[ p = qd \]

Points from − to +. C·m. Molecule (slide method): \(d = p/Ne\).

Dipole field (far, on axis)

\[ E = \frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{z^3} = \frac{2kp}{z^3} \]

Falls as \(1/z^3\). On the bisector: \(kp/r^3\).

Torque on a dipole

\[ \tau = pE\sin\theta,\quad \vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E \]

Max \(pE\) at 90°; zero at 0° and 180°.

Dipole potential energy

\[ U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p\cdot\vec E \]

\(-pE\) at 0° (stable), \(+pE\) at 180° (unstable).

Work rotating a dipole

\[ W_{field} = -\Delta U,\quad W_{agent} = U_f - U_i \]

0°→180°: \(2pE\); 0°→θ: \(pE(1-\cos\theta)\).

Cross product

\[ \vec a\times\vec b = \begin{vmatrix}\hat i&\hat j&\hat k\\a_x&a_y&a_z\\b_x&b_y&b_z\end{vmatrix} \]

Don't drop the minus on the ĵ term.

Field-line rules (short questions + drawings)
  • Tangent to a line = direction of \(\vec E\); line density ∝ \(E\) (close lines = strong field).
  • Lines start on + and end on − (or at infinity); they never cross (E has one direction at a point).
  • Number of lines ∝ charge (draw twice as many for 2q). Introduced by Faraday.
  • Sphere: radial. Large sheet: parallel, uniform. Dipole: curve from + to −. Two like charges: repel, with a null point between.
  • Dipole in a uniform field: no net force (equal, opposite), but a torque that turns \(\vec p\) toward \(\vec E\). Microwave ovens rotate water dipoles.

3. Electric Potential — notes: electric-potential.html

Potential energy change

\[ \Delta U = U_f - U_i = -W \]

W = work by the field. Field does + work → U falls.

Potential

\[ V = \frac{U}{q} \]

Volt = J/C. A scalar — add with signs.

Potential difference

\[ \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} = -\frac{W}{q} \]

Keep the sign of q (electron: q = −e).

Work to bring q from ∞

\[ W_{ext} = qV \]

Path doesn't matter.

From the field

\[ V_f - V_i = -\int_i^f \vec E\cdot d\vec s \]

V falls along \(\vec E\).

Uniform field

\[ \Delta V = -Ed \]

Magnitude \(V = Ed\).

Point charge

\[ V = \frac{kq}{r} \]

Sign of q stays in. 1/r, not 1/r².

Group of charges

\[ V = \sum \frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]

No components, no angles.

Equal distances

\[ V = \frac{k\sum q_i}{r} \]

Square centre, arc centre, ring axis.

Point-charge shortcut

\[ V = Er \]

From \(E = kq/r^2\), \(V = kq/r\).

Dipole (far)

\[ V = \frac{kp\cos\theta}{r^2} \]

θ from the axis; 0 on the bisector.

Continuous charge

\[ V = \int \frac{k\,dq}{r} \]

Still a scalar sum.

Gradient

\[ E_s = -\frac{dV}{ds} \]

E points where V falls fastest.

Pair energy

\[ U = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r} \]

Like: + ; unlike: −.

System energy

\[ U = \sum_{pairs}\frac{kq_iq_j}{r_{ij}} \]

3 charges → 3 pairs; 4 → 6.

Closest approach

\[ K_{initial} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r_{min}} \]

Two movers: add both K's (slide method).

Electron-volt

\[ \Delta U\,[\text{eV}] = (q/e)\,\Delta V \]

J ÷ \(1.6\times10^{-19}\) → eV.

Energy conservation

\[ K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f \]

Speed from a potential drop: \(\tfrac12mv^2 = |q\Delta V|\).

Equipotentials (short questions + drawings)
  • Equipotential = surface of equal V. Moving along it: \(\Delta V = 0\), so no work.
  • Always perpendicular to field lines; closer together where E is stronger.
  • Point charge: concentric spheres. Uniform field: parallel planes. Dipole: the bisector is the V = 0 surface.
  • V can be 0 where E ≠ 0 (midway in a dipole); E can be 0 where V ≠ 0 (midway between equal + charges).

5. Direction Rules — "Which Way?"

  • Force: like charges push apart (force on a charge points away from the other); unlike pull together (toward the other).
  • Field: points away from a + source and toward a − source — regardless of what's at the point.
  • Negative test charge: its force is opposite to \(\vec E\). So an electron moves against the field, toward higher V.
  • Potential: falls along \(\vec E\). A + charge moves toward lower V, a − charge toward higher V; both lose U.
  • Dipole: \(\vec p\) points − → +. On its axis \(\vec E\) is along \(\vec p\); on the bisector \(\vec E\) is opposite to \(\vec p\). Torque turns \(\vec p\) toward \(\vec E\).
  • Symmetry: equal charges at equal distances on opposite sides cancel (field/force). For potential nothing cancels by direction — only by sign.
  • Angle convention: give θ from +x (0–360°), or "x° above the −x axis". Check the quadrant from the signs of the components.

8. One-Line Definitions

Electric fieldForce per unit positive test charge at a point, \(\vec E = \vec F/q_0\).
Electric dipoleEqual and opposite charges ±q a distance d apart; moment \(p = qd\), from − to +.
Electric potentialWork to bring a unit positive charge from the reference point (∞) to the point without acceleration; \(V = U/q\).
Potential energyEnergy of a configuration of charges from the conservative Coulomb force; \(\Delta U = -W\).
Equipotential surfaceAdjacent points with the same V; no work is done moving along it.
Electron-voltEnergy gained by charge e through 1 V: \(1.6\times10^{-19}\) J.
Linear charge densityCharge per unit length λ (C/m).
Quantization / conservationq = ne; the total charge of an isolated system never changes.

10. Traps & Known Slide Errors

Easy marks people lose

  • Formula line first, then numbers — method marks survive arithmetic slips.
  • Draw the arrows before calculating. Most wrong answers are direction errors.
  • Vectors for F and E, scalars for V and U. Never resolve a potential; never add field magnitudes at an angle.
  • r vs r²: V and U use r; F and E use r².
  • Signs: use |q| for force/field sizes and get direction from the picture; keep the sign in V and U.
  • Electrons: force opposite to E; \(\Delta V = \Delta U/q\) with q = −e.
  • Agent vs field work: \(W_{agent} = +\Delta U\), \(W_{field} = -\Delta U\).
  • Units: cm → m, μ/n/p/f prefixes, fm, eV ↔ J.
  • State assumptions when a question is ambiguous (which corner, which axis, which value of k).

⚠️ Slide answers that are wrong — know both

  • Coulomb's Law Example-5: slide prints \(0.802\times10^{-4}\) N — that's \(F^2\); the force is \(\sqrt{0.802} = 0.896\times10^{-4}\) N.
  • Electric Potential Problem-1: slide prints ΔV = 45 kV; \(1.2\times10^{-14}/1.6\times10^{-19} = 7.5\times10^4\) V (exactly \(Ed = 7.8\times10^4\) V unrounded).
  • Closest approach (proton + alpha): slide method gives \(4ke^2/5mv^2\); the momentum-correct value is \(5ke^2/4mv^2\). Use the slide's in the exam.
