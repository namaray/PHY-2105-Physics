A2 — Core formulas

1. Coulomb's Law

\[ F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} \]

Like charges repel, unlike attract.

2. Coulomb constant

\[ k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \]

3. Quantization

\[ Q = Ne \;\Longrightarrow\; N = \frac{Q}{e} \]

4. Superposition

\[ \vec F_{net} = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \cdots \]

Vector sum — resolve into components.

5. Components & resultant

\[ F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2},\quad \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{F_y}{F_x} \]

6. Two spheres of radius R

\[ F = \frac{kq^2}{(r+2R)^2} \]

If \(r\) is surface-to-surface. Centre-to-centre → use \(r\) directly.

7. Current

\[ I = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{dq}{dt} \]

8. Charge from a current graph

\[ Q = \int i\,dt \]

= area under the \(i\)–\(t\) graph. ← graph question

9. Voltage

\[ V = \frac{dW}{dQ} \]

volts = joules/coulomb

10. Power

\[ P = VI = \frac{dW}{dt},\quad W=\int P\,dt \]

11. Gravity (for comparison problems)

\[ F_g = \frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2} \]

12. Force ratio

\[ \frac{F_c}{F_g} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{Gm_1m_2} \]

\(r^2\) cancels — independent of distance.

13. Charge on a sample of matter

\[ q = \left(\frac{m}{M}N_A\right)\!Z\,e \]

\(m\)=mass, \(M\)=molar mass, \(Z\)=protons/atom.

14. Newton's second law (pairs with Coulomb)

\[ a = \frac{F}{m} \]

"find their acceleration as they separate".

🧮 B. The Six Slide Examples — Fully Worked

These are Examples 1–6 from Coulombs law_b.pdf. Example-5 is the most likely to appear in some form — it is the only 2-D vector one.

Example-1 — Quantization  Easy

Charge an object with 1 nC. How many elementary charges?

\[ N = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{10^{-9}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} \approx 6\times10^{9} \]

Ans: ≈ 6 billion electrons.

Example-2 — Two positive charges  Easy

Two charges of 1 nC each, 1 cm apart.

\[ F = \frac{(10^{10})(10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{(10^{-2})^2} = 10^{-4}\ \text{N (repulsion)} \]

Equivalent to the weight of ~10 mg. Why so weak? Because a nanocoulomb is a minute amount of charge — \(k\) is huge, but \(q^2\) is far smaller.

Example-3 — Two pennies stripped of electrons  Medium

Two 3 g copper pennies, 1 m apart, all electrons removed. (i) Force? (ii) Acceleration as they separate?

Cu: \(Z = 29\), \(M = 63.5\) g/mol.

\[ q = \frac{3}{63.5}(6\times10^{23})(29)(1.6\times10^{-19}) \approx 1.4\times10^{5}\ \text{C} \]
\[ F = \frac{(10^{10})(1.4\times10^{5})^2}{1^2} \approx 2\times10^{20}\ \text{N} \]
\[ a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{2\times10^{20}}{3\times10^{-3}} \approx 7\times10^{22}\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

Example-4 — Three charges in a line  Likely

\( q_1=+25 \) nC at 0, \( q_2=-12 \) nC at \(x=2\) m, \( q_3=+18 \) nC at \(x=3\) m. Net force on \(q_1\)?

\(q_2\) is opposite → attracts \(q_1\) toward \(+x\). \(q_3\) is like → repels toward \(-x\).

\[ F = kq_1\!\left[\frac{|q_2|}{2^2} - \frac{q_3}{3^2}\right] = (10^{10})(25\times10^{-9})\!\left[\frac{12\times10^{-9}}{4}-\frac{18\times10^{-9}}{9}\right] \]

Ans: \( +2.5\times10^{-7} \) N along \(+x\)  (\(2.2\times10^{-7}\) N if you use \(k=8.99\times10^9\)).

Example-5 — Three charges in 2-D  Method to know

\( q_1=+1 \) nC at origin, \( q_2=+1 \) nC at 1 cm along \(+x\), \( q_3=-2 \) nC at 1 cm across and 2 cm up (so \( r_{13}=\sqrt5 \) cm). Net force on \( q_1 \) and its direction?

1. Force from \(q_2\) — repulsive, purely along \(x\):

\[ F_{12} = \frac{(10^{10})(10^{-9})^2}{(10^{-2})^2} = 1\times10^{-4}\,\text{N},\qquad F_{12y}=0 \]

2. Force from \(q_3\) — attractive, at an angle:

\[ F_{13} = \frac{(10^{10})(2\times10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{(\sqrt5\times10^{-2})^2} = 0.40\times10^{-4}\,\text{N} \]

With the 1 : 2 : \(\sqrt5\) triangle, \( \cos\theta = 1/\sqrt5 \), \( \sin\theta = 2/\sqrt5 \):

\[ F_{13x} = 0.179\times10^{-4}\,\text{N},\qquad F_{13y} = 0.358\times10^{-4}\,\text{N} \]

3. Add components (the \(x\) parts oppose):

\[ F_{x} = (1-0.179)\times10^{-4} = 0.821\times10^{-4}\,\text{N} \]
\[ F_{y} = 0.358\times10^{-4}\,\text{N} \]

4. Resultant:

\[ F_{net} = \sqrt{(0.821)^2+(0.358)^2}\times10^{-4} = \sqrt{0.802}\times10^{-4} = 0.896\times10^{-4}\,\text{N} \]
\[ \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\frac{0.358}{0.821} = 23.6^\circ \text{ from the } -x \text{ axis} \]

⚠️ The slide's final number is wrong — know both

The slide prints \( F_{net} = 0.802\times10^{-4} \) N, but \(0.802\) is \( (0.821)^2+(0.358)^2 \) — the square root was never taken. The correct answer is \( \sqrt{0.802} = 0.896 \), i.e. \( \mathbf{0.896\times10^{-4}\ N} \).

Write the square-root line out explicitly so your working defends itself whichever number the marker expects. The angle 23.6° on the slide is correct.

Example-6 — Electric vs gravitational force  Likely

Ratio of Coulomb force to gravity for two electrons separated by \(r\)?

\[ \frac{F_c}{F_g} = \frac{ke^2/r^2}{Gm_e^2/r^2} = \frac{ke^2}{Gm_e^2} \]
\[ = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{(6.67\times10^{-11})(9.11\times10^{-31})^2} \approx 4\times10^{42} \]

Key insight: the \(r^2\) cancels, so the ratio is independent of separation. Gravity is utterly negligible inside an atom.

Variant: electron–proton gives \( \approx 2\times10^{39} \) (use \(m_e m_p\) on the bottom).

💬 C. Short-Question Bank

20–30% of the marks. These come straight off the slides.

Q. What are the four empirical facts electrostatics is based on?

Conservation of charge; quantization of charge; Coulomb's law; the principle of superposition.

Q. State Coulomb's law. Who discovered it and how?

\( F = kq_1q_2/r^2 \). Charles Augustin Coulomb, 1785, reported to the Royal Academy using a torsion balance with two charged mulberry-pith balls, finding the force varied inversely with the square of the distance.

Q. What is meant by quantization of charge?

Charge exists only in indivisible lumps of \(e\), so \(Q = Ne\). Discovered by Robert A. Millikan in 1911 in the oil-drop experiment. The unit is so tiny we never notice the graininess.

Q. State conservation of charge with examples.

Charge is never created, only transferred. Rubbing transfers it (teflon −, silk +). Examples: \( 0 = e^+ + e^- \); \( ^{238}_{92}U \rightarrow\ ^{234}_{90}Th + ^{4}_{2}He \); \( e^- + p^+ \rightarrow e^- + p^+ + n^0 \).

Q. What is the principle of superposition?

The force between two charges is unaffected by the presence of others, so the net force on a charge is the vector sum of the individual forces.

Q. Name the three methods of charging.

Friction (rubbing — objects become oppositely charged and attract), contact/conduction (touching — same sign), and induction (bringing a charge near without touching).

Q. Distinguish conductors and insulators.

Conductors: electrons flow easily between atoms, 1–3 valence electrons (silver, copper, gold, aluminium). Insulators: flow is difficult, electrons tightly bound, 5–8 valence electrons (mica, glass, quartz). A semiconductor has a few free electrons and is in between.

Q. What is the triboelectric series?

An ordering of materials by how easily they lose electrons. Materials at the positive end lose electrons easily and charge positive; those at the negative end gain electrons.

Q. Why does a charged object attract a neutral one?

Charged objects always attract neutral objects — the nearby charge induces a rearrangement of charge in the neutral object, bringing opposite charge closer and like charge further away, so attraction wins.

Q. Define electric current and give its direction convention.

Current is the movement of charge through a substance, \( I = q/t \) (amps = C/s). Its direction was historically defined as the direction positive charges move. In metals only electrons move, so conventional current is opposite to the electron flow. In conductive liquids both signs carry current.

Q. Difference between AC and DC?

DC has a constant value. AC changes sinusoidally in value and direction, so no net charge is transferred.

Q. Define voltage and power.

\( V = dW/dQ \) — joules per coulomb; the potential energy per unit charge that drives current. Always measured as a difference relative to ground. Also called EMF. \( P = VI = dW/dt \), in watts.

Q. Why can spheres be treated as point charges?

For uniformly charged spheres the force is the same as if all the charge were concentrated at the centre, so use the centre-to-centre distance.

Q. What is electrostatics?

The study of electricity when charges are not in motion. We know charge exists only because electric forces cause objects to move in experiments.

Q. Why is an object electrically neutral, and what is excess charge?

Neutral means equal amounts of positive and negative charge. Net (excess) charge is the imbalance — a tiny imbalance causes static electricity.

⚡ E. Last-Minute Traps

Things that cost easy marks

  • Convert cm → m before squaring. 1 cm = \(10^{-2}\) m, so \(r^2 = 10^{-4}\) m². This is the single most common slip.
  • nC → C: 1 nC = \(10^{-9}\) C. Also μC = \(10^{-6}\), pC = \(10^{-12}\).
  • Forces are vectors. Put the direction on the diagram first: like charges push apart, unlike pull together. Then assign signs.
  • Don't add magnitudes at angles. Resolve into \(x\) and \(y\), add separately, then \( \sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2} \).
  • Take the square root at the end — the slide itself forgot this in Example-5.
  • State the direction as well as the magnitude when asked for a net force.
  • \(k\) vs \(1/4\pi\varepsilon_0\) are the same thing. Slides use \(k \approx 10^{10}\) for speed; \(8.99\times10^9\) is exact. Say which you used.
  • In the force ratio, \(r^2\) cancels — don't waste time hunting for a separation.
  • Sphere problems: if the gap is surface-to-surface, the centre-to-centre distance is \( r + 2R \).
