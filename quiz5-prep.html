A2 — Energy and potential

1. Potential energy and work

\[ \Delta U = U_f - U_i = -W \]

\(W\) = work done by the field. Field does + work → U falls.

2. Definition of potential

\[ V = \frac{U}{q} \]

Unit volt = J/C. A scalar.

3. Potential difference

\[ \Delta V = \frac{\Delta U}{q} = -\frac{W}{q} \]

Keep the sign of \(q\) — electrons flip it.

4. Work to bring a charge from ∞

\[ W_{ext} = qV \]

Path doesn't matter — only the end point.

5. Electron-volt

\[ \Delta U\ [\text{eV}] = (q\ \text{in } e)\times(\Delta V\ \text{in V}) \]

Divide joules by \(1.6\times10^{-19}\) to get eV.

6. Uniform field

\[ \Delta V = -Ed \]

V falls along the field. (General: \( V_f - V_i = -\int \vec E\cdot d\vec s \).)

A3 — Potential of charges

7. Point charge

\[ V = \frac{kq}{r} \]

Sign of q stays in. V = 0 at infinity.

8. Group of charges

\[ V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i} \]

Plain sum with signs — no components, no angles.

9. Same distance shortcut

\[ V = \frac{k\,(\sum q_i)}{r} \]

Square centre (\(r = a/\sqrt2\)), arc centre, ring axis.

10. Field ↔ potential (point charge)

\[ V = Er \]

From \(E = kq/r^2\) and \(V = kq/r\).

11. Dipole (far)

\[ V = \frac{kp\cos\theta}{r^2},\quad p = qd \]

θ from the axis. On the bisector (90°) V = 0.

12. Continuous charge

\[ V = \int \frac{k\,dq}{r} \]

Recognise it; integrals are unlikely.

A4 — Systems of charges

13. One pair

\[ U = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r} \]

Like → +, unlike → −.

14. Three or more charges

\[ U = \sum_{\text{pairs}} \frac{kq_iq_j}{r_{ij}} \]

3 charges → 3 pairs; 4 charges → 6 pairs.

15. Closest approach

\[ K_{initial} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r_{min}} \]

Two moving particles: add both K's (slide P4, PPS 8).

16. Gradient (excluded)

\[ E_s = -\frac{dV}{ds} \]

💬 B. Short-Question Bank

20–30% of the marks. All of these come from the slides.

Q. Define electric potential. What is its unit?

The work needed to move a unit positive charge from a reference point (usually Earth or infinity) to a specific point in the field without producing any acceleration. Equivalently, potential energy per unit charge, \(V = U/q\). Unit: volt (1 V = 1 J/C).

Q. Define electric potential energy.

The potential energy (in joules) that results from conservative Coulomb forces and is associated with the configuration of a particular set of point charges in a system. When the configuration changes, \(\Delta U = -W\), where \(W\) is the work done by the electrostatic force.

Q. Distinguish between electric potential and electric potential energy.

Potential is a property of the field at a point (J/C = V) and doesn't depend on any test charge. Potential energy belongs to a system of charges (J) and depends on the charge placed there: \(U = qV\). Dividing the charge out of U leaves V, "a property of the electric field itself".

Q. Is potential a scalar or a vector? Why does it matter?

A scalar. The potential of several charges is a plain sum with signs, \(V = \sum kq_i/r_i\) — no components and no directions. That's why the angles in PPS-5 #12 don't matter.

Q. Can V be zero where E is not? Can E be zero where V is not?

Yes to both. Midway between \(+q\) and \(-q\): \(V = 0\) but \(E \ne 0\) (slide P3, PPS 10). Midway between two equal positive charges: \(E = 0\) but \(V = 2kq/r \ne 0\) (PPS 18).

Q. What is an equipotential surface?

A surface formed by adjacent points that all have the same potential. It can be imaginary or a real, physical surface.

Q. Why is no work done moving a charge along an equipotential surface?

\(\Delta V = 0\) between any two points on it, so \(W = -q\Delta V = 0\).

Q. Why are field lines perpendicular to equipotential surfaces?

If \(\vec E\) had a component along the surface, moving a charge along the surface would take work — contradicting \(\Delta V = 0\). So \(\vec E\) has no component along it: field lines cross equipotentials at 90°.

Q. Describe the equipotentials of a point charge, a uniform field and a dipole.

Point charge: concentric spheres (circles in a drawing). Uniform field: parallel planes perpendicular to the field. Dipole: closed loops around each charge, with the perpendicular bisector as the \(V = 0\) surface.

Q. Which way does potential change along a field line?

It decreases in the direction of \(\vec E\). A positive charge moves toward lower V; a negative charge (like an electron) moves toward higher V.

Q. What is an electron-volt?

The energy gained by one elementary charge moving through 1 V: \(1\ \text{eV} = 1.6\times10^{-19}\) J.

Q. How do V and E of a point charge fall off with distance?

\(V \propto 1/r\), \(E \propto 1/r^2\). Double the distance and V halves, while E drops to a quarter.

Q. What does a negative potential energy of a system mean?

The system is bound: an external agent must do positive work to pull the charges apart to infinity (slide P6: −17 mJ).

⚡ E. Last-Minute Traps

Things that cost easy marks

  • Write the formula first, then substitute. It earns method marks even if the arithmetic slips.
  • Keep the sign of every charge in V and U. Negative charges give negative potential.
  • Don't resolve potentials into components. V is a scalar; angles don't enter \(V = \sum kq_i/r_i\).
  • V uses r, not r². Writing \(kq/r^2\) gives the field, not the potential.
  • Electrons flip signs: \(\Delta V = \Delta U/q\) with \(q = -e\). Moving against \(\vec E\) raises V but lowers the electron's U.
  • U of a system = one term per pair. Three charges → three terms; don't forget the third.
  • Find distances first: square centre \(a/\sqrt2\), rectangle corner \(\sqrt{a^2 + (a/2)^2}\), right-triangle hypotenuse \(a\sqrt2\).
  • Convert units: cm → m, μC/nC/pC/fC, fm → \(10^{-15}\) m, eV ↔ J.
  • State assumptions when the sheet is ambiguous (PPS 16: which corner holds the right angle).
