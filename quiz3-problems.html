SECTION 1: Slide Examples 1–6

Example-1 Slide

Problem: Suppose in a typical experiment we charge an object with a nanocoulomb of charge (\(10^{-9}\) C). How many elementary units of charge is this?

📝 Solution

Charge is quantized, so \( Q = Ne \):

\[ N = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{10^{-9}\ \text{C}}{1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}} = 6.25\times10^{9} \approx 6\times10^{9} \]
Answer: about \( 6\times10^{9} \) elementary charges — six billion electrons.

Example-2 Slide

Problem: What is the force between two positive charges of 1 nC each, 1 cm apart in a typical demonstration? Why is the force so weak here?

📝 Solution

Convert first: \( q_1 = q_2 = 10^{-9} \) C, \( r = 1\ \text{cm} = 10^{-2} \) m.

\[ F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{(10^{10})(10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{(10^{-2})^2} = \frac{10^{-8}}{10^{-4}} = 10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]

(With the exact \( k = 8.99\times10^9 \) this is \( 8.99\times10^{-5} \) N.) The charges are both positive, so it is a repulsion.

Why so weak? \( 10^{-4} \) N is the weight of a mass of about \( 10^{-5} \) kg = 10 mg — roughly a long strand of hair. Even though \( k \) is enormous, a nanocoulomb is a minute quantity of charge, and the product \( q_1q_2 = 10^{-18} \) crushes it.

Answer: \( F = 10^{-4} \) N, repulsive.

Example-3 Slide

Problem: (i) What is the force between two 3 gm pennies one meter apart if we remove all the electrons from the copper atoms? (ii) What is their acceleration as they separate?

📝 Solution

(i) First find how much charge a 3 g copper penny holds. Copper has \( Z = 29 \) protons per atom and molar mass \( M = 63.5 \) g/mol:

\[ N_{atoms} = \frac{m}{M}N_A = \frac{3}{63.5}\,(6\times10^{23}) \approx 2.8\times10^{22} \]
\[ q = N_{atoms}\,Z\,e = (2.8\times10^{22})(29)(1.6\times10^{-19}) \approx 1.4\times10^{5}\ \text{C} \]

Now apply Coulomb's law with \( r = 1 \) m:

\[ F = \frac{kq^2}{r^2} = \frac{(10^{10})(1.4\times10^{5})^2}{1^2} \approx 2\times10^{20}\ \text{N} \]

(ii) From Newton's second law, with \( m = 3\ \text{g} = 3\times10^{-3} \) kg:

\[ a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{2\times10^{20}}{3\times10^{-3}} \approx 7\times10^{22}\ \text{m/s}^2 \]
Answer: \( F \approx 2\times10^{20} \) N, \( a \approx 7\times10^{22} \) m/s². An absurd result — which is the point: matter is neutral to extraordinary precision, and stripping it is impossible.

Example-4 — Charges in a line Slide Likely

Problem: Three charges lie on the x axis: \( q_1 = +25 \) nC at the origin, \( q_2 = -12 \) nC at \( x = 2 \) m, \( q_3 = +18 \) nC at \( x = 3 \) m. What is the net force on \( q_1 \)?

📝 Solution

Step 1 — directions first, before any arithmetic. Take \(+x\) as the positive direction.

  • \( q_2 \) is negative and sits at \(+x\) → it attracts \( q_1 \) toward \(+x\)
  • \( q_3 \) is positive and sits at \(+x\) → it repels \( q_1 \) toward \(-x\)

The two forces oppose, so this is a signed 1-D sum:

\[ F = kq_1\left[\frac{|q_2|}{(2)^2} - \frac{q_3}{(3)^2}\right] \]
\[ F = (10^{10})(25\times10^{-9})\left[\frac{12\times10^{-9}}{4} - \frac{18\times10^{-9}}{9}\right] \]
\[ = (10^{10})(25\times10^{-9})\left[3\times10^{-9} - 2\times10^{-9}\right] = (250)(10^{-9}) = 2.5\times10^{-7}\ \text{N} \]

With the exact \( k = 8.99\times10^9 \) this becomes \( 2.25\times10^{-7} \) N.

Answer: \( F = 2.5\times10^{-7} \) N directed along \( +x \) (toward \( q_2 \)) — the attraction wins because \( q_2 \) is closer.

Example-5 — Three point charges in 2-D Slide ★ Most likely

Problem: \( q_1 = +1 \) nC at the origin, \( q_2 = +1 \) nC at 1 cm along \(+x\), \( q_3 = -2 \) nC placed 1 cm across and 2 cm up (so \( r_{13} = \sqrt5 \) cm). What is the net force on \( q_1 \), and in what direction?

📝 Solution

Step 1 — force from \( q_2 \). Both positive → repulsion, and since \( q_2 \) lies on the \(x\) axis this force has no \(y\) component:

\[ F_{12} = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r_{12}^2} = \frac{(10^{10})(10^{-9})(10^{-9})}{(10^{-2})^2} = 1\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{12x} = 1\times10^{-4}\ \text{N}, \qquad F_{12y} = 0 \]

Step 2 — force from \( q_3 \). Opposite signs → attraction, pulling \( q_1 \) toward \( q_3 \). With \( r_{13} = \sqrt5 \times 10^{-2} \) m so \( r_{13}^2 = 5\times10^{-4} \) m²:

\[ F_{13} = \frac{(10^{10})(2\times10^{-9})(1\times10^{-9})}{5\times10^{-4}} = 0.40\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]

Step 3 — resolve it. The 1 cm across, 2 cm up geometry gives a 1 : 2 : \( \sqrt5 \) triangle:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt5} = 0.447, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{2}{\sqrt5} = 0.894 \]
\[ F_{13x} = (0.40\times10^{-4})(0.447) = 0.179\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{13y} = (0.40\times10^{-4})(0.894) = 0.358\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]

Step 4 — add the components. The two \(x\) contributions point in opposite directions (repulsion from \( q_2 \) one way, attraction toward \( q_3 \) the other), so they subtract:

\[ F_{x,net} = (1 - 0.179)\times10^{-4} = 0.821\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{y,net} = (0 + 0.358)\times10^{-4} = 0.358\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]

Step 5 — resultant and angle.

\[ F_{net} = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} = \sqrt{(0.821)^2 + (0.358)^2}\times10^{-4} \]
\[ = \sqrt{0.674 + 0.128}\times10^{-4} = \sqrt{0.802}\times10^{-4} = 0.896\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \]
\[ \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{F_y}{F_x}\right) = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{0.358}{0.821}\right) = 23.6^\circ \]
Answer: \( F_{net} = 0.896\times10^{-4}\ \text{N} \approx 0.90\times10^{-4} \) N, at \( 23.6^\circ \) from the \( -x \) axis.

⚠️ The slide's final number is wrong — be ready for both

The slide prints \( F_{net} = 0.802\times10^{-4} \) N. But \( 0.802 \) is the value of \( (0.821)^2 + (0.358)^2 \) — the square root was never taken. The correct magnitude is \( \sqrt{0.802} = 0.896 \).

Write the \( \sqrt{\ } \) line out explicitly. Then your working is defensible whichever number the marker is expecting. The angle \( 23.6^\circ \) on the slide is correct.

Example-6 — Electric force vs gravity Slide Likely

Problem: In an atom, can we neglect the gravitational force between the electrons and protons? What is the ratio of Coulomb's electric force to Newton's gravitational force for two electrons separated by a distance \( r \)?

📝 Solution
\[ F_c = \frac{ke^2}{r^2}, \qquad F_g = \frac{Gm_e^2}{r^2} \]

Both go as \( 1/r^2 \), so when you divide, the \( r^2 \) cancels completely:

\[ \frac{F_c}{F_g} = \frac{ke^2}{Gm_e^2} \]
\[ = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{(6.67\times10^{-11})(9.11\times10^{-31})^2} = \frac{2.30\times10^{-28}}{5.54\times10^{-71}} \]
Answer: \( \dfrac{F_c}{F_g} \approx 4\times10^{42} \). The electric force is about \( 10^{42} \) times stronger, so yes — gravity is completely negligible inside an atom. The ratio does not depend on the separation at all.

Common variant: for an electron and a proton (i.e. hydrogen), put \( m_em_p \) on the bottom instead and the ratio becomes \( \approx 2.3\times10^{39} \).

SECTION 2: The Halliday Sample Problem in the Deck (Fig. 21-7)

Why this one matters

Pages 46–47 of the deck embed this Halliday sample problem. It builds from easy to hard with the same particle 1 each time: one force → two opposing forces → two forces at different angles. As the deck itself says: "The key to all three examples is to draw the forces correctly before you reach for a calculator, otherwise you may be calculating nonsense."

Setup In the deck

ParticleChargePositionEffect on particle 1
1 (the one of interest)\( q_1 = +1.60\times10^{-19} \) Corigin—
2\( q_2 = +3.20\times10^{-19} \) C\( x = R = 0.0200 \) msame sign → pushed away (along \(-x\))
3\( q_3 = -3.20\times10^{-19} \) C\( x = \tfrac34 R \)opposite sign → pulled toward (along \(+x\))
4\( q_4 = -3.20\times10^{-19} \) C\( \tfrac34 R \) at \( \theta = 60^\circ \)opposite sign → pulled toward (up and right)

Read the signs off the figure before anything else — that is what fixes every direction below.

(a) Two particles — a single force

📝 Solution
\[ F_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1||q_2|}{R^2} = (8.99\times10^{9})\frac{(1.60\times10^{-19})(3.20\times10^{-19})}{(0.0200)^2} \]
\[ F_{12} = 1.15\times10^{-24}\ \text{N} \]

Both charges are positive, so particle 1 is pushed away from particle 2. Since particle 2 lies on the \(+x\) side, that force points along \(-x\).

Answer: \( 1.15\times10^{-24} \) N at \( 180^\circ \), i.e. \( \vec F_{12} = -(1.15\times10^{-24}\ \text{N})\,\hat{i} \)

(b) Three particles — two forces, opposite directions

📝 Solution

Particle 3 sits closer, at \( \tfrac34 R \), so its force is larger even for the same charge magnitude:

\[ F_{13} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q_1||q_3|}{\left(\frac34 R\right)^2} = (8.99\times10^{9})\frac{(1.60\times10^{-19})(3.20\times10^{-19})}{\left(\frac34\right)^2(0.0200)^2} \]
\[ F_{13} = 2.05\times10^{-24}\ \text{N} \quad \text{along } +x, \quad \vec F_{13} = (2.05\times10^{-24}\,\text{N})\,\hat i \]

Both lie on the \(x\) axis, so here you may combine magnitudes with signs:

\[ \vec F_{1,net} = \vec F_{12} + \vec F_{13} = (-1.15 + 2.05)\times10^{-24} = 9.00\times10^{-25}\ \text{N} \]
Answer: \( 9.00\times10^{-25} \) N at \( 0^\circ \) (along \(+x\)).

Notice the \( (3/4)^2 = 9/16 \) in the denominator — moving a charge to three-quarters of the distance multiplies its force by \( 16/9 = 1.78 \).

(c) Four particles — two forces at different angles ★

📝 Solution

Particle 4 is at the same distance \( \tfrac34 R \), so its magnitude matches \( F_{13} \):

\[ F_{14} = 2.05\times10^{-24}\ \text{N}, \qquad \theta = 60^\circ \]

Now \( \vec F_{12} \) and \( \vec F_{14} \) are not along the same axis, so you cannot add magnitudes. Resolve into components:

\[ F_{14x} = F_{14}\cos 60^\circ = 1.03\times10^{-24}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{14y} = F_{14}\sin 60^\circ = 1.77\times10^{-24}\ \text{N} \]

Add \( \vec F_{12} = -1.15\times10^{-24}\,\hat i \) component by component:

\[ F_{x} = (-1.15 + 1.03)\times10^{-24} = -1.3\times10^{-25}\ \text{N} \]
\[ F_{y} = 1.77\times10^{-24}\ \text{N} \]
\[ \vec F_{1,net} \approx (-1.3\times10^{-25}\,\text{N})\,\hat i + (1.77\times10^{-24}\,\text{N})\,\hat j \]

If a magnitude and angle are wanted:

\[ |\vec F_{1,net}| = \sqrt{(1.3\times10^{-25})^2 + (1.77\times10^{-24})^2} = 1.78\times10^{-24}\ \text{N} \]
\[ \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\frac{1.77\times10^{-24}}{-1.3\times10^{-25}} = 94.1^\circ \]
Answer: \( \vec F_{1,net} = (-1.3\times10^{-25})\hat i + (1.77\times10^{-24})\hat j \) N, magnitude \( 1.78\times10^{-24} \) N at \( 94.1^\circ \) from \(+x\).

Watch the quadrant: \( F_x \) is negative and \( F_y \) positive, so the resultant is in the second quadrant. A calculator's \( \tan^{-1} \) returns \( -85.9^\circ \) — you must add \( 180^\circ \) to land in the right quadrant.

SECTION 3: Practice Set — One Per Problem Type

How to use this section

These are not from the slides — I wrote them to cover every problem type in the chapter, since the notice allows "or similar problem". Cover the solution, try it, then check. Each one drills a different skill.

P1 — Counting elementary charges Practice

A glass rod is rubbed with silk and acquires a charge of \( +2.0\ \mu\text{C} \). How many electrons were removed from it?

📝 Show Solution

A positive charge means electrons were removed. Using \( Q = Ne \):

\[ N = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{2.0\times10^{-6}}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = 1.25\times10^{13} \]
Answer: \( 1.25\times10^{13} \) electrons removed.

P2 — Force between two point charges Practice

\( q_1 = +3.0\ \mu\text{C} \) and \( q_2 = -5.0\ \mu\text{C} \) are 20 cm apart. Find the magnitude and nature of the force.

📝 Show Solution

Convert: \( r = 0.20 \) m, so \( r^2 = 0.040 \) m².

\[ F = \frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(3.0\times10^{-6})(5.0\times10^{-6})}{0.040} \]
\[ = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(1.5\times10^{-11})}{0.040} = \frac{0.1348}{0.040} = 3.37\ \text{N} \]
Answer: \( F = 3.37 \) N, attractive (opposite signs).

P3 — Working backwards to find the charge Practice

Two identical small spheres 10 cm apart repel each other with a force of 0.90 N. What charge does each carry?

📝 Show Solution

Identical spheres means \( q_1 = q_2 = q \), so \( F = kq^2/r^2 \). Rearranging:

\[ q = \sqrt{\frac{Fr^2}{k}} = \sqrt{\frac{(0.90)(0.10)^2}{8.99\times10^{9}}} = \sqrt{1.00\times10^{-12}} \]
Answer: \( q = 1.0\times10^{-6} \) C \( = 1.0\ \mu\text{C} \) on each sphere. Since they repel, both have the same sign.

P4 — Three charges in a line Practice ★

\( q_1 = +4.0\ \mu\text{C} \) at \( x=0 \), \( q_2 = -3.0\ \mu\text{C} \) at \( x = 0.20 \) m, \( q_3 = +2.0\ \mu\text{C} \) at \( x = 0.50 \) m. Find the net force on \( \mathbf{q_2} \).

📝 Show Solution

Directions first. \( q_2 \) is negative:

  • \( q_1 \) is positive and sits to the left → attracts \( q_2 \) toward \(-x\)
  • \( q_3 \) is positive and sits to the right → attracts \( q_2 \) toward \(+x\)

Distances: \( r_{12} = 0.20 \) m, \( r_{23} = 0.50-0.20 = 0.30 \) m.

\[ F_{21} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(4.0\times10^{-6})(3.0\times10^{-6})}{(0.20)^2} = 2.70\ \text{N (toward } -x) \]
\[ F_{23} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(3.0\times10^{-6})(2.0\times10^{-6})}{(0.30)^2} = 0.599\ \text{N (toward } +x) \]
\[ F_{net} = -2.70 + 0.599 = -2.10\ \text{N} \]
Answer: \( 2.10 \) N directed toward \( -x \) (toward \( q_1 \)).

P5 — Equilateral triangle Practice ★ Classic

Three charges of \( +5.0\ \mu\text{C} \) each sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side 0.50 m. Find the magnitude of the net force on any one of them.

📝 Show Solution

By symmetry, each of the other two pushes with the same magnitude:

\[ F = \frac{kq^2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(5.0\times10^{-6})^2}{(0.50)^2} = \frac{0.2248}{0.25} = 0.899\ \text{N} \]

In an equilateral triangle the two forces are separated by \( 60^\circ \). For two equal vectors at angle \( \phi \) the resultant is \( 2F\cos(\phi/2) \):

\[ F_{net} = 2F\cos 30^\circ = 2(0.899)(0.866) = 1.56\ \text{N} \]
Answer: \( F_{net} = 1.56 \) N, directed outward along the bisector — i.e. directly away from the centre of the triangle.

Shortcut worth memorising: two equal forces at \( 60^\circ \) give \( \sqrt3\,F = 1.732F \). Check: \( 1.732 \times 0.899 = 1.56 \) ✓

P6 — Where is the net force zero? Practice ★ Classic

\( q_1 = +9.0\ \mu\text{C} \) is at \( x = 0 \) and \( q_2 = +4.0\ \mu\text{C} \) is at \( x = 1.0 \) m. At what point on the line joining them is the net force on a third charge zero?

📝 Show Solution

Both charges are positive, so the point must lie between them — only there do the two pushes oppose. Let it be a distance \( x \) from \( q_1 \), hence \( (1-x) \) from \( q_2 \). Setting the magnitudes equal (the test charge \( q \) and \( k \) cancel):

\[ \frac{9}{x^2} = \frac{4}{(1-x)^2} \]

Take the square root of both sides — much faster than expanding:

\[ \frac{3}{x} = \frac{2}{1-x} \;\Longrightarrow\; 3(1-x) = 2x \;\Longrightarrow\; 3 = 5x \]
Answer: \( x = 0.60 \) m from the \( 9\ \mu\text{C} \) charge (equivalently 0.40 m from the \( 4\ \mu\text{C} \) one).
Check: \( 9/0.6^2 = 25 \) and \( 4/0.4^2 = 25 \) ✓

Note: if the two charges had opposite signs, the zero point would lie outside the pair, on the side of the smaller magnitude charge.

P7 — Coulomb vs gravity in hydrogen Practice

Find the ratio of the electric force to the gravitational force between the electron and the proton in a hydrogen atom.

📝 Show Solution
\[ \frac{F_c}{F_g} = \frac{ke^2/r^2}{Gm_em_p/r^2} = \frac{ke^2}{Gm_em_p} \]
\[ = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{(6.67\times10^{-11})(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.67\times10^{-27})} \]
\[ = \frac{2.30\times10^{-28}}{1.01\times10^{-67}} \approx 2.3\times10^{39} \]
Answer: \( \approx 2.3\times10^{39} \). Note you were never told \( r \) — you don't need it, because \( r^2 \) cancels.

P8 — Charge hidden in ordinary matter Practice

If one electron were removed from every atom in a 1.0 g copper coin, what charge would it carry? (\( M_{Cu} = 63.5 \) g/mol)

📝 Show Solution
\[ N_{atoms} = \frac{m}{M}N_A = \frac{1.0}{63.5}(6.022\times10^{23}) = 9.48\times10^{21} \]
\[ q = N_{atoms}\,e = (9.48\times10^{21})(1.6\times10^{-19}) \approx 1.5\times10^{3}\ \text{C} \]
Answer: about \( 1.5\times10^{3} \) C — around 1500 coulombs from a single gram, which is why a "large" charge in the lab is only microcoulombs.

P9 — Charged spheres, surface separation Practice Trap

Two metal spheres, each of radius 2.0 cm and carrying \( 3.0\ \mu\text{C} \), are placed so that their surfaces are 6.0 cm apart. Find the force between them.

📝 Show Solution

Uniformly charged spheres act as point charges at their centres — so you must use the centre-to-centre distance, not the surface gap:

\[ r = 6.0 + 2(2.0) = 10.0\ \text{cm} = 0.10\ \text{m} \]
\[ F = \frac{kq^2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(3.0\times10^{-6})^2}{(0.10)^2} = \frac{0.0809}{0.010} = 8.09\ \text{N} \]
Answer: \( F = 8.09 \) N, repulsive.

The trap: using \( r = 6 \) cm gives 22.5 N — nearly three times too big. Always ask whether a stated separation is surface-to-surface or centre-to-centre.

P10 — Current and electron count Practice

3.0 C of charge passes through a wire in 6.0 s. Find (i) the current and (ii) how many electrons passed.

📝 Show Solution
\[ \text{(i)}\quad I = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{3.0}{6.0} = 0.50\ \text{A} \]
\[ \text{(ii)}\quad N = \frac{q}{e} = \frac{3.0}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = 1.9\times10^{19} \]
Answer: \( I = 0.50 \) A; \( N = 1.9\times10^{19} \) electrons.

P11 — Charge from a current graph Practice Graph

A current is constant at 4.0 A for the first 3.0 s, then falls linearly to zero over the next 4.0 s. How much charge is delivered in total?

📝 Show Solution

Since \( Q = \int i\,dt \), the charge is the area under the current–time graph. Split it into a rectangle and a triangle:

\[ Q_{rect} = (4.0)(3.0) = 12\ \text{C} \]
\[ Q_{tri} = \tfrac12(4.0)(4.0) = 8.0\ \text{C} \]
\[ Q_{total} = 12 + 8 = 20\ \text{C} \]
Answer: \( Q = 20 \) C.

The mirror-image question: given a charge–time graph, the current is its slope, \( i = dq/dt \).

P12 — Force and acceleration in a nucleus Practice

Two protons in a nucleus are \( 1.0\times10^{-15} \) m apart. Find the electric force between them and the resulting acceleration of one proton.

📝 Show Solution
\[ F = \frac{ke^2}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99\times10^{9})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{(1.0\times10^{-15})^2} = \frac{2.30\times10^{-28}}{1.0\times10^{-30}} = 230\ \text{N} \]
\[ a = \frac{F}{m_p} = \frac{230}{1.67\times10^{-27}} = 1.4\times10^{29}\ \text{m/s}^2 \]
Answer: \( F \approx 230 \) N (repulsive), \( a \approx 1.4\times10^{29} \) m/s².

230 N on a single proton is enormous — which raises the obvious question of why nuclei hold together at all. The answer is the strong nuclear force, which beats electrostatic repulsion at this range.

P13 — Symmetry (no calculation needed) Practice

Two equal charges \( +q \) are fixed a distance \( d \) apart. A third charge \( Q \) is placed exactly midway between them. (i) What is the net force on \( Q \)? (ii) What happens if one of the \( +q \) charges is doubled?

📝 Show Solution

(i) Each \( +q \) is the same distance \( d/2 \) from \( Q \) and carries the same charge, so the two forces have equal magnitude \( kqQ/(d/2)^2 \) but point in opposite directions along the line.

\[ F_{net} = 0 \]

(ii) If one becomes \( 2q \), that side's force doubles while the other is unchanged, so:

\[ F_{net} = \frac{k(2q)Q}{(d/2)^2} - \frac{kqQ}{(d/2)^2} = \frac{kqQ}{(d/2)^2} = \frac{4kqQ}{d^2} \]
Answer: (i) zero by symmetry; (ii) \( 4kqQ/d^2 \), directed away from the doubled charge if \( Q \) is positive (toward it if \( Q \) is negative).

⚡ The five mistakes that cost the most marks

  1. Not converting cm to m before squaring. \( r = 2\ \text{cm} \Rightarrow r^2 = 4\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2 \), not \( 4 \).
  2. Adding magnitudes when the forces are at an angle. Resolve → add components → then \( \sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2} \).
  3. Forgetting the square root at the end of a resultant (the slide itself did this in Example-5).
  4. Using a surface gap as the centre distance in sphere problems — see P9.
  5. Giving a magnitude with no direction when a net force was asked for.
